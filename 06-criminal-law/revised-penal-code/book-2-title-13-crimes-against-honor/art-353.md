> Article 353. Definition of libel.-A libel is a public and malicious imputation of a crime, or of a vice or defect, real or imaginary, or any act, omission, condition, status, or circumstance tending to cause the dishonor, discredit, or contempt of a natural or juridical person, or to blacken the memory of one who is dead.

[^revised-penal-code]

# Explanation

## Definition and Scope

Article 353 of the Revised Penal Code supplies the single substantive definition of libel across Philippine criminal and civil law. In [*Causing v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70337)[^2], the Supreme Court En Banc clarified that libel is defined in Article 353 rather than in [Article 355](art-355.md). The succeeding articles merely specify the modes of committing that offense, namely written or similar means under [Article 355](art-355.md), oral defamation under [Article 358](art-358.md) and slander by deed under [Article 359](art-359.md).[^3] In [*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827)[^4], the Supreme Court applied the same Article 353 standards on defamatory imputation and malice to oral defamation because slander is spoken libel. In [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112)[^5], the Supreme Court traced this statutory definition to Act No. 277 of the Philippine Commission and the Spanish Penal Code.

Online or cyber libel under [Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175](../../cybercrime-prevention-act-of-2012-ra-10175/chapter-02-punishable-acts/sec-04.md) rests on the same Article 353 definition. In its February 18, 2014 Decision in [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650)[^6], its April 22, 2014 Resolution in [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56830)[^7], its October 11, 2023 Decision in [*Causing v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69180)[^8] and its April 8, 2026 En Banc Resolution in [*Causing v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70337)[^9], the Supreme Court held that cyberlibel is not a new crime. Republic Act No. 10175 merely recognizes a computer system as another means of publishing the felony defined in Article 353 in relation to [Article 355](art-355.md) and treats the use of information and communications technology as a qualifying circumstance that raises the penalty by one degree.[^10] Consequently, the entire body of constitutional jurisprudence protecting free speech applies equally to online libel.[^11]

The Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of Article 353 because libel falls outside constitutionally protected speech in [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650)[^12], [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56830)[^13] and [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^14]. Only Congress and not the judiciary may decriminalize libel.[^15] At the same time, criminal statutes defining libel are strictly construed in favor of the accused, as the Supreme Court ruled in [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112)[^16], [*Torreda v. Toshiba Information Equipment (Phils.), Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40967)[^17] and [*Peñalosa v. Ocampo, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68864)[^18]. In [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650), the Supreme Court restricted online libel liability to the original author of a defamatory post and declared Section 4(c)(4) and Section 5 of Republic Act No. 10175 unconstitutional insofar as they penalized mere recipients who react, like, share or comment without introducing a new defamatory imputation.[^19] In the same ruling, the Supreme Court struck down dual prosecution under both Article 353 and Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175 as a violation of the constitutional bar against double jeopardy because both charges involve the same elements and constitute one offense.[^20] Imposable penalties and fines are governed by [Article 355](art-355.md), prescription is governed by [Article 90](../book-1-title-04-extinction-of-criminal-liability-and-of/art-090.md) and persons liable and venue are governed by [Article 360](art-360.md).

In [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650), the Supreme Court stated the four essential elements of libel:[^21]

1. the allegation of a discreditable act or condition concerning another;

2. publication of the charge;

3. identity of the person defamed; and

4. existence of malice.

All four elements must concur to sustain a criminal conviction, a finding of probable cause or a civil judgment for libel, as held in [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^22], [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^23], [*Manila Bulletin Publishing Corp. v. Domingo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63097)[^24] and [*Lopez v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34876)[^25].

## Imputation of a Discreditable Act or Condition

An allegation is defamatory under Article 353 when it ascribes to another the commission of a crime, the possession of a real or imaginary vice or defect or any act, omission, condition, status or circumstance tending to cause dishonor, discredit or contempt or to blacken the memory of the dead, as stated in [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^26], [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112)[^27], [*Manila Bulletin Publishing Corp. v. Domingo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63097)[^28] and [*Lopez v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34876)[^29]. Courts examine whether the statement is defamatory as a threshold question before testing publication, identity or malice, as demonstrated in [*Lopez v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34876)[^30], [*Manila Bulletin Publishing Corp. v. Domingo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63097)[^31], [*Novicio v. Aggabao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47189)[^32] and [*Insular Life Assurance Co., Ltd. v. Serrano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43928)[^33]. Where the words are non-defamatory on the face of the Information, the charge must be quashed or dismissed outright.[^34]

Two settled rules of construction govern whether a publication is defamatory: 

- ***First***, the publication must be construed as a whole in its entirety rather than from isolated phrases, which requires reading a newspaper headline together with the body of the story.[^35] 

- ***Second***, the words must be taken in their plain, natural and ordinary meaning as average readers would understand them, disregarding any after-the-fact explanation of what the author subjectively intended, as held in [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^36], [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112)[^37], [*Buatis, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40749)[^38], [*Flor v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43704)[^39] and [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663)[^40]. Applying these rules in [*Figueroa v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42300)[^41], the Supreme Court acquitted columnists who used the words stupid and ignorant to describe mall patrons inside a commentary praising a shopping center because the column could not be twisted into an attack on a corporate officer.

A plain report that merely replicates what a source said without adopting the accusation as the writer's own is not a defamatory imputation. In [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762), the Supreme Court held that a newspaper report repeating remarks attributed to a Presidential Commission on Good Government official was a plain replication that a person said those words and was not libelous even though the paper inadvertently attributed the quote to the wrong official.[^42] The Supreme Court similarly found no defamatory imputation in a news story reporting employee graft complaints filed with government agencies in [*Manila Bulletin Publishing Corp. v. Domingo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63097)[^43], in a published Notice of Termination stating that a former agency manager no longer represented an insurer in [*Insular Life Assurance Co., Ltd. v. Serrano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43928)[^44], in a corporate treasurer's letter notifying depository banks of a court resolution and a change of authorized bank signatories in [*Novicio v. Aggabao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47189)[^45] and in an employee's grievance letter protesting a superior's workplace conduct in [*Torreda v. Toshiba Information Equipment (Phils.), Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40967)[^46].

Words that are merely insulting, vulgar or offensive to personal feelings are not actionable as libel *per se*. Defamation law protects relational reputation in the community rather than personal pride or emotional tranquility, as the Supreme Court En Banc held in [*MVRS Publications, Inc. v. Islamic Da'wah Council of the Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48023)[^47]. The Supreme Court applied that rule in [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112)[^48], [*Lopez v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34876)[^49] and [*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827)[^50], holding that an ironic banner greeting an official with the phrase *Santong Mayor* or angry street epithets calling a barangay councilor ignorant did not by themselves establish actionable defamation without a concrete discreditable imputation.

When a statement crosses from mere insult into attributing concrete wrongdoing, vice or disreputable status, it satisfies the first element without need of extrinsic proof when defamatory on its face. The Supreme Court held imputations of crimes defamatory *per se* where publications accused targets of embezzlement and fleeing justice in [*Fermin v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45169)[^51], demanding bribes for municipal permits in [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^52], carnapping in [*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917)[^53], dog theft in [*Magno v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40444)[^54] and stealing exclusive broadcast footage in [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67813)[^55]. Actionable imputations of vices, defects or professional unfitness likewise include branding a hospital supervisor mentally sick and paranoid in [*Lagaya v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55047)[^56], calling a lawyer lousy and inutile in [*Buatis, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40749)[^57], labeling a neighbor a swindler in [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663)[^58] and calling a medical college a dumping ground of moral and physical misfits in [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995)[^59]. Article 353 also reaches humiliating personal status or veiled insinuations, such as portraying an adopted minor girl as an extravagance-inducing *ampon* in [*Binay v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42104)[^60] or depicting a businessman as a corporate crony and dummy in [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949)[^61].

## Publication

Publication in libel means making the defamatory matter known after it is written or spoken to any third person other than the author and the person defamed. Communication to a single third person suffices even if the offended party never personally read or heard the statement, as the Supreme Court held in [*Vasquez v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38132)[^62], [*Manila Bulletin Publishing Corp. v. Domingo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63097)[^63], [*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917)[^64], [*Buatis, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40749)[^65] and [*Magno v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40444)[^66]. Under the multiple publication rule recognized in [*Brillante v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46218)[^67], a single defamatory utterance gives rise to separate causes of action when published in distinct media or separate editions.

Jurisprudence illustrates diverse channels that satisfy third-person publication. Mass-media circulation in newspapers, tabloids, radio broadcasts and Facebook posts read by online witnesses establishes publication, as seen in [*Fermin v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45169)[^68], [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^69] and [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995)[^70]. Private or localized acts also constitute publication when exposed to third parties: dictating a letter to a secretary and leaving it unsealed in a mailbox where the addressee's spouse retrieved it in [*Buatis, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40749)[^71], painting accusations on a garage wall seen by a neighbor's son or addressing an envelope to both spouses when the letter defames the husband alone in [*Magno v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40444)[^72], stapling a third party's criminal complaint onto a public jeepney terminal dispatch board in [*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917)[^73] and circulating a complaint letter among rank-and-file hospital employees before a departmental hearing in [*Lagaya v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55047)[^74]. Even filing a sealed omnibus motion in court resulted in publication in [*Belen v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62853)[^75] when clerical staff opened the envelope in the ordinary course of business and an adverse party's representative received a copy.

The prosecution must prove publication and authorship through competent evidence. In [*Maglasang v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67065)[^76], the Supreme Court acquitted the accused because unauthenticated photocopies and bare testimony that a letter was delivered did not prove due execution or actual reading by a third person. In [*Torralba v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42403)[^77], the Supreme Court acquitted a radio commentator after ruling that an unauthenticated tape recording is inadmissible unless the actual operator establishes seven foundational requisites and that broadcast publication requires a witness who either saw the accused speak on air or actually listened to the broadcast and recognized the speaker's voice. By contrast, eyewitnesses and witness-identified photographs proved bulletin-board publication in [*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917)[^78], while surrounding transaction records informed the evaluation of e-mail correspondence in [*Dio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62001)[^79].

Two doctrinal exceptions negate actionable publication even when another person reads the document. First, sending a communication to a public officer, corporate superior or quasi-judicial office having an official duty to act on its subject matter (and furnishing copies solely to legally required parties) does not amount to publication in the law of defamation, as held in [*Alonzo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/feb1995/gr_110088_1995.html)[^80], [*Arquiza v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69726)[^81], [*Alcantara v. Ponce*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41255)[^82], [*Fortich v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34505)[^83] and [*Novicio v. Aggabao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47189)[^84]. Second, voluntary disclosure of a defamatory letter by the offended party to third persons cannot be attributed to the author, as established in [*Alonzo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/feb1995/gr_110088_1995.html)[^85] and [*Magno v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40444)[^86].

## Identity of the Person Defamed

To satisfy the third element, the prosecution or plaintiff must show that the defamatory statement referred to the offended party. Explicit naming in the text is unnecessary if a third person who reads or hears the publication can recognize the victim from intrinsic clues, subsequent articles in a series or extrinsic circumstances known to the audience, as held in [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^87], [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^88], [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46807)[^89] and [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949)[^90]. In [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517), witnesses identified an unnamed town mayor from Facebook references to the municipality's highest post and his signature orange campaign color.[^91] In [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46807), follow-up columns naming a Bureau of Customs intelligence officer supplied the identity omitted from the initial column.[^92]

Identifiability fails when no third person can link the defamatory words to a specific target. Neither the victim's own assumption nor vague speculation by a general reader establishes identification, as the Supreme Court ruled in [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^93], [*Diaz v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43691)[^94] and [*Manila Bulletin Publishing Corp. v. Domingo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63097)[^95]. Thus, blind items referring only to an unnamed conference organizer in [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^96] or an unnamed screen celebrity with common traits in [*Diaz v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43691)[^97] could not sustain liability. Similarly, in [*Corpuz v. Del Rosario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35131)[^98], an official inquiry letter asking an employee to explain an unauthorized alteration expressed genuine doubt over the culprit's identity and negated a targeted imputation.

Under the group libel doctrine laid down by the Supreme Court En Banc in [*MVRS Publications, Inc. v. Islamic Da'wah Council of the Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48023)[^99], sweeping defamatory statements directed at a large class or religious community cannot be construed to refer to an identifiable individual. Absent specific circumstances singling out a particular member, neither an individual member nor a class representative has a cause of action for libel.[^100] The Supreme Court applied that rule in [*Soriano v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64717)[^101] to acquit a religious broadcaster whose remarks attacked rival church leaders as a group without naming or singling out the private complainant. Conversely, where a statement targets a small and restricted group or expressly names individual corporate officers alongside their company, individual members are sufficiently identified, as noted in [*MVRS Publications, Inc. v. Islamic Da'wah Council of the Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48023)[^102] and applied in [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949)[^103].

Article 353 protects three categories of defamed subjects: natural persons, juridical persons and the memory of the dead. Natural persons include minor children, such as the adopted child represented by her parents in [*Binay v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42104)[^104]. Juridical persons such as corporations possess an institutional reputation protected by Article 353 and may validly complain of libel, as the Supreme Court held in [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995)[^105], [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67813)[^106] and [*Insular Life Assurance Co., Ltd. v. Serrano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43928)[^107]. In [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995), the Supreme Court ruled that a learning corporation maligned by libelous *per se* radio broadcasts had a direct cause of action under Article 353 and Article 2219(7) of the Civil Code, whereas its dean could not recover personally because the broadcasts targeted the school rather than her.[^108] Finally, Article 353 punishes imputations that blacken the memory of a deceased person, as illustrated by the prosecution for maligning a late judge in [*Torralba v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42403)[^109].

## Malice

### Presumed Malice Versus Actual Malice

Malice indicates ill will or spite and forms the legal essence of libel, as stated in [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^110], [*Brillante v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46218)[^111] and [*Almendras, Jr. v. Almendras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/58855)[^112]. In [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^113], the Supreme Court held that whether established facts demonstrate malice is a question of law reviewable in a Rule 45 petition. Philippine law distinguishes between presumed malice (malice in law) and actual malice (malice in fact).[^114]

When an unprivileged defamatory statement targets a private individual, [Article 354](art-354.md) presumes malice in law from the defamatory nature of the imputation itself. That presumption attaches even if the accusation is true unless the offender shows good intention and justifiable motive, as applied in [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650)[^115], [*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917)[^116], [*Buatis, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40749)[^117], [*Almendras, Jr. v. Almendras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/58855)[^118] and [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67813)[^119].

A stricter constitutional standard governs when the offended party is a public official criticized in relation to official duties or a public figure involved in a matter of public interest. In those cases, presumed malice does not apply: the prosecution in a criminal case must prove actual malice beyond reasonable doubt and the plaintiff in a civil case must prove actual malice by preponderance of evidence, as held in [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650)[^120], [*Vasquez v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38132)[^121], [*Guingguing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42238)[^122], [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818)[^123] and [*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302)[^124]. Public figures include election candidates in [*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302)[^125], public-affairs broadcast commentators in [*Guingguing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42238)[^126] and organizers of public-interest national conferences in [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^127]. Moreover, true statements on matters of public record concerning a public official or public figure are not actionable as libel, as established in [*Vasquez v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38132)[^128] and [*Guingguing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42238)[^129].

### Reckless Disregard

Actual malice exists only when the offender publishes a defamatory falsehood with actual knowledge that it is false or with reckless disregard of whether it is false or not, as defined in [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650)[^130], [*Vasquez v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38132)[^131], [*Flor v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43704)[^132], [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818)[^133] and [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^134]. Reckless disregard is a subjective standard rather than an objective negligence test.[^135] To establish reckless disregard, the evidence must show that the author in fact entertained serious doubts as to the truth of the publication or possessed a high degree of awareness of its probable falsity, whereas even gross or extreme negligence is insufficient.[^136]

Applying this subjective threshold, the Supreme Court held that a journalist may rely on information supplied by a lone source without counter-checking or obtaining the public official's side so long as the reporter did not harbor a high degree of awareness of probable falsity, as ruled in [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^137], [*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302)[^138], [*Flor v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43704)[^139] and [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112)[^140]. Honest mistakes, inaccuracies or a lay reporter's imprecise legal terminology in describing a court pleading likewise fail to prove actual malice, as held in [*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302)[^141], [*Yambot v. Tuquero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36758)[^142] and [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^143]. Nor do an existing business grudge, failure to verify prior to posting on social media or subsequent public apologies prove actual malice beyond reasonable doubt against a public officer.[^144]

Conversely, courts find actual malice when concrete evidence proves knowing fabrication or calculated disregard of truth. The Supreme Court affirmed actual malice where a columnist leveled baseless extortion charges with zero investigation and repeated the attacks after being sued in [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46807)[^145], where a tabloid publisher fabricated criminal accusations against a congressional candidate's family in [*Fermin v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45169)[^146], where a newspaper waged a seven-part personal smear campaign over private corporate disputes in [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949)[^147], where radio hosts aired unverified student gripes despite having a month to check official accreditation records in [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995)[^148] and where a politician broadcast unverified criminal accusations at a press conference without verifying them through official channels in [*Brillante v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46218)[^149]. Furthermore, while lifestyle criticisms tied to alleged corruption in public office remain matters of public concern under [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112)[^150], attacks on the purely private life, private business or minor children of a public officer or public figure enjoy no actual-malice shield, as held in [*Fermin v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45169)[^151], [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949)[^152], [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663)[^153] and [*Binay v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42104)[^154].

### Privileged Communications

Privileged communications negate malice and divide into qualified and absolute privileges. Qualified privilege destroys the legal presumption of malice and requires proof of actual malice across four recognized categories: private communications made in the performance of a legal, moral or social duty under [Article 354](art-354.md)(1), fair and true reports of non-confidential official proceedings or official acts without comments under [Article 354](art-354.md)(2), fair commentaries on matters of public interest recognized in [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^155] and fair reports on matters of public interest established in [*Yambot v. Tuquero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36758)[^156] and [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^157]. Once actual malice is proven, however, a claim of qualified privilege fails, as emphasized in [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949)[^158] and [*Dio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62001)[^159].

Absolute privilege bars criminal and civil liability altogether regardless of malice when statements are made within protected official functions. It covers parliamentary speech delivered while Congress is in session under [*Trillanes IV v. Castillo-Marigomen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63938)[^160], relevant statements by litigants, witnesses or counsel in judicial and preliminary investigation pleadings under [*Alcantara v. Ponce*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41255)[^161] (inapplicable to irrelevant personal attacks on a judge in [*Belen v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62853)[^162] or public reposting by non-party strangers in [*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917)[^163]) and relevant filings in quasi-judicial proceedings satisfying the four-fold test in [*Arquiza v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69726)[^164]. The specific requisites of each privilege are developed under [Article 354](art-354.md), penalties under [Article 355](art-355.md), prescription under [Article 90](../book-1-title-04-extinction-of-criminal-liability-and-of/art-090.md) and persons responsible and venue under [Article 360](art-360.md).

## Doctrinal Shifts

### Public Officers and the Burden of Proving Actual Malice

Supreme Court doctrine on defamatory criticisms of public officers underwent a decisive shift regarding the burden of proof. In [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663)[^165], the Supreme Court stated that defamatory remarks directed at a public officer's official duties were not actionable if the defendant proved their truth and treated the absence of malice as a matter for the defense to establish. Subsequently, in the En Banc decision in [*Vasquez v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38132)[^166], the Supreme Court applied the actual-malice standard to acquit a citizen who criticized a barangay official, holding that even false statements concerning official conduct are not actionable unless made with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard of truth. In [*Guingguing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42238)[^167], the Supreme Court extended that constitutional standard to public figures in criminal libel and held that true statements on matters of public record are not libelous.

In [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818)[^168], the Supreme Court squarely repudiated lower-court rulings that required the accused to prove truth or reasonable verification, holding that in criminal libel involving a public officer it is the prosecution's burden to prove actual malice beyond reasonable doubt and not the defense's burden to disprove it. In [*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827)[^169], the Supreme Court explicitly noted that [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818)[^170] modified the older formulation in [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663)[^171]. The Supreme Court reaffirmed that prosecutorial burden in [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^172].

### Reconciliation of Disini and Peñalosa in Causing

A second doctrinal tension arose over the statutory anchor of online libel. In [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650)[^173] and [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56830)[^174], the Supreme Court En Banc stated that cyberlibel under Republic Act No. 10175 is not a new crime because Article 353 in relation to [Article 355](art-355.md) already punishes libel and online defamation constitutes similar means. Subsequently, in [*Peñalosa v. Ocampo, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68864)[^175], the Supreme Court Second Division applied *noscitur a sociis* and Article 22 of the Revised Penal Code to hold that [Article 355](art-355.md) did not encompass computer systems prior to Republic Act No. 10175, rendering an August 2011 Facebook post unpunishable as criminal libel.

The Supreme Court reconciled both pronouncements in its October 11, 2023 Decision in [*Causing v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69180)[^176] and its April 8, 2026 En Banc Resolution in [*Causing v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70337)[^177]. The Supreme Court En Banc explained that while [Article 355](art-355.md) originally omitted two-way computer systems as held in [*Peñalosa v. Ocampo, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68864)[^178], the crime of libel itself has always been defined in Article 353 rather than [Article 355](art-355.md).[^179] [Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175](../../cybercrime-prevention-act-of-2012-ra-10175/chapter-02-punishable-acts/sec-04.md) therefore created no new offense when it added computer systems as a mode of committing Article 353 libel upon the effectivity of Republic Act No. 10175, leaving pre-statute online posts outside criminal prosecution while subjecting cyberlibel to the Revised Penal Code's prescriptive rules under [Article 90](../book-1-title-04-extinction-of-criminal-liability-and-of/art-090.md).[^180]

### Expansion of Privileged Communications

Jurisprudence also progressively expanded privileged communications beyond the text of [Article 354](art-354.md). Recognizing that the two statutory exceptions in [Article 354](art-354.md) are non-exclusive, the Supreme Court in [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^181] anchored fair commentaries on matters of public interest directly in the constitutional guarantee of free expression. In [*Yambot v. Tuquero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36758)[^182] and [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^183], the Supreme Court recognized a fourth class of qualified privilege covering fair reports on matters of public interest. In parallel, the Supreme Court extended absolute privilege from courtroom pleadings to preliminary investigation submissions in [*Alcantara v. Ponce*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41255)[^184] (subject to strict relevancy limits in [*Belen v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62853)[^185]) and to quasi-judicial proceedings meeting a four-fold test in [*Arquiza v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69726)[^186]. Full treatment of these privileges appears under [Article 354](art-354.md).

## Independent Civil Action Under Civil Code Article 33

Under [Article 33 of the Civil Code](../../../03-civil-law/civil-code/preliminary-title/art-0033.md), an injured party may bring a separate and independent civil action for damages in cases of defamation that proceeds independently of any criminal prosecution and requires only a preponderance of evidence. Even in an independent civil suit, however, the substantive definition of libel in Article 353 and its four elements govern whether actionable defamation was committed, as applied in [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949)[^187], [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^188], [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^189], [*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302)[^190], [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995)[^191] and [*Trillanes IV v. Castillo-Marigomen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63938)[^192]. In [*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302), the Supreme Court held that allegations of malicious publication stamp a civil complaint as an action for defamation rather than ordinary quasi-delict, requiring a public-figure plaintiff to prove actual malice by preponderance of evidence.[^193] Rules governing the measure of moral, exemplary and actual damages belong to the Civil Code provisions under [Article 33](../../../03-civil-law/civil-code/preliminary-title/art-0033.md).

## Case Discussions

The decisions below apply the definition and the four elements of Article 353 to particular facts. Each discussion states the facts, the rulings below, the holding of the Supreme Court and the disposition.

### [*Torreda v. Toshiba Information Equipment (Phils.), Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40967): Libel as Serious Misconduct

Article 353 also supplies the yardstick when an employer asks whether an employee's accusation was defamatory. Jeffrey Torreda found ₱200.00 missing from his office drawer and sent an e-mail to his superior, Finance Manager Sepulveda, calling the loss "a plain and simple robbery on your part." He furnished copies of the e-mail to four senior officers of the company.[^194] An internal investigation found no factual basis for the charge. The company dismissed Torreda. The National Labor Relations Commission upheld the dismissal as serious misconduct and the Court of Appeals affirmed it but treated the accusation as grave slander.[^195]

The Supreme Court held that the Court of Appeals erred in calling the act grave slander. Slander is defamation by spoken words. Because the false charge of robbery was made in writing, Torreda committed libel under Article 353.[^196] The Supreme Court noted that he knew another employee had opened his drawer and he later admitted that the charge was baseless and made out of anger. His malicious and public imputation of a crime was serious misconduct under Article 282(a) of the Labor Code. The petition was denied and the Court of Appeals decision was affirmed.[^197]

### [*Novicio v. Aggabao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47189): Corporate Notice to Depository Banks

The four elements must appear in the Information itself. Ligaya Novicio, treasurer of Philippine International Life Insurance Company, wrote to the company's depository banks. Her letter said that the Court of Appeals had restrained seven named persons, including corporate secretary Alma Aggabao, from exercising their rights as shareholders. It told the banks that the board had changed the account signatories and warned them not to deal with the restrained persons.[^198] Aggabao filed a libel complaint. Three Informations were filed in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, which denied Novicio's motion to quash. The Court of Appeals dismissed her certiorari petition.[^199]

The Supreme Court ruled that, except for identification, the Informations failed to allege the other three elements of libel. Read in its plain and ordinary meaning, the letter only informed the banks of the change in signatories. It made no defamatory imputation.[^200] No malice was alleged and the letter was a privileged communication made in good faith to persons with a corresponding interest. Nor was there publication, because the letter went only to the banks that had to act on it.[^201] The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and ordered the three criminal cases dismissed.[^202]

### [*Insular Life Assurance Co., Ltd. v. Serrano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43928): A Notice of Meeting Is Not Defamatory

A juridical person may complain of libel, but the imputation must still be defamatory. Manuel Serrano believed that his Diamond Jubilee policies would be self-liquidating after seven years. When the insurer kept collecting premiums, he published a notice in the Manila Bulletin addressed to those who were a "victim" of Insular Life's refusal to honor that representation to a meeting of policyholders.[^203] Insular Life filed a libel complaint. The City Prosecutor of Makati dismissed it for lack of probable cause, finding no defamatory imputation and no malice. The Secretary of Justice and the Court of Appeals agreed.[^204]

The Supreme Court noted that publication and identity were not disputed.[^205] The prosecutor and the Secretary of Justice had read the notice in its entirety and treated it as a mere notice of meeting. Saying that the insurer refused to honor a representation is not the imputation of a crime, vice or defect. Because that shared conclusion was neither whimsical nor arbitrary, the Supreme Court applied its policy of not interfering with the executive determination of probable cause. The petition was denied.[^206]

### [*Figueroa v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42300): Name-Calling in a Column

Name-calling aimed at a private person is not saved by calling the article a general report. Columnist Tony Figueroa wrote in his column "Footprints" in the People's Daily Forum that Bangkerohan public market was overrun by bullies and thugs. He named vendors' federation president Aproniano Rivera as one of the "leeches" and a "pseudobully" backed by city hall hooligans.[^207] The Regional Trial Court of Davao City convicted Figueroa and publisher-editor Rogelio Flaviano. It ordered them to pay Rivera ₱50,000.00 as moral damages and ₱10,000.00 as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed in all respects.[^208]

The Supreme Court read the entire article and found it defamatory. An article that only described market conditions would have had no need for the name-calling it was full of. In libel the question is not what the writer meant but what his words mean.[^209] The Supreme Court also held that Rivera was a private citizen, not a public officer, because membership in the market committee vested in him no sovereign function. The article was therefore not privileged under Article 354. Even if he were a public officer, the column was not a fair and true report without comments.[^210] Moral damages were proper under Article 2219(7) of the Civil Code. The petition was denied and the Court of Appeals decision was affirmed.[^211]

### [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67813): Sufficiency of a Libel Information

[*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67813) shows that the elements of Article 353 are first tested on the face of the Information. Two Informations charged Erwin Tulfo and the producers and reporters of an ABS-CBN program with libel for a July 23, 2004 broadcast accusing GMA-7 of stealing and pirating video footage.[^212] The Regional Trial Court found probable cause and denied their motions and the Court of Appeals upheld the Informations.[^213]

The Supreme Court ruled that both Informations alleged every element: the discreditable act of stealing and pirating footage, its publication by broadcast, the identity of GMA-7 and malice.[^214] Defenses on identifiability and malice were matters for trial. Even though GMA-7 admitted the unauthorized use of the footage, the imputation was presumed malicious under Article 354 because the presumption applies even to a true imputation absent good intention and justifiable motive. Whether the broadcast was a mere news report made with good motives was best left to trial.[^215] The petition was denied and the Court of Appeals decision was affirmed in toto.[^216]

### [*Lagaya v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55047): Imputing Psychiatric Behavior

An imputation of mental illness is defamatory. Dr. Alfonso Lagaya, an official of the Philippine Institute of Traditional and Alternative Health Care, issued a memorandum to the Plant Manager and staff of the Cagayan Valley herbal processing plant. It accused plant manager Dr. Marilyn Martinez of unauthorized lobbying and directed the staff to report information on "her psychiatric behavior."[^217] The Sandiganbayan convicted him of libel.[^218]

The Supreme Court found all four elements present. To an ordinary reader, a memorandum dwelling on abnormal behavior meant a judgment of mental deficiency.[^219] Malice was presumed and was also shown by a final Court of Appeals ruling on the same facts in the administrative case. Publication occurred when the memorandum reached the staff and Martinez was named.[^220] The memorandum was not privileged under Article 354(1) because it was addressed to plant staff who had no supervisory power over Martinez. It was not privileged under Article 354(2) because the remarks did not relate to her official duties.[^221] The Supreme Court partly granted the petition. It affirmed the conviction but imposed a fine of ₱6,000.00, with subsidiary imprisonment in case of insolvency, in lieu of imprisonment.[^222]

### [*Binay v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42104): A Minor's Adoption and Purchases

The private status of a public official's child is not fair game. A Pinoy Times article on the lifestyle of the Binay family said that 13-year-old Joanna Marie Bianca, described as the Binays' adopted daughter, bought panties costing ₱1,000.00 each.[^223] Her mother filed a libel complaint against the writer and the publisher. The City Prosecutor of Makati found a prima facie case and filed an Information. The Acting Secretary of Justice reversed and ordered its withdrawal and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^224]

The Supreme Court found publication and identity present and limited its review to defamation and malice.[^225] The paragraph was prima facie defamatory because it had nothing to do with her father's qualifications and exposed the child to the public as a spoiled and spendthrift adopted daughter. It was not a qualifiedly privileged communication, since no legal, moral or social duty required publishing her adoption and no public interest attached to her purchases.[^226] Privilege was in any event a matter of defense for trial and malice was presumed under Article 354. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and ordered the City Prosecutor of Makati to proceed with the libel case.[^227]

### [*Brillante v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46218): Unverified Intelligence Reports

A candidate's sense of public duty does not excuse spreading unconfirmed accusations to the press. In January 1988, Makati council candidate Roberto Brillante held a press conference before some 50 journalists. He accused Jejomar Binay of plotting the assassination of mayoral candidate Augusto Syjuco and circulated an open letter to President Aquino detailing the charge. The letter was published in several newspapers.[^228] The Regional Trial Courts of Manila and Makati convicted him of libel and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^229]

The Supreme Court held that the first three elements were not in dispute and that the case turned on malice.[^230] Brillante claimed a social duty to warn, but his charges rested only on unconfirmed intelligence reports. Above all, the letter was not sent only to persons with a corresponding duty to act. It was released to the press and published in newspapers of general circulation. The statements were therefore not privileged and malice was presumed.[^231] The Supreme Court also rejected his equal protection claim and applied the multiple publication rule to sustain the penalties. It found the moral damages excessive. It affirmed the Court of Appeals decisions but reduced the moral damages awarded to Dr. Nemesio Prudente to ₱500,000.00. It reduced the moral damages awarded to Binay and Francisco Baloloy to ₱500,000.00 and ₱25,000.00 in their respective cases.[^232]
### [*Belen v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62853): Insults in a Motion Before the Prosecutor

The absolute privilege of pleadings covers only statements that are relevant. Medel Arnaldo Belen, then a practicing lawyer, filed an estafa complaint against his uncle. When Assistant City Prosecutor Suñega-Lagman dismissed it without acting on his request for a clarificatory hearing, he filed an Omnibus Motion for reconsideration and disqualification. He mailed it in a sealed envelope to the Office of the City Prosecutor of San Pablo City, where the receiving and records staff handled it before it reached her.[^233] The prosecutor filed a libel complaint and an Information was filed. The Regional Trial Court convicted Belen and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^234]

The Supreme Court found publication. Belen knew that the sealed envelope would be opened and read by the office staff before reaching the prosecutor.[^235] It also held that the motion was not absolutely privileged. Privilege extends to preliminary investigations, but the statements must be relevant to the subject of the inquiry. Belen's statements failed that test. They imputed bribery and called the prosecutor an "idiot" and "imbecile" with "stupendous stupidity," which went beyond any legitimate criticism of her ruling.[^236] The petition was denied. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification and increased the fine to ₱6,000.00, with subsidiary imprisonment in case of insolvency.[^237]

### [*Maglasang v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67065): Unauthenticated Photocopies

The prosecution must prove the defamatory writing itself. Galileo Maglasang was charged with libel for a letter allegedly sent to the Philippine Coast Guard commander in Northern Mindanao, arising from a dispute over the management of a maritime school. The trial court convicted him and imposed a fine of ₱4,000.00 and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^238]

The Supreme Court held that proof of the letter was necessary for conviction.[^239] The prosecution offered only photocopies. The letter was a private document, but no witness authenticated its execution under Section 20 of Rule 132. The prosecution also failed to justify the photocopies under the best evidence rule.[^240] Its only witness, a Coast Guard community relations officer, did not know who sent him his copy and could not show that the original was lost. The private complainant never saw the original. Maglasang's failure to present evidence did not lift the burden that rests on the prosecution. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and acquitted him.[^241]

### [*Torralba v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42403): Blackening the Memory of the Dead

Article 353 protects the memory of the dead, but the broadcast must still be proved. In his radio program "Tug-Ani ang Lungsod" over DYFX, Cirse "Choy" Torralba allegedly called the late CFI Judge Agapito Hontanosas a wartime collaborator and a traitor to his native land. He was charged with libel in Tagbilaran City.[^242] The Regional Trial Court convicted him and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^243]

The Supreme Court disallowed the tape recording of the April 11, 1994 broadcast. The prosecution failed to lay its foundation because the person who actually recorded it, the witness's adopted daughter, was never presented.[^244] No witness saw Torralba make the broadcast. The witness who claimed to recognize his voice had an ax to grind, having been convicted of libel on Torralba's complaint. The private complainant admitted that he did not listen to the program.[^245] Finding the evidence insufficient to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt, the Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals, acquitted Torralba and ordered his cash bond released.[^246]

### [*Dio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62001): Curing an Information That Omits Publication

An Information that fails to allege publication may be amended before arraignment. Timothy Desmond, Chair of Subic Bay Marine Exploratorium, charged its Treasurer, Virginia Dio, with libel for e-mails accusing him and his wife of bleeding the company with a payroll of almost ₱1 million a month.[^247] The Regional Trial Court quashed the Informations for failure to allege publication. The Court of Appeals reversed.[^248]

The Supreme Court held that Rule 117, Section 4 requires the court to give the prosecution a chance to amend when the defect can be cured. Dio had not yet been arraigned.[^249] *Agustin v. Pamintuan*, as cited in [*Dio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62001), involved an accused who had already been arraigned. The contrary statement in *Leviste v. Alameda* was obiter dictum.[^250] Whether e-mails sent to officials of the Subic Bay Metropolitan Authority were sufficiently public under Articles 353 and 355 was a matter of defense for trial, as was Dio's claim of good faith. The petition was denied and the Court of Appeals decision was affirmed.[^251]
### [*Alonzo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/feb1995/gr_110088_1995.html): An Inspector's Official Report

A report made by a public officer to superiors in the line of duty is neither published nor presumed malicious. Dr. Merle Alonzo was the Field Operations Officer of the Philippine Medical Care Commission for Region XI. On orders of the Commission she inspected two Medicare-accredited clinics owned by Dr. Angeles Velasco and submitted her findings to the Commission's Vice-Chairman.[^252] The Regional Trial Court of Davao City convicted her of libel based on remarks in the report and the Court of Appeals affirmed in toto.[^253]

The Supreme Court held that the report fell within the first paragraph of Article 354. This negated the presumption of malice, so malice in fact had to be proved.[^254] The prosecution failed. Dr. Velasco's testimony that Alonzo was perhaps angry over refused favors conveyed only uncertainty and the Court of Appeals' theory of revenge rested on a mere rumor. There was also no publication in law. A communication by a public officer in the discharge of official duties to officers with a duty to act on it is not a publication. The later reading of the report by a lawyer did not count either, because the complainants themselves handed it to him.[^255] The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and acquitted Alonzo.[^256]

### [*Alcantara v. Ponce*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41255): A Newsletter Submitted to the Investigating Prosecutor

Absolute privilege extends to papers submitted in a preliminary investigation. During the preliminary investigation of an estafa complaint he had filed against Nicasio Alcantara, Vicente Ponce gave the investigating prosecutor a newsletter. It described the Alcantaras as an example of land grabbing, corruption of public office and corporate grabbing.[^257] The Makati City Prosecutor found probable cause for libel. The Secretary of Justice reversed and directed withdrawal of the Information because the newsletter was privileged. The trial court granted the withdrawal and the Court of Appeals found no grave abuse of discretion.[^258]

The Supreme Court adopted the rule that preliminary steps leading to judicial action are absolutely privileged, regardless of their defamatory tenor and the presence of malice, if they are relevant to the subject of the inquiry.[^259] The newsletter passed the liberal test of relevancy. Like the estafa complaint, it dealt with alleged rapacity and deceit and it was disclosed only to the official investigating the complaint. The petition was denied and the Court of Appeals decision and resolution were affirmed.[^260]

### [*Fortich v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34505): A Supervisor's Investigation Memorandum

The same privilege protects internal reports in private employment. Stanley Fortich was an area salesman of San Miguel Corporation in Dipolog City. His supervisor wrote a memorandum to management accusing him of misappropriating collections. It added that Fortich was an avid mahjong player and cockfighting enthusiast.[^261] Fortich sued for damages arising from libel. The Regional Trial Court awarded him ₱150,000.00 as moral damages, ₱50,000.00 as exemplary damages, ₱20,000.00 as attorney's fees and ₱1,000.00 as litigation expenses. The Court of Appeals reversed on the ground that the memorandum was privileged.[^262]

The Supreme Court found no publicity under Article 353. The memorandum was part of the initial investigation into the non-remittance of collections and was addressed to the proper company officials.[^263] As immediate supervisor, the writer had the duty to investigate irregularities in customer accounts. No malice was shown. Even if the paragraph on his vices could have been slanderous, it was an honest statement arising from a moral and legal obligation owed to the company. The petition was denied.[^264]

### [*Diaz v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43691): The Unidentified Miss S

An article may be libelous and still fail for want of an identifiable victim. Ogie Diaz's column "Pakurot" in the tabloid Bandera retold Philip Henson's lurid account of his sexual encounters with a "Miss S."[^265] Florinda Bagay, whose screen name was "Patricia Santillan," claimed that she was Miss S. The Regional Trial Court of Manila convicted Diaz and managing editor Manny Pichel. The Court of Appeals acquitted Pichel but sustained the conviction of Diaz.[^266]

The Supreme Court found the article defamatory, presumptively malicious and published in the December 28, 1991 issue of Bandera.[^267] The last element was missing. The article gave no description or other indication that Miss S and Florinda Bagay were the same person. Veteran entertainment writers and a talent manager testified that they had never heard of a Patricia Santillan and could not connect her to the article.[^268] Although the article was libelous, Bagay could not have been the person defamed. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and acquitted Diaz.[^269]
### [*Corpuz v. Del Rosario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35131): An Office Memorandum Between Assistant Solicitors General

At the preliminary investigation stage, the elements need only be shown prima facie. Assistant Solicitor General Roman del Rosario charged a fellow Assistant Solicitor General, Azucena Corpuz, with libel over her June 13, 1997 memorandum. It stated that there was no such thing as *palabra de honor* as far as he was concerned.[^270] The investigating prosecutor of Makati City found the words defamatory and found probable cause and an Information was filed. The Regional Prosecutor did not give due course to her appeal and the Court of Appeals denied her certiorari petition.[^271]

The Supreme Court found no error, much less grave abuse of discretion. The prosecutor had determined that the four elements were present, which showed prima facie a well-founded belief that a crime had been committed.[^272] Corpuz's claim that the memorandum was a private communication under Article 354(1) was evidentiary in nature. It was a matter of defense to be heard at trial. The petition was denied and the Court of Appeals decision was affirmed.[^273]

### [*Soriano v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64717): A Named Pastor and an Unnamed Congregation

Insults against a named religious leader are libelous, but sweeping attacks on his congregation are not. In his radio program "Ang Dating Daan," broadcast on July 31, 1998, Eliseo Soriano called the pastors who had predicted a De Venecia victory "gago," "tarantadong pastor" and "pastor ng demonyo."[^274] He was charged in two Informations. The Regional Trial Court of Iriga City convicted him of two counts of libel and imposed a fine of ₱6,000.00 for each case. The Court of Appeals affirmed.[^275]

The Supreme Court held the words clearly defamatory. No good motive could be inferred from them and they were not fair commentaries even though religion is a matter of public interest.[^276] Soriano's guilt, however, stemmed only from his statements against Evangelist Wilde Almeda, head of the Jesus Miracle Crusade International Ministry. Applying [*MVRS Publications, Inc. v. Islamic Da'wah Council of the Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48023), the Supreme Court held that declarations about a large class cannot be read as referring to an identifiable individual. The Information in Criminal Case No. IR-4848 named no specific pastor.[^277] The petition was partially granted. Soriano was held guilty in Criminal Case No. IR-5273 and acquitted in Criminal Case No. IR-4848.[^278]

### [*Almendras, Jr. v. Almendras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/58855): Letters Calling a Brother a Blackmailer

A letter is not privileged when its writer asks that it be spread widely. In 1996, Representative Alejandro Almendras, Jr. wrote to House Speaker Jose de Venecia, Jr. and others that his brother Alexis was "a reknown blackmailer" with no authority to transact business for him. He asked the Speaker's office to circulate the information to the officials and employees of the House.[^279] Alexis sued for damages. The petitioner presented no evidence except his Answer. The trial court awarded ₱5,000,000.00 as moral damages, ₱100,000.00 as exemplary damages, ₱10,000.00 for litigation expenses and attorney's fees of 25 percent of the amounts recovered.[^280]

The Supreme Court found the letters defamatory when read in their plain and natural meaning.[^281] They were not privileged. His request to circulate them showed a lack of selectivity that indicated malice.[^282] Because Alexis did not suffer grave damage to his reputation, the Supreme Court equitably reduced the award. It denied the petition but modified the Court of Appeals decision. Moral damages were reduced from ₱5,000,000.00 to ₱100,000.00 and exemplary damages from ₱100,000.00 to ₱20,000.00. Litigation expenses and attorney's fees were deleted.[^283]

### [*Trillanes IV v. Castillo-Marigomen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63938): Media Interviews Outside the Legislative Sphere

The elements of Article 353 also measure a civil complaint for defamation filed under Article 33 of the Civil Code. Senator Antonio Trillanes IV told the media during breaks in Senate hearings on the so-called Hacienda Binay that businessman Antonio Tiu was a "dummy" of then Vice President Binay.[^284] Tiu sued for damages in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, which denied the senator's motion to dismiss.[^285]

The Supreme Court held that parliamentary non-accountability does not cover statements made outside sessions, hearings or debates. Responding to media interviews is not an official function of a lawmaker.[^286] Measured against the four requisites of libel, the complaint sufficiently stated a cause of action. It alleged an unprivileged and presumptively malicious imputation that tarnished Tiu's reputation as a legitimate businessman.[^287] Whether Tiu was in truth a dummy or a public figure raised factual matters for a full-blown trial. The petition was dismissed and the trial court's orders were affirmed insofar as consistent with the decision.[^288]
### [*Lopez v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34876): Cadiz Forever, Bading and Sagay Never

The defamatory character of the words must be proved first and personal hurt is not enough. In November 2002, billboards in Cadiz City first read "CADIZ FOREVER" with a blank space before "NEVER." Days later the blank was filled with "BADING AND SAGAY." Dionisio Lopez admitted placing them. Mayor Salvador Escalante, known as "Bading," claimed they branded him a "tuta" or puppet of Sagay City.[^289] The Regional Trial Court of Cadiz City convicted Lopez of libel and the Court of Appeals affirmed with modification.[^290]

The Supreme Court held that the defamatory nature of the words must be proved first, because if the words are not defamatory, malice is immaterial.[^291] The phrase did not, directly or indirectly, call the mayor a puppet of Sagay City. His reading drew on circumstances in another billboard that was not the subject of the charge. The witnesses' negative impressions and the mayor's displeasure were insufficient, since personal hurt or embarrassment is not actionable by itself.[^292] The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and acquitted Lopez.[^293]

### [*Flor v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43704): Reliance on a Lone Source

A reporter who relies in good faith on one source does not act with reckless disregard. The Bicol Forum, with Noel Flor as managing editor, published Ramos's news story headlined "VILLAFUERTE'S DENIAL CONVINCES NO ONE." It reported that Governor Luis Villafuerte's trips to Japan and Israel were a junket funded by about ₱700,000.00 in cash advances.[^294] The story rested on a note from an unnamed source connected with the provincial treasurer's office. The Regional Trial Court convicted the editor and the correspondent and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^295]

The Supreme Court held that a public official may recover only when the statement was made with actual malice. Reckless disregard is not measured by what a prudent man would have investigated. It requires evidence that the writer in fact entertained serious doubts about the truth of what he published.[^296] The prosecution failed to meet that standard. The cash advances were already a public issue, the writers held information on them and slight errors in temperate criticism are excused. A reporter may rely on a lone source and placing on the accused the burden of proving the truth of charges of official misconduct would run counter to the Constitution.[^297] The headline had to be read with the story. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals decision and the trial court's joint decision.[^298]

### [*MVRS Publications, Inc. v. Islamic Da'wah Council of the Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48023): Group Libel

A sweeping slur against a large religious group gives no individual member a cause of action. The tabloid Bulgar published an article stating that Muslims worship the pig as their god. The Islamic Da'wah Council, a federation of more than 70 Muslim organizations and several individual Muslims sued for damages.[^299] The Regional Trial Court of Manila dismissed the complaint because the persons defamed were not specifically identified. The Court of Appeals reversed.[^300]

The Supreme Court En Banc held that words merely insulting are not actionable per se.[^301] Declarations about a large class cannot be read as referring to an identifiable individual unless circumstances point to a particular member. The Muslim community of over five million was too vast to determine who among its members was defamed. No individual Muslim had a cause of action.[^302] The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated the trial court's dismissal of the complaint.[^303]

### [*Magno v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40444): Wall Writings and an Unsealed Letter

Publication may occur through a wall facing a neighbor's house or through an unsealed letter. Dolores Magno painted on her garage wall that a "maniac at magnanakaw ng aso" named Cerelito was barred from passing. She later handed an unsealed envelope with an abusive unsigned letter to Cerelito's sister Evelyn for delivery to his wife Fe.[^304] The Regional Trial Court of Baguio City convicted her in two of four libel cases and ordered her to pay Cerelito ₱5,000.00 as moral damages in each. The Court of Appeals affirmed in toto.[^305]

The Supreme Court upheld the trial court's finding that Cerelito's son saw Dolores paint the wall writing.[^306] On the letter, defamation, malice and identity were not disputed and only publication was in issue.[^307] There was no publication to Evelyn because Cerelito himself voluntarily disclosed the letter to her. There was publication to Fe, however, since writing to a person other than the one defamed is publication. As to Fe, she was a third person. Dolores's authorship was shown by her own act of handing over the letter and by its contents.[^308] The petition was denied and the Court of Appeals decision was affirmed.[^309]

### [*Yambot v. Tuquero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36758): Reporting What a Source Said

A news report that faithfully quotes a party's charge does not by itself show malice. The Philippine Daily Inquirer published Volt Contreras's article "Judge mauled me, says court employee." It reported Robert Mendoza's account that Makati Judge Escolastico Cruz, Jr. punched him and Mendoza's statement that the judge had a pending sexual harassment case.[^310] No such case was pending, although a reply in another case had alleged sexual advances. The City Prosecutor of Makati filed a libel Information against Mendoza and the newspaper staff. The Secretary of Justice dismissed the staff's petition for review and the Court of Appeals dismissed their certiorari petition.[^311]

The Supreme Court held that the article merely reported what Mendoza said. A newspaper should not be held to account to the point of suppression for honest mistakes and a reporter without legal training cannot be expected to distinguish a sexual harassment case from an allegation of sexual advances.[^312] The inaccuracy was insufficient to establish malice. The article was a straightforward narration of the incident without comment from the reporter.[^313] The Supreme Court granted the petition and reversed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution.[^314]
### [*Manila Bulletin Publishing Corp. v. Domingo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63097): Two Columns on a Regional Director

The same writer may be cleared for different reasons on two articles. In his column "Bull's Eye" in the tabloid Tempo, Ruther Batuigas wrote on December 20, 1990 about a letter-complaint of DTI Region VIII employees accusing officials, including Regional Director Victor Domingo, of mismanagement and nepotism. A second column on January 4, 1991 spoke of Domingo's "lousy performance."[^315] Domingo filed a criminal complaint for libel and a separate civil action for damages in Palo, Leyte. The Regional Trial Court found Batuigas guilty and held him and Manila Bulletin liable and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^316]

The Supreme Court held that the first column was not defamatory when viewed in its entirety. Batuigas only relayed the specifics of the employees' complaints without ascribing them to Domingo as his own charge. His closing remarks were a figure of speech addressed to the incoming DTI Secretary.[^317] The second column was defamatory, but it was a fair comment on a public official's performance. Domingo did not prove actual malice. Batuigas relied on documents he had received and he had no personal ill will, since Domingo admitted never having met him. Failure to counter-check the status of the complaints did not amount to reckless disregard.[^318] With no libel committed, no civil liability arose. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals, acquitted Batuigas and dismissed the complaint for damages.[^319]

### [*Buatis, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40749): Lousy, Inutile and Carabao English

Insulting words in a dictated letter are defamatory and are published once others see the letter. Jose Buatis, Jr., head of an association claiming under the Rodriguez estate, answered a lawyer's demand letter with a reply calling it "lousy" and "inutile" and written in "carabao English." It also accused the lawyer of "stupidity" and called him "satan." The letter arrived open in the lawyer's mailbox, where his wife retrieved it.[^320] The trial court convicted Buatis. It ordered him to pay ₱20,000.00 as compensatory damages, ₱10,000.00 as moral damages and ₱10,000.00 as exemplary damages. The Court of Appeals affirmed.[^321]

The Supreme Court held that the words cast aspersion on the lawyer's character and exposed him to ridicule. No evidence aliunde was needed to prove it.[^322] Publication and identity were established. The letter was not a privileged communication. It gave no explanation of the member's claim to the premises and contained only irrelevant insults. Buatis also dictated it to a secretary instead of confining it to persons with an interest in the matter.[^323] The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals with modifications. It imposed a fine of ₱6,000.00, with subsidiary imprisonment in case of insolvency, in lieu of imprisonment and deleted the award of compensatory damages.[^324]

### [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663): Mandurugas in a Homeowners' Newsletter

Words are judged by what they mean, not by what the writer says he meant. Fernando Sazon, editor of the PML-Homemaker newsletter of a Marikina homeowners' association, lost patience with fellow member Abdon Reyes over an election protest. In the February 10, 1984 issue he called Reyes "mandurugas" and accused him of deceit.[^325] The Regional Trial Court of Pasig convicted Sazon of libel and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^326]

The Supreme Court held that branding Reyes "mandurugas" exposed him to public contempt and ridicule.[^327] Malice was presumed under Article 354. The article was not a private communication in the performance of a duty, because none of the homeowners had power of supervision over Reyes. It was not a protected comment on a public officer either, because it had no reference to his work as a public relations consultant.[^328] The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals with the modification that a fine of ₱3,000.00, with subsidiary imprisonment in case of insolvency, be imposed in lieu of imprisonment and fine.[^329]

### [*Fermin v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45169): A Tabloid on Celebrity Fugitives

Public figures receive wide latitude, but an article that goes beyond fair comment is still libelous. Gossip Tabloid, with Cristinelli Fermin as publisher and president, ran a June 1995 article on spouses Annabelle Rama Gutierrez and Eddie Gutierrez. It suggested that they had pocketed money from cookware customers in America and had fled to the Philippines to evade prosecution.[^330] The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City convicted Fermin and editor-in-chief Bogs Tugas. It ordered them to pay ₱500,000.00 as moral damages to each complainant and ₱50,000.00 as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals acquitted Tugas but affirmed Fermin's conviction.[^331]

The Supreme Court found evident imputations of the crime of malversation, of being fugitives from the law and of being a wastrel. The complainants refuted these imputations at trial.[^332] Fermin admitted her close association with then Congressman Golez, against whom Eddie Gutierrez ran and her plan to campaign for him. This showed motive. Freedom of the press does not cover statements that exceed fair comment.[^333] Given the latitude due to public figures and Administrative Circular No. 08-2008, the Supreme Court affirmed the conviction but imposed a fine of ₱6,000.00 in each case, with subsidiary imprisonment, in lieu of imprisonment. It increased the moral damages to each complainant from ₱300,000.00 to ₱500,000.00.[^334]

### [*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917): Posting a Carnapping Complaint After an Election

Posting another person's complaint on a bulletin board is publication and the poster cannot borrow the privilege of the pleading. Junar Orillo and Florencio Danieles lost the March 23, 2002 election of a jeepney operators and drivers association to Romeo Cabatian, who won as Vice President. On April 26, 2002, documents on a carnapping complaint against Cabatian appeared on the terminal bulletin board used for dispatch notices.[^335] The Regional Trial Court of Pasig City convicted them. It ordered them to pay Cabatian ₱200,000.00 as damages, ₱20,000.00 as acceptance fee, ₱20,000.00 as termination fee and ₱2,000.00 per court appearance. The Court of Appeals affirmed.[^336]

The Supreme Court held that the defamatory character and the identity of the victim were uncontested. Publication was established because association members read the posted documents at the terminal.[^337] Because Cabatian was a private individual, malice was presumed. The posting could not have been made out of duty, since the election was already over. The privilege attaching to pleadings in judicial proceedings belongs to the parties and their counsel, not to strangers who post them.[^338] Only the complaint against Cabatian, not any document in his favor, was posted. The petition was denied. The Supreme Court affirmed with modification and imposed on each petitioner a fine of ₱6,000.00, with subsidiary imprisonment in case of insolvency, in lieu of imprisonment.[^339]

### [*Arquiza v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69726): Absolute Privilege in Quasi-Judicial Proceedings

Godofredo Arquiza filed with the COMELEC a Petition to Deny Due Course to the certificate of nomination of Francisco Datol, Jr., third nominee of the Senior Citizen Party-List. It alleged that Datol had a string of criminal cases and was a fugitive from justice.[^340] The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City convicted Arquiza of libel and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^341]

The Supreme Court laid down a four-fold test for absolute privilege in quasi-judicial proceedings. It asks whether the statement was made in or as a necessary step to a quasi-judicial proceeding, whether the proceeding affords safeguards similar to judicial process, whether the statement is relevant and whether it was communicated only to those with a duty to act on it.[^342] All four were met. Cancellation of a certificate calls for the COMELEC's quasi-judicial power and the statements were the very grounds of the petition. Copies went only to the Clerk of the Commission and to the impleaded respondents, as the COMELEC rules require.[^343] The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and acquitted Arquiza.[^344]

### [*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827): Criticism of a Barangay Kagawad

The actual malice rule applies to oral defamation of public officers. Argelyn Labargan shouted from her terrace beside a highway that barangay kagawad Aileen Macabangon was "bugo, walay grado" and "ignorante." The remarks arose from Macabangon's supposed partiality in a barangay conciliation.[^345] The Municipal Circuit Trial Court convicted Labargan of grave oral defamation. The Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^346]

The Supreme Court accepted the lower courts' factual finding that Labargan uttered the words.[^347] It traced the doctrine on defamation of public officers from [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663) to [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818), which modified the older rule. When the imputation relates to official duties, the prosecution must prove actual malice.[^348] The words criticized Macabangon's competence as a kagawad and related to her official duties. The prosecution did not show that they were uttered with knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and acquitted Labargan.[^349]

### [*Peñalosa v. Ocampo, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68864): A 2011 Facebook Post

A Facebook post made before Republic Act No. 10175 cannot be punished under Article 355. Jannece Peñalosa was charged in Mandaluyong City with libel for an August 3, 2011 Facebook post against Jose Ocampo, Jr. The Regional Trial Court granted the motion to withdraw the Information. On Ocampo's certiorari petition, the Court of Appeals reversed and held that the post was punishable under Article 355.[^350]

The Supreme Court held that the remedy against an order granting withdrawal of an Information is an appeal, which only the State may bring through the Solicitor General. Ocampo, as private complainant, had no personality to file certiorari on the criminal aspect.[^351] On the merits, the trial judge did not gravely abuse her discretion. Under *noscitur a sociis*, the means listed in Article 355 exclude computer systems. Punishing the 2011 post under Article 355 would give a penal law retroactive effect unfavorable to the accused, contrary to Article 22.[^352] The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated the order dismissing the Information.[^353]

### [*Guingguing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42238): True Records About a Public Figure

True statements of public record about a public figure cannot sustain a libel conviction. Segundo Lim paid for a one-page advertisement in the Sunday Post, a weekly edited and published by Ciriaco Guingguing. It listed criminal cases filed against Cebu broadcaster Cirse "Choy" Torralba and showed photographs of his arrest.[^354] The Regional Trial Court of Cebu City convicted Lim and Guingguing and the Court of Appeals affirmed with modification.[^355]

The Supreme Court held that a conviction for libel of a public figure requires proof beyond reasonable doubt of actual malice.[^356] Torralba, who hosted radio programs heard across the Visayas and Mindanao, was a public figure. Nothing in the advertisement was untruthful. The cases listed had in fact been filed and were matters of public record bearing on the character of a public affairs broadcaster.[^357] The Supreme Court reversed the decisions of the Court of Appeals and the Regional Trial Court as to Guingguing and acquitted him.[^358]
### [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995): A Juridical Person Defamed on Radio

A school corporation may sue for libel and recover moral damages. On December 14 and 15, 1989, radio broadcasters Carmelo Rima and Hermogenes Alegre aired alleged complaints of students, teachers and parents against Ago Medical and Educational Center (AMEC). Their remarks included "greed for money on the part of AMEC's administrators."[^359] AMEC and its dean sued the broadcasters and the station operator for damages. The Regional Trial Court of Legazpi City held them liable and the Court of Appeals affirmed with modification and ordered them to pay moral damages, attorney's fees and costs.[^360]

The Supreme Court held that the broadcasts were made public and imputed to AMEC defects tending to cause it dishonor, discredit and contempt.[^361] Malice was presumed. The broadcasters did not name a single student who complained or verify the reports before airing them, so the remarks were not fair comment.[^362] A juridical person generally cannot recover moral damages. Article 2219(7) of the Civil Code, however, allows them in cases of libel without distinguishing between natural and juridical persons. Because AMEC suffered no substantial damage to its reputation, the award was reduced. The station operator was solidarily liable with its broadcasters.[^363] The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification. It reduced the moral damages from ₱300,000 to ₱150,000 and deleted the attorney's fees.[^364]

### [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46807): Reckless Disregard and a Lone Unnamed Source

The privilege of fair comment does not protect accusations made with reckless disregard. In his column in Remate, Erwin Tulfo accused Atty. Carlos "Ding" So of the Bureau of Customs of corruption and called him "pinakagago at magnanakaw" among members of his church.[^365] Four Informations were filed in Pasay City against Tulfo and Remate's editors and president. The Regional Trial Court convicted all of them and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^366]

The Supreme Court held that Tulfo admitted he did not know So and did no research beyond one unnamed source. The case was not one of honest mistake.[^367] The columns were not fair commentaries under [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796) nor fair and true reports under Article 354(2), since they gave no details and were baseless accusations. Even if privileged, they were written with reckless disregard. His later column attacking So after the case began was further evidence of malice.[^368] Witnesses identified So from the columns. The editors and the president were liable under Article 360 regardless of their participation.[^369] The petitions were dismissed. The Supreme Court affirmed with modifications, imposing a fine of ₱6,000 for each count in lieu of imprisonment and deleting actual and exemplary damages. Each accused was ordered to pay So, jointly and severally, ₱1,000,000 as moral damages.[^370]

### [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949): Malice in Fact Against a Private Businessman

Proof of malice in fact defeats any claim of qualified privilege. The Manila Chronicle published a series of articles calling businessman Alfonso Yuchengco a "Marcos crony" and a front for the Marcos and Romualdez clans. The articles also accused him of unsound business practices.[^371] The Regional Trial Court of Makati City held the publisher, its editors and writers and Roberto Coyuito, Jr. liable for damages. The Court of Appeals first affirmed in toto, then reversed itself on reconsideration and dismissed the complaint.[^372]

The Supreme Court held that a qualified privilege merely prevents the presumption of malice from attaching. It is futile once malice in fact is proven.[^373] The trial court and the original Court of Appeals decision found actual malice in the timing and frequency of the articles. In any event the articles were not fair commentaries. They dealt with Yuchengco's private business, not his public positions and he had not thrust himself into a public controversy, so he was neither a public officer nor a public figure in that matter.[^374] The Supreme Court partly granted the petition and reinstated the trial court decision with reduced damages. On the first cause of action, the publisher, editors and writers were held solidarily liable for ₱2,000,000.00 as moral damages and ₱500,000.00 as exemplary damages. On the second, Coyuito and the publisher were held liable for ₱25,000,000.00 as moral damages and ₱10,000,000.00 as exemplary damages. All defendants were held liable for ₱1,000,000.00 as attorney's fees and legal costs.[^375]

## Key Cases

- [*Causing v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70337)[^394] (April 8, 2026 En Banc Resolution): Clarified that libel is defined in Article 353 while [Article 355](art-355.md) and [Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175](../../cybercrime-prevention-act-of-2012-ra-10175/chapter-02-punishable-acts/sec-04.md) specify modes of commission, reconciling [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650) and [*Peñalosa v. Ocampo, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68864).
- [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^395] (2026 Decision): Applied Article 353's four elements to Facebook posts, finding third-person identifiability of an unnamed mayor from extrinsic clues while acquitting the poster because the prosecution failed to prove actual malice beyond reasonable doubt.
- [*Arquiza v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69726)[^396] (2024 Decision): Adopted a four-fold test extending absolute privilege to quasi-judicial proceedings and held that filing a required pleading with an office having a duty to act and serving impleaded parties does not constitute publication.
- [*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827)[^397] (2023 Decision): Acquitted an accused of grave oral defamation against a barangay councilor and expressly recognized that [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818) modified [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663) by placing the burden of proving actual malice on the prosecution.
- [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^398] (2021 Decision): Reversed a civil libel judgment because a plain report replicating what an official source said was not defamatory despite an inadvertent misattribution and fell within qualifiedly privileged fair reports on matters of public interest.
- [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818)[^399] (2021 Decision): Held that in criminal libel involving a public officer the prosecution bears the burden of proving actual malice beyond reasonable doubt rather than the defense bearing the burden of disproving it.
- [*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112)[^400] (2021 Decision): Acquitted a columnist and editors after construing corruption columns in their entirety, holding that mere insults are not libelous *per se* and that linking lifestyle allegations to official misconduct required proof of actual malice.
- [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650)[^401] (February 18, 2014 En Banc Decision) and [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56830)[^402] (April 22, 2014 En Banc Resolution): Upheld Article 353, held that cyberlibel is the same Article 353 offense transposed to cyberspace, limited online liability to the original author and barred dual prosecution under Article 353 and Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175.
- [*Guingguing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42238)[^403] (2005 Decision): Held that in criminal libel involving a public figure the prosecution must prove actual malice beyond reasonable doubt and that true statements on matters of public record concerning a public figure are not libelous.
- [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995)[^404] (2005 Decision): Held that a juridical person such as an educational corporation can validly complain of libel under Article 353 and recover moral damages under Article 2219(7) of the Civil Code for libelous *per se* radio broadcasts.
- [*MVRS Publications, Inc. v. Islamic Da'wah Council of the Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48023)[^405] (2003 En Banc Decision): Established that mere insulting words are not libelous *per se* and that sweeping remarks directed at a large class or religious group give no individual member a cause of action for defamation.
- [*Vasquez v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38132)[^406] (1999 En Banc Decision): Adopted the constitutional actual-malice rule for criticisms of official conduct, acquitting a citizen even without proof of truth because the prosecution failed to prove knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard.
- [*Borjal v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29796)[^407] (1999 Decision): Dismissed a civil libel suit for lack of third-person identifiability in blind-item columns and recognized fair commentaries on matters of public interest as qualifiedly privileged beyond [Article 354](art-354.md).

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1:** Citizen M posted a public status on Facebook complaining that the chief executive in the orange shirt at their municipal hall refused to release business permits without *padulas*. Town Mayor O, whose well-known campaign color was orange, filed a complaint for cyberlibel under Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175. Two local residents testified that they immediately knew the post referred to Mayor O. At trial, the prosecution presented no proof that M knew the bribery claim was false. Instead, the prosecution argued that M had a family business grudge against Mayor O, did not verify the rumor before posting, apologized later and bore the burden of proving the truth of her accusation. Rule on M's criminal liability.

**Answer:** M must be acquitted. Under [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650)[^408] and [*Causing v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70337)[^409], cyberlibel under Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175 is governed by the definition and four elements of libel in Article 353 of the Revised Penal Code. As the Supreme Court held on parallel facts in [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^410], the first three elements are present because accusing a town executive of demanding *padulas* imputes a discreditable act, posting publicly on Facebook read by witnesses satisfies publication and third-person recognition through the mayor's office and signature orange campaign color establishes identity even without naming him. However, under [*Vasquez v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38132)[^411], [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818)[^412] and [*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827)[^413], when an imputation concerns a public officer's discharge of official duties, the prosecution bears the burden of proving actual malice beyond reasonable doubt rather than the defense bearing the burden of proving truth. Under [*Tan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70517)[^414], a prior grudge, failure to verify beforehand and subsequent apologies do not prove that M knew the statement was false or entertained serious doubts as to its truth, requiring M's acquittal.

**Question 2:** Reporter R of Daily Press wrote a news story based on a written statement handed to her by Commissioner C of a government anti-graft commission, reporting that Chairwoman Y of the commission had called former Minister E a plunderer over coconut levy funds. Chairwoman Y later denied making the statement. Without filing a criminal complaint, E instituted an independent civil action for defamation under Article 33 of the Civil Code against Daily Press and R, arguing that R's failure to verify the quote directly with Y before publication rendered the newspaper civilly liable. Decide the civil action.

**Answer:** The civil complaint must be dismissed. Under [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949)[^415] and [*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302)[^416], an independent civil action for defamation under [Article 33 of the Civil Code](../../../03-civil-law/civil-code/preliminary-title/art-0033.md) requires proof of the four elements of libel under Article 353 of the Revised Penal Code by preponderance of evidence. As the Supreme Court held in [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^417], a news report that merely replicates what an official source stated without adopting the accusation as the writer's own is a plain report that a person said those words and does not constitute a defamatory imputation by the newspaper even if inadvertently attributed to the wrong official. Moreover, under [*Yambot v. Tuquero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36758)[^418] and [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^419], fair reports on matters of public interest are qualifiedly privileged, shifting the burden to public figure E to prove actual malice. Relying on a lone official source without counter-checking does not establish a high degree of awareness of probable falsity under [*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302)[^420], [*Flor v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43704)[^421] and [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)[^422].

**Question 3:** Assess whether the elements of publication and identity under Article 353 are satisfied in two situations. First, Nominee A filed a verified petition to deny due course with the Clerk of the Commission on Elections alleging that rival party-list nominee B was a fugitive from justice based on a namesake's record and furnished copies only to B and the other impleaded respondents as required by COMELEC rules. Second, radio hosts aired a morning broadcast calling X Medical College Corp. a dumping ground of moral and physical misfits offering unaccredited courses, prompting both X Medical College Corp. and its Dean D (who was never mentioned in the broadcast) to sue for defamation.

**Answer:** In the first situation, actionable publication is absent and absolute privilege bars liability. As the Supreme Court held in [*Arquiza v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69726)[^423] (applying the rule in [*Alonzo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/feb1995/gr_110088_1995.html)[^424]), sending a communication exclusively to an office having an official duty to act on its subject matter and furnishing copies only to legally required parties in a quasi-judicial proceeding does not amount to publication in libel. In the second situation, publication and corporate identity are established as to X Medical College Corp. but not as to Dean D. Under Article 353 and [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995)[^425], a juridical person such as an educational corporation can validly complain of libel and recover moral damages under Article 2219(7) of the Civil Code when maligned by libelous *per se* radio broadcasts, whereas an unnamed school dean cannot recover personally when the defamatory broadcasts targeted only the corporate institution.

# Quotes

## Single Substantive Definition Across Print and Cyberspace

> "The Court agrees with the Solicitor General that libel is not a constitutionally protected speech and that the government has an obligation to protect private individuals from defamation. Indeed, cyberlibel is actually not a new crime since Article 353, in relation to Article 355 of the penal code, already punishes it. In effect, Section 4(c)(4) above merely affirms that online defamation constitutes “similar means” for committing libel."[^426]

> "The movants argue that Section 4(c)(4) is both vague and overbroad. But, again, online libel is not a new crime. It is essentially the old crime of libel found in the 1930 Revised Penal Code and transposed to operate in the cyberspace. Consequently, the mass of jurisprudence that secures the freedom of expression from its reach applies to online libel. Any apprehended vagueness in its provisions has long been settled by precedents."[^427]

> "Still, it is incorrect to state that Libel in Section 4(c)(4) of the Cybercrime Prevention Act is an entirely new crime that was not previously punished in the Revised Penal Code. The OSG overlooks the fact that libel is not defined in Article 355 of the Revised Penal Code, but in Article 353 thereof, to wit:"[^428]

## Construction of Defamatory Words, Plain Replication and Mere Insults

> "In determining whether a statement is defamatory, the words used are to be construed in their entirety and should be taken in their plain, natural and ordinary meaning as they would naturally be understood by persons reading them, unless it appears that they were used and understood in another sense."[^429]

> "The factual circumstances of Domingo are similar to the case at bar. A closer look at the article involved in this case reveals that it was not Cueto, the author of the article, who was asserting that Enrile was a "plunderer" or a "Marcos crony." In both of the paragraphs complained of, the author was merely repeating a supposed statement from PCGG Chairperson Yorac. It is true that Yorac subsequently disclaimed ownership of any of such statements, but the foregoing fact did not thereby make the defamatory imputations automatically from Inquirer or Cueto."[^430]

> "It must be stressed that words which are merely insulting are not actionable as libel or slander per se, and mere words of general abuse however opprobrious, ill-natured, or vexatious, whether written or spoken, do not constitute a basis for an action for defamation in the absence of an allegation for special damages. The fact that the language is offensive to the plaintiff does not make it actionable by itself."[^431]

## Non-Publication in Duty Communications and Juridical Persons as Offended Parties

> "Finally, as regards the non-publication test, We have laid down the rule that when a person sends a communication to an office/officer which/who has a duty to perform with respect to the subject matter of the communication, such communication does not amount to publication. Under COMELEC Resolution No. 9366, a petition to deny due course or to cancel a certificate of nomination of party-list nominees may only be filed with the Office of the Clerk of the Commission and no other office. Further, a copy of the petition must be furnished to the respondent. Private complainant admitted that only he and the other parties in the Petition to Deny Due Course were given copies thereof. Contrary to the ruling of the CA, the fact that Santos was also given a copy does not amount to publication because he is impleaded therein and Resolution No. 9366 requires that the petition be furnished to respondents."[^432]

> "Nevertheless, AMEC’s claim for moral damages falls under item 7 of Article 2219 of the Civil Code. This provision expressly authorizes the recovery of moral damages in cases of libel, slander or any other form of defamation. Article 2219(7) does not qualify whether the plaintiff is a natural or juridical person. Therefore, a juridical person such as a corporation can validly complain for libel or any other form of defamation and claim for moral damages."[^433]

## Actual Malice, Reckless Disregard and Burden of Proof

> "There is “actual malice” or malice in fact when the offender makes the defamatory statement with the knowledge that it is false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not. The reckless disregard standard used here requires a high degree of awareness of probable falsity. There must be sufficient evidence to permit the conclusion that the accused in fact entertained serious doubts as to the truth of the statement he published. Gross or even extreme negligence is not sufficient to establish actual malice."[^434]

> "In convicting the defendants, the lower courts paid particular heed to Article 354 of the Revised Penal Code, which provides that "every defamatory imputation is presumed to be malicious, even if it be true, if no good intention and justifiable motive for making it is shown...". We hold that this provision, as applied to public figures complaining of criminal libel, must be construed in light of the constitutional guarantee of free expression, and this Court's precedents upholding the standard of actual malice with the necessary implication that a statement regarding a public figure if true is not libelous. The provision itself allows for such leeway, accepting as a defense "good intention and justifiable motive." The exercise of free expression, and its concordant assurance of commentary on public affairs and public figures, certainly qualify as "justifiable motive," if not "good intention.""[^435]

> "This doctrine was modified in Daquer, Jr. v. People. This Court clarified that "[i]n cases of criminal libel where public figures, particularly public officers, are private complainants,...it is the burden of the prosecution to prove actual malice, and not the defense's burden to disprove.""[^436]

> "When malice in fact is proven, assertions and proofs that the libelous articles are qualifiedly privileged communications are futile, since being qualifiedly privileged communications merely prevents the presumption of malice from attaching in a defamatory imputation."[^437]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Test All Four Article 353 Elements in Sequence Across Every Mode of Defamation.** Whether a case involves print or broadcast libel under [Article 355](art-355.md), oral defamation under [Article 358](art-358.md) ([*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827)), online libel under [Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175](../../cybercrime-prevention-act-of-2012-ra-10175/chapter-02-punishable-acts/sec-04.md) ([*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650) and [*Causing v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70337)) or an independent civil action under [Article 33 of the Civil Code](../../../03-civil-law/civil-code/preliminary-title/art-0033.md) ([*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949) and [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)), verify all four elements in order: (1) defamatory imputation, (2) third-person publication, (3) third-person identifiability of a protected subject and (4) malice. Failure of any single element defeats criminal and civil liability ([*Manila Bulletin Publishing Corp. v. Domingo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63097) and [*Lopez v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34876)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Separate Plain Replication and Mere Insults From Defamation *Per Se*.** Construing the text as a whole in its plain reader sense ([*Tulfo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67112) and [*Flor v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43704)), check first whether the writer adopted a discreditable accusation or merely replicated what an identified source said ([*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)) or issued a factual business notice ([*Insular Life Assurance Co., Ltd. v. Serrano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43928) and [*Novicio v. Aggabao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47189)). Likewise, distinguish non-actionable insults or hurt feelings ([*MVRS Publications, Inc. v. Islamic Da'wah Council of the Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48023), [*Lopez v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34876) and [*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827)) from concrete imputations of crime, vice, mental defect or disreputable status ([*Fermin v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45169), [*Lagaya v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55047) and [*Filipinas Broadcasting Network, Inc. v. Ago Medical and Educational Center-Bicol Christian College of Medicine*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42995)).

> [!TIP]
> **Check Both Non-Publication Exceptions Before Reaching Privilege.** Even before analyzing privilege under [Article 354](art-354.md), check whether actionable publication occurred at all. Sending an official report, corporate audit memo or quasi-judicial pleading exclusively to an officer or body with a duty to act and legally required parties is not publication ([*Alonzo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/feb1995/gr_110088_1995.html), [*Fortich v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34505) and [*Arquiza v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69726)). Voluntary disclosure of a letter by the offended party to third persons is likewise not attributable to the author ([*Alonzo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/feb1995/gr_110088_1995.html) and [*Magno v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40444)), unless the author addressed a letter defaming one spouse to both spouses ([*Magno v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40444)) or posted a stranger's complaint on a public bulletin board ([*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Apply the Subjective Reckless-Disregard Test and Prosecutorial Burden for Public Officers and Public Figures.** Presumed malice applies only to unprivileged imputations against private individuals ([*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650) and [*Orillo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68917)) or attacks on purely private life unrelated to public office or public interest ([*Fermin v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45169), [*Yuchengco v. The Manila Chronicle Publishing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50949) and [*Sazon v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33663)). When the statement concerns a public official's duties or a public figure on a public issue, the prosecution (or civil plaintiff) bears the affirmative burden of proving actual knowledge of falsity or subjective serious doubts as to truth ([*Vasquez v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38132), [*Guingguing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42238), [*Daquer, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67818) and [*Labargan v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69827)). Relying on a lone source without counter-checking does not by itself establish reckless disregard ([*Villanueva v. Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49302), [*Flor v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43704) and [*Philippine Daily Inquirer, Inc. v. Enrile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67762)).

> [!TIP]
> **Guard Against Online Reactor Charges and Double Jeopardy.** Under [*Disini, Jr. v. Secretary of Justice*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56650), online libel attaches only to the original author of a defamatory post (excluding mere recipients who like, share or comment without adding a new defamatory imputation) and an offender cannot be prosecuted simultaneously under Article 353 of the Revised Penal Code and Section 4(c)(4) of Republic Act No. 10175 for the same online post.
