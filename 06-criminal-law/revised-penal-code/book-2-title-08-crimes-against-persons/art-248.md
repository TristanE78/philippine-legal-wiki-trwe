> Art. 248. Murder. - Any person who, not falling within the provisions of Article 246 shall kill another, shall be guilty of murder and shall be punished by reclusion perpetua, to death if committed with any of the following attendant circumstances:
>
> With treachery, taking advantage of superior strength, with the aid of armed men, or employing means to weaken the defense or of means or persons to insure or afford impunity.
>
> In consideration of a price, reward or promise.
>
> By means of inundation, fire, poison, explosion, shipwreck, stranding of a vessel, derailment or assault upon a railroad, fall of an airship, or by means of motor vehicles, or with the use of any other means involving great waste and ruin.
>
> On occasion of any of the calamities enumerated in the preceding paragraph, or of an earthquake, eruption of a volcano, destructive cyclone, epidemic or other public calamity.
>
> With evident premeditation.
>
> With cruelty, by deliberately and inhumanly augmenting the suffering of the victim, or outraging or scoffing at his person or corpse.

# Explanation

## Concept, Scope and Elements of Murder

Article 248 defines and penalizes murder as the unlawful killing of another person that is neither parricide nor infanticide and is attended by any of the six groups of qualifying circumstances in the statute.[^2] The presence of any one of those enumerated circumstances suffices to qualify an unlawful killing from homicide under [Article 249](art-249.md) to murder under Article 248.[^3] When more than one attendant circumstance is alleged and proven beyond reasonable doubt, only one circumstance qualifies the killing to murder.[^4] Any remaining proven circumstance operates as a generic aggravating circumstance in fixing the penalty and civil liabilities.[^5]

In [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^6] and [*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643)[^7], the Supreme Court held that to convict an accused of murder, the prosecution must prove the following elements:

1. that a person was killed;

2. that the accused killed him or her;

3. that the killing was attended by any of the qualifying circumstances mentioned in Article 248 of the Revised Penal Code; and

4. that the killing is not parricide or infanticide.[^8]

### Specific Intent to Kill, Motive and *Corpus Delicti*

Murder is a crime requiring specific intent to kill.[^9] In [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059), the Supreme Court distinguished specific intent from motive.[^10] Specific intent refers to the state of mind in which the offender actively desires the victim's death to follow his act.[^11] It must be alleged in the Information and proven at trial.[^12] When the victim dies from a deliberate act of the malefactor, intent to kill is conclusively presumed.[^13] When the victim survives, [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) (quoting *Esqueda v. People*)[^14] and [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^15] explain that intent to kill separates attempted or frustrated murder from physical injuries. Courts infer that intent from motive, the nature or number of weapons used, the nature and number of wounds inflicted, the manner of attack and the words uttered by the offender.[^16]

Motive, by contrast, is the reason that prompts the accused to commit the crime and is not an essential element of murder.[^17] As the Supreme Court ruled in [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059)[^18], [*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339)[^19] and [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532)[^20], lack of proof of motive does not bar a conviction when eyewitnesses positively identify the assailant. Motive becomes material only when the evidence is circumstantial or inconclusive and the assailant's identity is in doubt.[^21]

In every prosecution for murder or homicide, [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059) holds that the prosecution must prove the *corpus delicti* beyond reasonable doubt through direct or circumstantial evidence.[^22] *Corpus delicti* in felonious killings consists of the death of the person alleged to be dead, proof that the death resulted from someone else's criminal act rather than accident, natural cause or suicide and proof that the accused was criminally responsible for that act.[^23] Under Section 4, Rule 133 of the Revised Rules of Evidence as applied in [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059), circumstantial evidence supports a conviction when more than one circumstance is present, the underlying facts are proven and their combination establishes guilt beyond reasonable doubt.[^24]

## Pleading Qualifying and Aggravating Circumstances in the Information

Under Sections 8 and 9, Rule 110 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure, an Information must specify qualifying and aggravating circumstances in ordinary and concise language sufficient to enable a person of common understanding to know the offense and attendant circumstances charged.[^25] Prior to 2019, Supreme Court decisions diverged between two views on how treachery must be alleged.[^26] One line held that merely alleging the statutory phrase "with treachery" sufficed, while another line held that such phrases were conclusions of law unless accompanied by factual averments describing the specific acts constituting treachery.[^27]

### The En Banc Guidelines in [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)

In [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) (promulgated August 6, 2019), the Supreme Court En Banc resolved this conflict and issued prospective policy guidelines for the Bench and the Bar.[^28] Enforcing the constitutional right of the accused to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation, [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) held that prosecutors cannot merely state that a killing was committed with treachery, abuse of superior strength or evident premeditation without describing the specific acts constituting those circumstances.[^29] Under the [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) guidelines:

1. Any Information alleging a qualifying or aggravating circumstance in which the law uses a broad term embracing various situations (such as treachery, abuse of superior strength, evident premeditation or cruelty) must state the ultimate facts relative to that circumstance. Alternatively, it may reference the pertinent portions of the resolution finding probable cause attached to the Information under Section 8(a), Rule 112 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure.[^30]

2. If the Information fails to state those ultimate facts, the accused may assail the defect before arraignment through a motion to quash under Section 3(e), Rule 117 for failure to conform substantially to the prescribed form or through a motion for a bill of particulars.[^31]

3. Failure of the accused to avail of either remedy prior to entering a plea waives the right to question the defective statement in the Information. The qualifying or aggravating circumstance may then be appreciated if proven beyond reasonable doubt during trial.[^32]

4. Cases that attained finality before [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) remain undisturbed under conclusiveness of judgment. Cases pending before trial courts may be amended when still permissible under the Rules, while cases pending on appeal turn on whether the accused preserved or waived the objection below.[^33]

Applying these rules to the appeal before it, [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) reversed the January 13, 2015 Decision of the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CR-HC No. 05757.[^34] The appellate court had downgraded the murder conviction rendered by the Regional Trial Court of Las Piñas City, Branch 202 in Criminal Case No. 08-0616 to homicide (modifying the trial court's award of P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral damages and P25,000.00 exemplary damages to P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral damages and P25,000.00 temperate damages).[^35] Because Rolando Solar y Dumbrique pleaded and went to trial without filing a motion to quash or a motion for a bill of particulars, he waived the formal defect in the Information.[^36] The eyewitness saw Rolando and Mark Kenneth Solar hit Joseph Capinig y Mato on the nape with a baseball bat.[^37] The Court of Appeals found conspiracy from their concerted action.[^38] Adopting the factual findings of the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court convicted Rolando Solar y Dumbrique of murder, sentenced him to *reclusion perpetua* and ordered him to pay the heirs of Joseph Capinig y Mato P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P75,000.00 moral damages, P75,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 temperate damages, with 6% annual legal interest from finality until fully paid.[^39]

### Exceptions Where Descriptive Facts Suffice Without Statutory Labels and Formal Post-Plea Amendments

Even when an Information omits the exact statutory label of a qualifying circumstance, factual averments that unmistakably describe that circumstance satisfy the constitutional right to be informed. In [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678), the Supreme Court held that alleging in the Information that the hacked victim was a one-year-old minor suffices to qualify the killing to murder by treachery without detailing the manner of assault, because killing a child of tender years is *ipso facto* treacherous.[^40] Similarly, in [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783), an Information that omitted the phrase "use of fire" but alleged that the accused poured gasoline on the two victims and lit them, causing fatal third-degree burns, validly charged murder by means of fire under Article 248, paragraph 3.[^41] Likewise, in [*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759), alleging that the accused hit his employer with wood and thereafter cut him into pieces using a saw sufficiently pleaded outraging or scoffing at the corpse under Article 248, paragraph 6.[^42]

Conversely, a circumstance completely omitted from the Information cannot be appreciated to increase the criminal penalty even if proven at trial. The Supreme Court applied Sections 8 and 9 of Rule 110 retroactively to pending appeals to bar unpleaded aggravating circumstances in [*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339) (unpleaded cruelty)[^43], [*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833) (unpleaded nighttime)[^44], [*People v. Abrazaldo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47695) (unpleaded nighttime and commission where public authorities were discharging duties)[^45], [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059) (unpleaded dwelling and lack of firearm license)[^46], [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656) (unpleaded dwelling in the frustrated murder Information)[^47], [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021) (unpleaded unlawful possession of explosives)[^48] and [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678) (unpleaded positive test for THC-metabolites under Section 25 of Republic Act No. 9165).[^49] However, in [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584), the Supreme Court held under Section 14, Rule 110 that amending a murder Information after arraignment to insert the generic aggravating circumstances of dwelling and insult or disregard of rank, age or sex is a permissible formal amendment.[^50] That amendment does not charge a different offense and affects only the penalty range, especially where the defense raised no objection to the evidence.[^51]

## Attendant Qualifying Circumstances Under Article 248

### Paragraph 1: Treachery, Superior Strength and Related Modes

#### Treachery (*Alevosia*)

Under [Article 14](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-014.md), paragraph 16 of the Revised Penal Code, treachery exists when the offender commits a crime against persons by employing means, methods or forms in its execution that tend directly and specially to ensure its execution without risk to himself from any defense the offended party might make.[^52] Across [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^53], [*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712)[^54], [*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643)[^55], [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198)[^56] and [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)[^57], two concurring conditions must be proven as conclusively as the killing itself:

1. the employment of means, methods or manner of execution that gives the person attacked no opportunity to defend himself or herself or to retaliate; and

2. the deliberate or conscious adoption of such means, methods or manner of execution by the offender.[^58]

Treachery was proven in [*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643), where the assailant greeted the victim and announced that the victim owed him money. After the victim paid him PhP 50 and came out of an alley, the assailant suddenly placed his arm around him and slit his neck with a two-foot samurai.[^59] It was likewise established in [*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833)[^60] and [*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339)[^61], where the assailants approached unsuspecting victims from behind and stabbed them without warning. Treachery also attended the shooting in [*People v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36488), where the appellant shot a victim lying prostrate with his leg pinned beneath a motorcycle after a co-assailant shot him in the back.[^62]

A frontal attack does not negate treachery when the assault is sudden and unexpected and leaves an unarmed victim unable to defend himself or retaliate. The Supreme Court applied this rule in [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532) (suddenly firing caliber .30 carbines at unarmed youths riding a tricycle)[^63], [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591) (suddenly firing a gun at an unsuspecting group without provocation)[^64] and [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656) (suddenly mauling and shooting an unarmed woman called out of her house and stabbing a caretaker four times while his hands were tied).[^65] Nor does a prior altercation bar treachery when an appreciable interval separates the quarrel from a later ambush. In [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198), after a companion taunted and kicked the victim on the road, the appellant and his companion sped ahead on their motorcycle, hid near banana shrubs and suddenly shot the unarmed cousins as they pushed their empty motorbike home.[^66] Furthermore, when an adult unlawfully attacks a child of tender years, treachery is present *ipso facto* even without proof of how the assault began, as applied to a 13-year-old girl and a three-and-a-half-year-old toddler in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^67] and to a one-year-old baby in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678).[^68]

By contrast, treachery cannot rest on mere suppositions and fails in three recurring evidentiary situations:

- **Unwitnessed inception of the attack:** When no eyewitness saw how the aggression began, treachery cannot be appreciated even if the victim was earlier abducted while defenseless ([*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059))[^69], stabbed 18 times inside her home ([*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584))[^70], struck inside a factory with defense wounds on his hand ([*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759))[^71] or shot outside a house where the lone eyewitness opened the door only at the moment of gunfire after the victim called for help ([*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712)).[^72]
- **Chance encounters and public attacks without conscious preparation:** In [*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712), a chance encounter outside the house of the victim's godmother negated conscious selection of a risk-free mode of attack.[^73] In [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678), hacking adult neighbors in a public place in full view of witnesses and relatives showed no conscious preparation to eliminate risk, because mere suddenness does not establish treachery.[^74]
- **Prior shouting and face-to-face grappling:** In [*People v. Abrazaldo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47695), where the eyewitness testified that the accused and the *barangay tanod* shouted loudly at each other and grappled face to face before the stabbing, the victim was forewarned, requiring the conviction to be downgraded to homicide.[^75]

#### Taking Advantage of Superior Strength and Absorption Into Treachery

To take advantage of superior strength means to purposely use excessive force out of proportion to the means of defense available to the person attacked.[^76] In [*People v. Regalario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47107), five men (including three wielding wooden nightsticks called *bahi* and one holding a seven-inch knife) chased an unarmed victim, blocked his escape and beat him in turns, qualifying the killing to murder under Article 248, paragraph 1.[^77] Similarly, in [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584), a male driver armed with a kitchen knife who overpowered his unarmed female employer and inflicted 18 stab wounds took advantage of superior strength (appreciated as a generic aggravating circumstance because evident premeditation qualified the murder).[^78]

Numerical superiority and weapons do not establish this circumstance automatically. In [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059), the bare fact that armed malefactors outnumbered the unarmed victim during his abduction did not prove that they deliberately cooperated to exploit their combined strength at the unwitnessed moment of the killing.[^79] Moreover, when treachery is proven, [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532) holds that abuse of superior strength is absorbed in treachery and cannot operate as a separate generic aggravating circumstance.[^80]

#### Other Modes in Paragraph 1

Paragraph 1 also enumerates killing with the aid of armed men, employing means to weaken the defense or employing means or persons to ensure or afford impunity. None of the decisions discussed on this page applies those three clauses to sustain a conviction, although the statutory text is quoted in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^81], [*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643)[^82] and [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783).[^83]

### Paragraph 2: Price, Reward or Promise

Under Article 248, paragraph 2, killing another in consideration of a price, reward or promise qualifies the felony to murder.[^84] In [*People v. Gerolaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34166), the Supreme Court explained that when proven, this qualifying circumstance affects both the offeror as a principal by inducement and the offeree as a principal by direct participation.[^85] The circumstance does not apply when the reward was offered only for identifying and apprehending a suspect.[^86] In [*People v. Gerolaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34166), spouses harassed by extortion letters signed in the name of an alleged rebel commander offered P3,000.00 to anyone who could apprehend the letter-writer.[^87] An unarmed former employee entered a compound to identify the suspect and inflicted seven wounds on him after the suspect lunged with a double-bladed dagger.[^88] Because the P3,000.00 reward was offered solely to identify and apprehend the extortionist rather than to kill anyone and an uncounselled extrajudicial confession was void under Section 12(1), Article III of the 1987 Constitution, neither price or reward nor conspiracy was proven beyond reasonable doubt.[^89] The Supreme Court acquitted the alleged offeror and houseboy and convicted the stabber only of homicide.[^90]

### Paragraph 3: Fire, Explosion, Motor Vehicles and Other Destructive Means

Article 248, paragraph 3 qualifies a killing committed by means of inundation, fire, poison, explosion, shipwreck, stranding of a vessel, derailment or assault upon a railroad, fall of an airship, by means of motor vehicles or with the use of any other means involving great waste and ruin.[^91] Three clauses of paragraph 3 are directly applied in the decisions on this page, while inundation, poison, shipwreck, stranding of a vessel, railroad derailment or assault, fall of an airship and other means involving great waste and ruin appear only in statutory quotations in [*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643)[^92] and [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783).[^93]

- **By means of fire:** When the offender burns a structure or person with the main objective of killing the victim, the resulting death is murder qualified by fire under paragraph 3.[^94] In [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783) (citing *People v. Gaffud, Jr.*), beating two women with a baseball bat, pouring gasoline on their bodies and setting them and their house ablaze constituted the complex crime of double murder qualified by fire.[^95]
- **By means of explosion:** In [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021), lobbing an MK2 hand grenade onto a terrace roof where a group was drinking, killing one person and wounding four others with shrapnel, constituted murder qualified by explosion under Article 248, paragraph 3.[^96] When a killing is attended by both treachery and explosion, explosion serves as the qualifying circumstance as the principal mode of attack while treachery becomes a generic aggravating circumstance.[^97] Furthermore, Republic Act No. 8294 (amending Presidential Decree No. 1866 to make the use of unlawfully possessed explosives in a crime an aggravating circumstance rather than a separate offense) did not amend Article 248, paragraph 3 and applies only when unlicensed possession is alleged and proven.[^98]
- **By means of a motor vehicle:** In [*People v. Mallari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48136), after failing to catch a fleeing victim on foot with bladed weapons, the appellant boarded a parked Isuzu Canter Elf truck, pursued the victim and deliberately ran over him, crushing his head.[^99] Distinguishing *People v. Muñoz* (where a patrol jeep was used merely to look for the victim and dump his body after he was shot), [*People v. Mallari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48136) held that using the motor vehicle itself to kill the victim qualifies the killing to murder under Article 248.[^100]

### Paragraph 4: On Occasion of Public Calamities

Article 248, paragraph 4 qualifies a killing committed on occasion of any of the calamities in paragraph 3 or of an earthquake, volcanic eruption, destructive cyclone, epidemic or other public calamity.[^101] None of the decisions on this page applies paragraph 4 to an actual killing. The paragraph appears only where decisions quote Article 248 in full, as in [*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643)[^102] and [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783).[^103]

### Paragraph 5: Evident Premeditation

As stated in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^104], [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)[^105], [*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833)[^106] and [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532)[^107], evident premeditation requires that execution of the criminal act be preceded by cool thought and reflection upon the resolution to carry out the criminal intent during a space of time sufficient to arrive at a calm judgment. To appreciate evident premeditation, the prosecution must prove three concurring elements beyond reasonable doubt:

1. the time when the accused determined to commit the crime;

2. an overt act manifestly indicating that the accused clung to his or her determination to commit the crime; and

3. a sufficient lapse of time between the determination and the execution to allow the accused to reflect upon the consequences of his or her act.[^108]

Evident premeditation qualified the killing to murder in [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584), where coworkers testified that on the day before the stabbing and again on the day of the crime, the driver confided his plan to stab his employer deeply in the back and flee to his province while warning an associate to stay away.[^109] It was also appreciated in [*People v. Moises*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/aug1975/gr_32495_1975.html), where three brothers ambushed a school janitor whom one of the brothers had previously charged with frustrated murder.[^110]

Evident premeditation failed for lack of cool reflection time in [*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833), where a 30-minute interval between a store commotion and the fatal stabbing was too brief for conscience to overcome resolution.[^111] It likewise failed in [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532), where the assailants fetched carbines immediately after a heated argument and mounted the ambush before initial anger could subside.[^112] Where the record shows no external acts establishing when and how the offender planned the attack, evident premeditation cannot be inferred, as held in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678).[^113]

### Paragraph 6: Cruelty and Outraging or Scoffing at the Person or Corpse

Article 248, paragraph 6 contains two distinct clauses: cruelty by deliberately and inhumanly augmenting the suffering of the living victim and outraging or scoffing at the victim's person or corpse.[^114] While the trial court in [*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339) invoked cruelty from 21 stab wounds, the Supreme Court disallowed it because cruelty was omitted from the Information.[^115]

By contrast, the second clause of paragraph 6 applies to post-mortem or immobilizing acts of mockery and mutilation:

- **Qualifying circumstance (dismemberment):** In [*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759), after striking his employer on the head with coconut lumber in an unwitnessed factory altercation, the appellant used a carpenter's saw to cut the corpse into seven pieces (severing the head, limbs and torso) and placed them in boxes and a sack.[^116] Because the Information alleged that he cut the victim into pieces using a saw, the dismemberment qualified the killing to murder under Article 248, paragraph 6.[^117]
- **Generic aggravating circumstance (hog-tying an immobilized body):** In [*People v. Regalario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47107), where abuse of superior strength under paragraph 1 already qualified the fatal beating to murder, two *barangay* officials obeyed the *barangay* chairman's order to tie the neck, hands and feet of the immobile victim with a nylon carabao rope.[^118] The Information alleged cruelty and the tying of the victim's hands and feet with a rope. The Supreme Court affirmed scoffing at the corpse as a generic aggravating circumstance because the appellants tied the victim hog-style after rendering him immobile.[^119]

## Stages of Execution, Multiple Counts and Boundary With Sibling Offenses

### Consummated, Frustrated and Attempted Murder

Under [Article 6](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-006.md) of the Revised Penal Code, when an attack attended by an Article 248 qualifying circumstance and intent to kill does not cause death, the stage of execution turns on the character of the wound.[^120] As synthesized in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^121], [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656)[^122], [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198)[^123] and [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)[^124]:

- **Frustrated stage:** The offender performs all acts of execution by inflicting a mortal wound that would have caused death were it not for timely medical intervention independent of the offender's will.[^125] Normally, medical certificates corroborated by the attending physicians' testimony prove the fatal character of the wounds ([*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)).[^126] However, [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656) held that frustrated murder was proven without physician testimony where the bound victim sustained four abdominal stab wounds, suffered damaged intestines and massive blood loss, underwent surgery during a two-month hospitalization and showed his scars in court.[^127]
- **Attempted stage:** The offender commences murder directly by overt acts with intent to kill (such as firing a gun at victims and missing in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^128] and [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)[^129]) or inflicts only non-mortal wounds. When the prosecution presents a medical certificate without physician testimony and the remaining evidence leaves the fatal nature of the wound doubtful, courts convict only of the attempted felony, as applied to gunshot wounds in [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198)[^130] and [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)[^131] and to a bolo wound in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678).[^132]

### Single Impulse Complex Crimes Versus Separate Counts

When multiple deaths or injuries arise from one incident, liability under [Article 48](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-048.md) of the Revised Penal Code (compound crime, where a single act constitutes two or more grave or less grave felonies) depends on whether the harm flowed from one physical act or distinct acts:

| Mode of Execution | Legal Classification | Penalty Rule | Controlling Decisions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Single physical act killing or injuring multiple victims (for example setting one house fire or throwing one hand grenade) | Complex (compound) crime of Double Murder or Murder with Multiple Attempted Murder under Article 48 in relation to Article 248 | Single penalty for the most serious crime imposed in its maximum period (death, reduced by R.A. No. 9346 to one term of *reclusion perpetua* without eligibility for parole), with separate civil damages per victim | [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783)[^133], [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021)[^134] |
| Successive gunshots or separate blows by one or more assailants killing or injuring multiple victims | Separate counts of Murder, Frustrated Murder or Attempted Murder (not Double Murder or a complex crime) | Separate penalty imposed for each count proven (if joined in one duplicitous Information without a pre-plea motion to quash under Rule 117, Sec. 9, the accused may be convicted of all counts charged and proved) | [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^135], [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532)[^136] |

In [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867), the Supreme Court corrected the trial court's nomenclature of "Double Murder" and "Multiple Attempted Murder" where three armed men stripped a nipa hut wall and fired successive shots inside, holding that each pull of the trigger was a distinct act producing two counts of murder and four counts of attempted murder.[^137] Because the appellant did not move to quash the duplicitous Informations under Section 13, Rule 110 before arraignment, he waived the objection under Section 9, Rule 117 and was sentenced for every count charged and proved.[^138] The same rule led the Supreme Court in [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532) to modify a single death sentence for "Multiple Murder with Double Frustrated Murder" into four *reclusion perpetua* sentences for four counts of murder and two indeterminate sentences for two counts of frustrated murder.[^139] Conversely, in [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783) (single act of burning a house killing two occupants)[^140] and [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021) (single act of detonating one hand grenade killing one victim and injuring four others)[^141], Article 48 mandated a single complex-crime penalty.

### Boundary With Homicide, Parricide, Infanticide, Kidnapping and Special Complex Crimes

Article 248 operates within clear statutory boundaries alongside neighboring offenses in the Revised Penal Code:

- **Parricide ([Article 246](art-246.md)) and Infanticide ([Article 255](art-255.md)):** Under the opening clause of Article 248 ("not falling within the provisions of Article 246") and its elements in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^142], [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^143] and [*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643)[^144], killing a person within the relationships of Article 246 or killing a child less than three days old under Article 255 falls outside Article 248 even if attended by treachery or evident premeditation. Where the deceased child is one year old ([*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678))[^145] or three and a half years old ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867))[^146] and outside Article 246, the crime is murder under Article 248.
- **Homicide ([Article 249](art-249.md)):** When no qualifying circumstance under Article 248 is properly pleaded (or unchallenged under [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)) and proven beyond reasonable doubt, an unlawful killing outside parricide and infanticide defaults to homicide under Article 249, as held in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^147], [*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712)[^148], [*People v. Abrazaldo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47695)[^149], [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059)[^150] and [*People v. Gerolaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34166).[^151]
- **Kidnapping and Serious Illegal Detention ([Articles 267](../book-2-title-09-crimes-against-personal-liberty-and-secu/art-267.md) and [268](../book-2-title-09-crimes-against-personal-liberty-and-secu/art-268.md)):** In [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059), armed kinsmen barged into the victim's house, hog-tied and gagged him, marched him away while lookouts guarded his wife and son overnight and shot and stabbed him to death.[^152] Resolving whether the Information charged kidnapping or murder, the Supreme Court held that when the offender's primary and ultimate specific intent is to kill the victim, incidental detention or abduction is absorbed into the killing as murder or homicide rather than kidnapping.[^153]
- **Special Complex (Composite) Crimes (such as Robbery with Homicide under [Article 294](../book-2-title-10-crimes-against-property/art-294.md) and Rape with Homicide under [Article 266-B](art-266b.md)):** In [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) (quoting *People v. De Leon* and *People v. Macabales*), the Supreme Court explained that in special complex crimes like robbery with homicide or rape with homicide, the word "homicide" is used in its generic sense to absorb murder, parricide and infanticide committed by reason or on the occasion of the robbery or rape.[^154] There is no special complex crime of robbery with murder.[^155] Treachery in such prosecutions serves only as a generic aggravating circumstance.[^156]

## Defenses, Evidentiary Doctrines and Pleas of Guilty to a Capital Offense

### Self-Defense, Insanity, Alibi, Retraction and *Res Gestae*

Litigation under Article 248 frequently turns on affirmative defenses and recurring evidentiary doctrines:

- **Self-defense ([Article 11](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-011.md), paragraph 1):** Invoking self-defense admits the killing and shifts to the accused the burden of proving by clear and convincing evidence unlawful aggression by the victim, reasonable necessity of the means employed and lack of sufficient provocation, relying on defense evidence rather than prosecution weakness ([*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)[^157], [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)[^158], [*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712)[^159] and [*People v. Gerolaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34166)[^160]). Unlawful aggression is indispensable, so once any initial aggression by the victim ceases (such as when the accused wrested the gun from the victim in [*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712)), the accused has no right to shoot the disarmed victim four times.[^161] Likewise, inflicting 18 chest stab wounds on a fleeing woman ([*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584))[^162] or seven wounds while emerging unscathed ([*People v. Gerolaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34166))[^163] negates reasonable necessity.
- **Insanity ([Article 12](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-012.md), paragraph 1):** In [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678) (applying the three-way test in *People v. Paña*), insanity requires clear and convincing proof that insanity existed at the time of the crime, was medically proven to be the primary cause of the criminal act and rendered the accused unable to appreciate the nature and quality or wrongfulness of the act.[^164] An uncorroborated claim of memory loss after allegedly being forced to inhale a drug and testing positive for THC-metabolites failed to prove insanity.[^165]
- **Denial, alibi and conspiracy:** Denial and alibi cannot prevail over positive eyewitness identification unless the accused proves both presence elsewhere and physical impossibility of reaching the crime scene, which failed where the claimed location was five minutes away ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867))[^166], 40 meters away ([*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339))[^167] or two kilometers away ([*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059))[^168] or where the appellant's sister contradicted the alibi ([*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198)).[^169] Under [Article 8](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-008.md), conspiracy may be inferred from concerted conduct before, during and after the killing, making a co-assailant who did not inflict the fatal blow ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^170] and [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)[^171]) or an armed lookout guarding the victim's family ([*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059))[^172] liable as a co-principal. Conversely, in [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021), mere presence on the street and simultaneous flight with the grenade thrower without any overt act in furtherance of the attack failed to prove conspiracy and required the acquittal of the two companions.[^173]
- **Witness retraction, *res gestae*, dying declarations and warrantless arrest:** In [*People v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36488), the Supreme Court held that a prosecution eyewitness's later retraction for the defense is viewed with disfavor and does not automatically destroy his earlier positive testimony, while *res gestae* governs admissibility rather than weight.[^174] In [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783), a burned victim's immediate utterances upon rescue and inside an ambulance naming her nephew as the assailant were upheld as dying declarations under Section 37, Rule 130 or part of the *res gestae* under Section 42, Rule 130.[^175] Both [*People v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36488)[^176] and [*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339)[^177] hold that initial witness silence out of fear does not impair credibility. In addition, [*People v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36488) holds that any defect in a warrantless arrest is waived by entering a plea without prior objection.[^178]

### Murder Remains a Capital Offense Under Rule 116, Section 3: [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584)

Notwithstanding the prohibition against imposing the death penalty under Republic Act No. 9346, [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584) (promulgated September 29, 2020) held En Banc that murder under Article 248 remains a capital offense by nature because it is punishable by *reclusion perpetua* to death.[^179] Consequently, whenever an accused pleads guilty to murder, Section 3, Rule 116 of the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure imposes a mandatory three-fold duty on the trial court:

1. conduct a searching inquiry into the voluntariness and full comprehension of the consequences of the plea;

2. require the prosecution to present evidence to prove the guilt of the accused beyond reasonable doubt and the precise degree of culpability; and

3. ask the accused whether he or she desires to present evidence and allow him or her to do so.[^180]

In [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584), Brendo P. Pagal pleaded guilty to murder before the Regional Trial Court of Hilongos, Leyte, Branch 18, which convicted him solely on his plea (imposing *reclusion perpetua*, P50,000.00 indemnification and P50,000.00 moral damages without naming the specific crime in the dispositive portion) after subpoenaed prosecution witnesses failed to appear on four hearing dates.[^181] On appeal, the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CR-HC No. 01521 annulled the conviction for failure to conduct a searching inquiry under Section 3, Rule 116 and remanded the case. Pagal then Pagal filed an ordinary notice of appeal instead of a Rule 45 petition, an error the Supreme Court excused in the interest of substantial justice by treating the appeal as a petition for review on certiorari.[^182]

Reversing the remand order and acquitting Pagal for failure to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt, [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584) adopted prospective guidelines on guilty pleas to capital offenses.[^183] While a conviction resting on an improvident guilty plea is remanded when the prosecution was deprived of an opportunity to present evidence or suffered undue prejudice from the plea, the accused must be **acquitted** when the prosecution was given a reasonable opportunity to present evidence at trial and failed to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt.[^184]

## Penalties, Statutory Evolution and Civil Liabilities

### Statutory Evolution of the Penalty for Murder and Unlicensed Firearms

The penalty for murder under Article 248 has passed through four distinct legal regimes:

1. **Original Revised Penal Code (Act No. 3815):** Under the original codal text, murder was punishable by *reclusion temporal* in its maximum period to death.[^185] In [*People v. Moises*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/aug1975/gr_32495_1975.html) (1975), adult brothers who committed murder with generic aggravating circumstances and no mitigating circumstance were sentenced to death, whereas their 16-year-old brother received a penalty one degree lower under [Article 68](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-068.md), paragraph 2 (*prision mayor* maximum to *reclusion temporal* medium).[^186] Expressly overruling *People v. Colman* (103 Phil. 6, March 26, 1958), [*People v. Moises*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/aug1975/gr_32495_1975.html) held that the Indeterminate Sentence Law (Act No. 4103, as amended by Act No. 4225) applies to a minor convicted of murder whenever the penalty actually imposed on him is not death.[^187]
2. **Constitutional suspension prior to Republic Act No. 7659:** Because the Constitution proscribed the imposition of the capital penalty before Republic Act No. 7659 was enacted, [*People v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36488) held that a murder committed in January 1991 carried *reclusion perpetua* whether or not attended by a generic aggravating circumstance.[^188]
3. **Republic Act No. 7659 (enacted December 13, 1993):** Section 6 of Republic Act No. 7659 amended Article 248 by raising the prescribed penalty for murder to two indivisible penalties: *reclusion perpetua* to death.[^189] Under [Article 63](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-063.md) of the Revised Penal Code, the lesser penalty of *reclusion perpetua* applies when neither mitigating nor generic aggravating circumstances attend the murder ([*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833)[^190], [*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339)[^191], [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532)[^192], [*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759)[^193] and [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)[^194]) or when voluntary surrender mitigates the offense without any generic aggravating circumstance ([*People v. Mallari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48136)).[^195] Flight or escape from detention is not an aggravating circumstance because the enumeration in Article 14 is exclusive ([*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833)).[^196] Prior to Republic Act No. 9346, when an ordinary aggravating circumstance or Article 48 applied, the death penalty was imposed ([*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021)).[^197]
4. **Republic Act No. 9346 (enacted June 24, 2006):** Republic Act No. 9346 prohibited the imposition of the death penalty.[^198] Under Republic Act No. 9346 (enacted on June 24, 2006 as applied in [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)[^199]), courts impose **reclusion perpetua without eligibility for parole** under the Indeterminate Sentence Law whenever the imposable penalty under Article 248 and Article 63(1) or Article 48 would have been death, such as when murder is aggravated by dwelling ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^200], [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656)[^201] and [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)[^202]), scoffing at the corpse ([*People v. Regalario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47107))[^203] or complexed under Article 48 ([*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783)).[^204] Where no ordinary aggravating circumstance or complex crime called for death, the Supreme Court imposed **reclusion perpetua** ([*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)[^205] and [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^206]).

For non-consummated stages of murder after Republic Act No. 7659, [Article 50](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-050.md), [Article 51](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-051.md) and [Article 61](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-061.md), paragraph 2 graduate the penalty downward from *reclusion perpetua* to death:

- **Frustrated murder:** One degree lower is *reclusion temporal*, with the Indeterminate Sentence Law minimum taken from *prision mayor* ([*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656)[^207] and [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532)[^208]).
- **Attempted murder:** Two degrees lower is *prision mayor* (applied in its medium period absent modifying circumstances or in its maximum period when aggravated by dwelling as in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^209]), with the Indeterminate Sentence Law minimum taken from *prision correccional* ([*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198)[^210] and [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)[^211]).

With respect to unlicensed firearms, [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532) held that Republic Act No. 8294 (effective July 6, 1997, amending Presidential Decree No. 1866 to make the use of an unlicensed firearm in murder or homicide an aggravating circumstance rather than a separate crime) applies retroactively under [Article 22](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-022.md) to dismiss a separate illegal possession charge, but cannot be applied retroactively to aggravate a 1995 murder to death without operating as an *ex post facto* law.[^212] Furthermore, unlicensed firearm aggravates murder only when alleged in the Information ([*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059))[^213] and proven beyond reasonable doubt by establishing both the existence of the gun and the lack of a license ([*People v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36488)[^214] and [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198)[^215]). None of the decisions on this page applies Republic Act No. 10591 or Republic Act No. 10951 to Article 248.

### Governing Schedule of Damages Under [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) (2016)

Under [Article 100](../book-1-title-05-civil-liability/art-100.md) of the Revised Penal Code and Articles 2206, 2219, 2224, 2229 and 2230 of the Civil Code, every person criminally liable for murder is also civilly liable.[^216] Prior to 2016, decisions applied varying lower awards.[^217] In 1975, [*People v. Moises*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/aug1975/gr_32495_1975.html) affirmed P12,000.00 death indemnity.[^218] Later decisions awarded P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral damages and P25,000.00 exemplary damages ([*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339))[^219], introduced P25,000.00 temperate damages when no funeral receipts were presented ([*People v. Abrazaldo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47695))[^220], extended P25,000.00 temperate damages when receipted expenses fell below P25,000.00 ([*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833)[^221] and [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198)[^222]), held actual and temperate damages mutually exclusive ([*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833)[^223] and [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)[^224]), raised exemplary damages to PhP 30,000.00 ([*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643))[^225] and awarded P75,000.00 civil indemnity when the imposable penalty would have been death prior to Republic Act No. 9346 ([*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)[^226], [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656)[^227] and [*People v. Regalario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47107)[^228]).

On April 5, 2016, the Supreme Court En Banc promulgated [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) to standardize civil indemnity, moral damages, exemplary damages and temperate damages across crimes resulting in death, superseding those earlier schedules.[^229] Under [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) (applied in [*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712)[^230], [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783)[^231], [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)[^232] and [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^233]), the governing awards for murder and downgraded homicide are:

| Offense & Penalty Tier | Stage of Execution | Civil Indemnity | Moral Damages | Exemplary Damages | Temperate Damages (if no burial/funeral receipts) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Murder** where imposable penalty is Death reduced to *reclusion perpetua* under R.A. No. 9346 (or complexed under Art. 48) | Consummated | P100,000.00 | P100,000.00 | P100,000.00 | P50,000.00 |
| Same tier | Frustrated | P75,000.00 | P75,000.00 | P75,000.00 | Not applicable |
| Same tier | Attempted | P50,000.00 | P50,000.00 | P50,000.00 | Not applicable |
| **Murder** where penalty imposed is *reclusion perpetua* only (no ordinary aggravating circumstance) | Consummated | P75,000.00 | P75,000.00 | P75,000.00 | P50,000.00 |
| Same tier | Frustrated | P50,000.00 | P50,000.00 | P50,000.00 | Not applicable |
| Same tier | Attempted | P25,000.00 | P25,000.00 | P25,000.00 | Not applicable |
| **Homicide** (divisible penalty under Art. 249 when qualifying circumstance fails) | Consummated | P50,000.00 | P50,000.00 | P50,000.00 only if an aggravating circumstance was proven | P50,000.00 |
| **Homicide** | Frustrated | P30,000.00 | P30,000.00 | P30,000.00 only if an aggravating circumstance was proven | P25,000.00 awarded in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^234] |
| **Homicide** | Attempted | P20,000.00 | P20,000.00 | P20,000.00 only if an aggravating circumstance was proven | Not applicable |

[*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) also clarified that in the civil aspect, an aggravating circumstance proven at trial supports exemplary damages even if omitted from the Information and that all monetary awards earn 6% legal interest per annum from finality until fully paid.[^235] Separately, indemnity for **loss of earning capacity** partakes of actual damages and requires documentary proof of income ([*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833)[^236] and [*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759)[^237]), unless the deceased was self-employed or a daily-wage worker earning below the statutory minimum wage ([*People v. Mallari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48136)).[^238] When proven, [*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339) computes lost earning capacity by multiplying net annual income (gross annual income less 50% for living expenses) by life expectancy under $\frac{2}{3} \times (80 - \text{age at death})$.[^239]

## Case Discussions

### [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678): Tender-Age Treachery Versus Public-Place Attacks Across Thirteen Counts

On July 14, 2013 in Padre Burgos, Southern Leyte, Jonie Sabandal Pilen hacked and stabbed 13 neighbors with an 18.5-inch bolo, killing one-year-old Leslie Ann Salem Kaindoy, Princess Aclao Jabonero and Maria R. Felicilda while injuring ten others, then claimed insanity from forced drug inhalation.[^240] The Regional Trial Court of Maasin City, Southern Leyte, Branch 25 convicted him of three murders (*reclusion perpetua*, P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P75,000.00 moral and P30,000.00 exemplary damages each), eight frustrated murders (eight years and one day of *prision mayor* to 14 years, eight months and one day of *reclusion temporal*, P40,000.00 moral and P20,000.00 exemplary damages each) and two attempted murders (two years and four months of *prision correccional* to eight years and one day of *prision mayor*, P40,000.00 moral and P20,000.00 exemplary damages each), which the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CEB CR. HC No. 03122 modified by affirming murder only for Leslie Ann (adding P75,000.00 exemplary, P50,000.00 temperate damages and parole ineligibility) and downgrading 12 counts for defective Informations (awarding P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral and P50,000.00 temperate damages for two homicides, P30,000.00 civil indemnity and P30,000.00 moral damages plus P30,000.00 exemplary and P40,000.00 temperate to Aiza Salem Kaindoy and P3,000.00, P40,000.00 and P20,000.00 temperate damages to Wenefredo Jabonero, Roger Salem and Genara C. Chu for five frustrated homicides and P20,000.00 civil indemnity and P20,000.00 moral damages plus P2,000.00 temperate damages to three victims and P500.00 to Maximo L. Palero for five attempted homicides).[^241] Dismissing Pilen's appeal and affirming with modification, the Supreme Court held that Pilen waived the formal defects under [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742), rejected insanity, affirmed **Murder** under Article 248 for one-year-old Leslie Ann (*reclusion perpetua*, P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P75,000.00 exemplary, P75,000.00 moral and P50,000.00 temperate damages) and downgraded the other 12 counts for unproven treachery and premeditation to **two counts of Homicide** (eight years and one day of *prision mayor* to 14 years, eight months and one day of *reclusion temporal*, P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral and P50,000.00 temperate damages per deceased victim), **seven counts of Frustrated Homicide** (two years, four months and one day of *prision correccional* to eight years and one day of *prision mayor*, P30,000.00 civil indemnity, P30,000.00 moral and P25,000.00 temperate damages per victim) and **three counts of Attempted Homicide** including Maximo L. Palero whose medical certificate lacked physician testimony (four months of *arresto mayor* to four years and two months of *prision correccional*, P20,000.00 civil indemnity and P20,000.00 moral damages per victim), all with 6% annual interest from finality.[^242]

### [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584): Acquittal After Improvident Guilty Plea When Prosecution Squandered Four Trial Dates

Charged with murder under Article 248 for allegedly stabbing Selma Pagal on December 15, 2008, Brendo P. Pagal pleaded guilty at arraignment before the Regional Trial Court of Hilongos, Leyte, Branch 18, which convicted him solely on his plea (*reclusion perpetua*, P50,000.00 indemnification and P50,000.00 moral damages without naming the crime in the dispositive portion) after subpoenaed prosecution witnesses failed to appear on four hearing dates.[^243] After the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CR-HC No. 01521 held that the trial court failed to conduct a searching inquiry, annulled the conviction and remanded the case for compliance with Section 3, Rule 116. The Supreme Court En Banc treated Pagal's notice of appeal as a Rule 45 petition, held that murder remains a capital offense despite Republic Act No. 9346 and **reversed** the remand order, **acquitting** Pagal of **Murder** and ordering his immediate release because the prosecution failed to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt despite reasonable opportunity.[^244]

### [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742): Waiver of Conclusory Treachery Averments and Prospective Pleading Guidelines

Charged with Mark Kenneth Solar (at large) for fatally beating Joseph Capinig y Mato on the head with a baseball bat on March 9, 2008, Rolando Solar y Dumbrique was convicted of murder by the Regional Trial Court of Las Piñas City, Branch 202 in Criminal Case No. 08-0616 (*reclusion perpetua*, P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral and P25,000.00 exemplary damages), which the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CR-HC No. 05757 downgraded to homicide (P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral and P25,000.00 temperate damages) for lack of factual details on treachery in the Information.[^245] Reversing the Court of Appeals while adopting its findings of fact on conspiracy and eyewitness identification, the Supreme Court En Banc convicted Rolando Solar of **Murder** under Article 248 (*reclusion perpetua*, P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P75,000.00 moral, P75,000.00 exemplary and P50,000.00 temperate damages with 6% annual interest from finality) because he waived the formal defect by failing to move to quash or for a bill of particulars before plea and promulgated prospective guidelines requiring Informations to aver ultimate facts for broad qualifying and aggravating circumstances.[^246]

On January 5, 1991 in Alaminos, Pangasinan, after Ming Basila shot Ferdinand Rabadon twice in the back, Noel Navarro shot the pinned, prostrate victim three times while eyewitness Jose Rabago saw the shooting and identified Navarro in court before later testifying for the defense to recant.[^314] The Regional Trial Court of Alaminos, Pangasinan convicted Navarro of murder in Criminal Case No. 2762-A (*reclusion perpetua*, P50,000.00 death indemnity and P50,000.00 actual damages) while treating the Presidential Decree No. 1866 charge in Criminal Case No. 2763-A as a generic aggravating circumstance despite non-presentation of the gun.[^315] Denying the appeal, the Supreme Court upheld Rabago's original eyewitness testimony over his retraction, held that Navarro waived any objection to his warrantless arrest by pleading without prior challenge, rejected the firearm aggravation because the gun's existence was unproven, noted that a January 1991 murder carried *reclusion perpetua* prior to Republic Act No. 7659 and affirmed his **Murder** conviction and P50,000.00 indemnity while deleting the unreceipted P50,000.00 actual damages.[^316] (Temperate damages in lieu of unreceipted burial expenses were later adopted in [*People v. Abrazaldo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47695)[^317] and raised in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867).[^318])

### [*People v. Gerolaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34166): Reward Offered for Lawful Apprehension Not Qualifying Under Paragraph 2

On March 21, 1987 in Aroroy, Masbate, after Remedios Ruado-Sy and her husband offered a P3,000.00 reward to identify and apprehend an extortionist writing as "Commander Helen Lepanto," unarmed former employee Roberto Gerolaga entered Antonio Sy's residence to identify the suspect and inflicted seven wounds on Antonio Sy after Sy lunged at him with a knife.[^319] Relying on circumstantial evidence and Gerolaga's uncounselled confession, the Regional Trial Court of Masbate (Criminal Case No. 5247) convicted Ruado-Sy, Gerolaga and houseboy Efren Ativo of murder (*reclusion perpetua* each and P30,000.00 solidary indemnity).[^320] Partially granting the direct appeal, the Supreme Court held that a reward offered to identify and apprehend a suspect does not establish murder in consideration of a price or reward under Article 248, paragraph 2, declared the uncounselled confession void, **acquitted** Remedios Ruado-Sy and Efren Ativo for lack of conspiracy and convicted Roberto Gerolaga individually of **Homicide** (ten years of *prision mayor* to 17 years and four months of *reclusion temporal* and P50,000.00 indemnity to Antonio Sy's heirs).[^321] (Homicide moral and temperate damages were later standardized in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867).)[^322]

### [*People v. Moises*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/aug1975/gr_32495_1975.html): Indeterminate Sentence Law Extended to Minors Convicted of Murder

On July 19, 1969 in San Manuel, Pangasinan, brothers Florentino, Eusebio (age 19) and Baltazar (age 16) Moises y Sanidad ambushed school janitor Jose Soloria (who earned P2,268.00 annually) with a .45 caliber pistol, a bolo and a stone, after which Soloria's widow spent about P2,700.00 overall including P1,600.00 in receipts submitted to the Workmen's Compensation Commission.[^323] Under the original codal penalty of *reclusion temporal* maximum to death, the Court of First Instance of Pangasinan, Branch V at Urdaneta (Criminal Case No. U-1426) convicted all three of murder attended by evident premeditation and abuse of superior strength, sentencing Florentino and Eusebio to death and minor Baltazar under Article 68(2) to ten years of *prision mayor* to 14 years, ten months and 21 days of *reclusion temporal*, plus joint and several payment of P12,000.00 indemnity, P1,600.00 actual, P15,000.00 moral and P10,000.00 exemplary damages.[^324] On review, the Supreme Court En Banc affirmed the death sentences of Florentino and Eusebio and the civil awards and overruled *People v. Colman* to hold that the Indeterminate Sentence Law applies to a minor whose actual sentence for **Murder** is below death, modifying Baltazar's indeterminate penalty to ten years of *prision mayor* to 17 years and four months of *reclusion temporal*.[^325] (Death imposition was later prohibited by Republic Act No. 9346[^326] and civil damages were raised in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867).[^327])

## Related Provisions

- **[Article 6 (Consummated, Frustrated and Attempted Felonies)](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-006.md):** Governs the distinction between frustrated murder (mortal wound prevented from causing death by timely medical intervention) and attempted murder (overt acts with intent to kill without a proven mortal wound), as applied in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^328], [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^329] and [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656).[^330]
- **[Article 8 (Conspiracy and Proposal)](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-008.md):** Makes co-conspirators sharing a common criminal design equally liable as principals for murder or homicide regardless of who delivered the fatal blow, as applied in [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)[^331], [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^332] and [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059)[^333], whereas mere presence and flight do not establish conspiracy under [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021).[^334]
- **[Article 11 (Justifying Circumstances: Self-Defense)](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-011.md) and [Article 12 (Exempting Circumstances: Insanity)](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-012.md):** Shift the burden of clear and convincing proof to an accused who invokes them. Self-defense failed for ceased or unproven unlawful aggression or excessive wounds in [*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712)[^335], [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)[^336] and [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)[^337], while insanity failed the three-way medical test in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678).[^338]
- **[Article 14 (Aggravating Circumstances)](../book-1-title-01-felonies-and-circumstances-which-affect/art-014.md):** Supplies the statutory definitions of treachery, evident premeditation, superior strength, dwelling and nighttime applied alongside Article 248 and sets an exclusive list that omits escape from detention under [*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833).[^339]
- **[Article 22 (Retroactive Effect of Penal Laws)](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-022.md):** Applied in [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532) to give retroactive effect to Republic Act No. 8294 insofar as it dismissed a separate illegal possession of firearms charge while refusing retroactive application to aggravate a prior murder to death.[^340]
- **[Article 48 (Penalty for Complex Crimes)](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-048.md):** Imposes a single maximum penalty when one physical act (such as burning a house in [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783)[^341] or throwing one grenade in [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021)[^342]) kills or injures multiple victims, unlike separate gunshots generating distinct counts in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^343] and [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532).[^344]
- **[Articles 50, 51, 61, 63 and 68 (Graduation and Application of Penalties)](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-063.md):** Govern the application of the two indivisible penalties of *reclusion perpetua* to death under Article 63 ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^345] and [*People v. Mallari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48136)[^346]), the one-degree and two-degree reductions for frustrated and attempted murder under Articles 50, 51 and 61(2) ([*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656)[^347] and [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198)[^348]) and the privileged mitigating circumstance of minority under Article 68(2) ([*People v. Moises*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/aug1975/gr_32495_1975.html)).[^349]
- **[Article 100 (Civil Liability of Person Guilty of Felony)](../book-1-title-05-civil-liability/art-100.md):** Anchors the mandatory civil indemnity, moral damages, exemplary damages and temperate damages schedule established for murder in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867).[^350]
- **[Article 246 (Parricide)](art-246.md), [Article 249 (Homicide)](art-249.md) and [Article 255 (Infanticide)](art-255.md):** Define the sibling unlawful-killing offenses excluded from or residual to Article 248, as delineated in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)[^351], [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^352] and [*People v. Abrazaldo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47695).[^353]
- **[Articles 267 and 268 (Kidnapping and Serious or Slight Illegal Detention)](../book-2-title-09-crimes-against-personal-liberty-and-secu/art-267.md):** Distinguished from murder in [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059), which holds that incidental restraint or abduction executed for the primary specific purpose of killing the victim is absorbed into murder or homicide.[^354]
- **[Article 266-B (Penalties for Rape / Rape with Homicide)](art-266b.md) and [Article 294 (Robbery with Violence Against or Intimidation of Persons / Robbery with Homicide)](../book-2-title-10-crimes-against-property/art-294.md):** Discussed in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) as special complex (composite) crimes in which "homicide" is understood in its generic sense to absorb murder committed by reason or on the occasion of the rape or robbery, treating treachery only as a generic aggravating circumstance.[^355]
- **[Republic Act No. 7659, Section 6](../../imposing-the-death-penalty-on-heinous-crimes-ra-7659/sec-06.md) and [Republic Act No. 9346, Sections 1 and 2](../../prohibiting-the-imposition-of-the-death-penalty-ra-9346/sec-01.md):** Republic Act No. 7659 raised the murder penalty to *reclusion perpetua* to death, while Republic Act No. 9346 prohibited imposing the death penalty and substituted *reclusion perpetua* without eligibility for parole when death would otherwise be imposable ([*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)[^356] and [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^357]), leaving murder a capital offense for purposes of Rule 116, Section 3 under [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584).[^358]

## Key Cases

- [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678) (G.R. No. 254875, February 13, 2023): Where an appellant hacked 13 neighbors with a bolo and invoked drug-induced insanity, the Regional Trial Court convicted him of three murders, eight frustrated murders and two attempted murders while the Court of Appeals downgraded 12 counts for defective Informations.[^359] Dismissing the appeal with modification, the Supreme Court held that he waived the pleading defects under [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)[^360], affirmed murder (*reclusion perpetua*, P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P75,000.00 moral, P75,000.00 exemplary and P50,000.00 temperate damages) only for a one-year-old baby due to tender-age treachery and downgraded the remaining public-place attacks to two homicides, seven frustrated homicides and three attempted homicides.[^361]
- [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584) (G.R. No. 241257, September 29, 2020): Where the accused pleaded guilty to murder and the Regional Trial Court convicted him (*reclusion perpetua*, P50,000.00 indemnification and P50,000.00 moral damages) on his plea alone after the prosecution presented no evidence across four hearing dates and the Court of Appeals remanded the case for lack of a searching inquiry, the Supreme Court En Banc treated his notice of appeal as a Rule 45 petition, held that murder remains a capital offense under Rule 116, Section 3 despite Republic Act No. 9346 and reversed the remand to acquit him for failure to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt.[^362]
- [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) (G.R. No. 225595, August 6, 2019): Where the Regional Trial Court convicted a co-conspirator in a fatal baseball-bat attack of murder and the Court of Appeals downgraded the offense to homicide because the Information alleged treachery without factual particulars, the Supreme Court En Banc reversed the Court of Appeals, reinstated murder (*reclusion perpetua*, P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P75,000.00 moral, P75,000.00 exemplary and P50,000.00 temperate damages) because he waived the defect by failing to move to quash or for a bill of particulars before plea and issued prospective guidelines requiring ultimate factual averments for broad qualifying and aggravating circumstances.[^363]
- [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783) (G.R. No. 218702, October 17, 2018): Where a nephew beat his aunt and her partner with a baseball bat, poured gasoline on them and set them and their house ablaze and the Regional Trial Court imposed one *reclusion perpetua* term for double murder while the Court of Appeals imposed two *reclusion perpetua* terms, the Supreme Court held that alleging fatal third-degree burns pleaded murder by fire under Article 248(3) and modified the sentence under Article 48 and Republic Act No. 9346 to one term of *reclusion perpetua* without eligibility for parole for the complex crime of double murder plus P100,000.00 civil indemnity, P100,000.00 moral, P100,000.00 exemplary and P50,000.00 temperate damages per deceased victim.[^364]
- [*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712) (G.R. No. 215748, November 20, 2017): Where an appellant claimed he wrested a gun from a holdupper and shot him outside a godmother's house and both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals convicted him of murder, the Supreme Court rejected self-defense because unlawful aggression ceased upon disarming the victim, ruled out treachery because the lone eyewitness missed the start of the chance encounter where the victim called for help and downgraded the conviction to homicide under Article 249 (eight years and one day of *prision mayor* to 14 years, eight months and one day of *reclusion temporal*, plus P50,000.00 each as civil indemnity, moral and temperate damages).[^365]
- [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) (G.R. No. 202124, April 5, 2016): Where armed men stripped a nipa hut wall and fired successive shots inside (killing two minor sisters and missing four family members) after which the lower courts affirmed convictions for Double Murder and Multiple Attempted Murder, the Supreme Court En Banc held that successive shots generate separate counts (two murders aggravated by dwelling carrying two terms of *reclusion perpetua* without eligibility for parole and four attempted murders carrying four years, two months and one day of *prision correccional* to ten years and one day of *prision mayor* each) and promulgated the P100,000.00, P75,000.00 and P50,000.00 damages schedule for crimes resulting in death.[^366]
- [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584) (G.R. No. 171271, August 31, 2006): Where a family driver who pre-announced his lethal plan fatally stabbed his employer 18 times inside her home and both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals imposed death (with the Court of Appeals disallowing post-plea amendments alleging dwelling), the Supreme Court En Banc ruled out treachery for unwitnessed inception, upheld murder qualified by evident premeditation, validated the post-plea addition of dwelling as a formal amendment under Rule 110, Section 14 and reduced the sentence under Republic Act No. 9346 (enacted June 24, 2006) to *reclusion perpetua* without possibility of parole (plus P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P298,210.25 actual, P50,000.00 moral and P25,000.00 exemplary damages).[^367]
- [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021) (G.R. No. 153559, June 8, 2004): Where one appellant lobbed a hand grenade onto a terrace roof while two relatives stood nearby and fled with him (killing one victim and wounding four) after which the Regional Trial Court sentenced all three to death for a complex crime, the Supreme Court En Banc acquitted the two bystanders for lack of conspiracy, held that explosion under Article 248(3) (untouched by Republic Act No. 8294 absent pleading and proof of unlicensed possession) qualified the murder with treachery as a generic aggravating circumstance and affirmed the death penalty (decided in 2004) against the thrower for murder with multiple attempted murder under Article 48.[^368]
- [*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759) (G.R. No. 147786, January 20, 2004): Where an employee fatally beat his employer in an unwitnessed factory altercation and sawed the corpse into seven pieces and the Regional Trial Court imposed death for murder, the Supreme Court En Banc rejected treachery due to unwitnessed commencement and a defense wound, held that dismembering the corpse qualified the killing to murder by outraging or scoffing at the corpse under Article 248(6) as alleged in the Information and reduced the penalty to *reclusion perpetua* (plus P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P38,068.00 actual, P50,000.00 moral, P25,000.00 exemplary and P25,000.00 attorney's fees).[^369]
- [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059) (G.R. No. 142773, January 28, 2003): Where armed kinsmen hog-tied and abducted the victim from his home while lookouts guarded his family and later shot and stabbed him unseen and the Regional Trial Court imposed death for aggravated murder, the Supreme Court En Banc held that incidental abduction for the primary specific intent to kill charges murder rather than kidnapping, bound the lookout as a co-principal in conspiracy and downgraded the offense to homicide under Article 249 (ten years and one day of *prision mayor* to 14 years, eight months and one day of *reclusion temporal*, plus P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral and P25,000.00 exemplary damages) because no witness saw the killing to prove treachery or superior strength.[^370]
- [*People v. Gerolaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34166) (G.R. No. 89075, October 15, 1996): Where spouses offered P3,000.00 to identify and apprehend an extortionist and an unarmed helper inflicted seven wounds on the suspect after the suspect lunged with a dagger, leading the Regional Trial Court to convict the wife, helper and houseboy of murder, the Supreme Court acquitted the wife and houseboy and downgraded the helper's liability to homicide (ten years of *prision mayor* to 17 years and four months of *reclusion temporal* and P50,000.00 indemnity) because a reward offered for lawful apprehension does not qualify a killing under Article 248(2) and an uncounselled confession is void.[^371]

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1:** An Information charged X and Y with Murder under Article 248 of the Revised Penal Code, alleging only that on May 10, 2021, X and Y, conspiring together and "with intent to kill and with treachery," beat Z on the head with a baseball bat, causing his death. No probable-cause resolution was referenced for the details of treachery. X pleaded not guilty at arraignment without filing a motion to quash or a motion for a bill of particulars. At trial, an eyewitness testified that Y struck the unsuspecting Z on the nape from behind with a baseball bat while X stood in front and mauled Z after he fell, after which both fled together. On appeal from his murder conviction, X argues that (a) he cannot be convicted as a principal because Y delivered the fatal blow and (b) the crime must be downgraded to homicide under [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) because the Information merely stated the legal conclusion "with treachery" without averring its ultimate facts. Rule on both arguments and state the proper crime, penalty and damages.

**Answer:** Both arguments of X are untenable. First, in [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)[^372], the Supreme Court adopted the Court of Appeals' finding that conspiracy may be deduced from the collective conduct of the assailants before, during and after the crime (being together at the scene, mauling the victim after the companion struck him on the nape with a baseball bat and fleeing together), making every conspirator liable as a co-principal regardless of who delivered the fatal blow. Second, while [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)[^373] requires an Information alleging treachery to state the ultimate facts describing how the attack ensured execution without risk to the offender, an accused who fails to challenge that formal defect before arraignment through a motion to quash under Section 3(e), Rule 117 or a motion for a bill of particulars waives the defect, allowing treachery to qualify the killing if proven at trial. Because X pleaded without availing of either remedy and the surprise blow to Z's nape established treachery, X is guilty of Murder under Article 248, punishable (absent any ordinary aggravating circumstance) by *reclusion perpetua*. He is liable to pay Z's heirs P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P75,000.00 moral damages, P75,000.00 exemplary damages and (if no funeral receipts were presented) P50,000.00 temperate damages, all earning 6% interest per annum from finality until fully paid under [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^374] and [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742).[^375]

**Question 2:** Charged with Murder under Article 248, B pleaded guilty at arraignment. Without conducting a recorded searching inquiry into B's comprehension of the plea, the Regional Trial Court scheduled four separate trial dates for the prosecution to present its witnesses under Section 3, Rule 116 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure. Despite duly served subpoenas, no prosecution witness appeared on any of the four dates. Both parties then submitted the case for decision without presenting evidence. The trial court convicted B solely on his guilty plea and sentenced him to *reclusion perpetua*. On appeal, the Solicitor General prays that the case be remanded to the trial court for re-arraignment and reception of prosecution evidence, arguing that murder is no longer a capital offense after Republic Act No. 9346 and that an improvident plea warrants remand. Resolve the appeal.

**Answer:** B must be acquitted and ordered immediately released unless detained for another lawful cause. In [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584)[^376], the Supreme Court En Banc held that murder under Article 248 remains a capital offense by nature because its prescribed penalty is *reclusion perpetua* to death, notwithstanding the prohibition against imposing the death penalty under Republic Act No. 9346. Under Section 3, Rule 116 of the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure, a trial court cannot convict an accused of a capital offense solely on a plea of guilty and must conduct a searching inquiry, require the prosecution to prove guilt and the precise degree of culpability beyond reasonable doubt and allow the defense to present evidence.[^377] Under the guidelines in [*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584)[^378], remand is proper only when the prosecution was deprived of an opportunity to present its evidence or when the improvident plea caused undue prejudice, whereas when the prosecution was given reasonable opportunity across four hearing dates and failed to present any evidence proving guilt beyond reasonable doubt, the presumption of innocence mandates acquittal.

**Question 3:** Contrast the proper crime designation and penalty (assuming all facts are properly alleged in the Informations or any formal defect was waived) in two separate prosecutions. In the first, M beat his aunt and her partner inside their bedroom, poured gasoline on both of them and lit a single fire that burned the house and caused third-degree burns resulting in both victims' deaths. In the second, J and two armed companions tore down the sack wall of a family's nipa hut at night without provocation and fired successive gunshots from their respective firearms into the hut, killing a 13-year-old girl and a three-and-a-half-year-old child and missing four other occupants.

**Answer:** The two cases differ fundamentally under [Article 48](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-048.md) and Article 248. In the first case, as held in [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783)[^379], setting a single fire with the main objective of killing two occupants constitutes one physical act producing two grave felonies qualified by fire under Article 248, paragraph 3, making M guilty of the complex (compound) crime of Double Murder under Article 48, for which the single maximum penalty of death is reduced by Republic Act No. 9346 to one term of *reclusion perpetua* without eligibility for parole (with P100,000.00 civil indemnity, P100,000.00 moral, P100,000.00 exemplary and P50,000.00 temperate damages for each deceased victim's heirs). In the second case, as held in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)[^380], firing successive gunshots from separate firearms involves distinct acts rather than a single act under Article 48, while attacking young children is treacherous under Article 248, paragraph 1 and violating the unprovoked victims' home establishes dwelling. J is guilty of two separate counts of Murder (two terms of *reclusion perpetua* without eligibility for parole under Article 63(1) and Republic Act No. 9346, plus P100,000.00 civil indemnity, P100,000.00 moral, P100,000.00 exemplary and P50,000.00 temperate damages per deceased child) and four separate counts of Attempted Murder under Article 248 in relation to Article 51 (an indeterminate term from *prision correccional* as minimum to *prision mayor* maximum for each count, plus P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral and P50,000.00 exemplary damages per surviving victim).[^381]

# Quotes

## Sufficiency of Factual Averments for Qualifying Circumstances in the Information

> "With the foregoing principles in mind, the Court thus agrees with the ruling enunciated in Valdez, as subsequently reiterated in Dasmariñas and Delector. Consequently, the Court holds that it is insufficient for prosecutors to indicate in an Information that the act supposedly committed by the accused was done "with treachery" or "with abuse of superior strength" or "with evident premeditation" without specifically describing the acts done by the accused that made any or all of such circumstances present. Borrowing the words of the Court in Dasmariñas, "to merely state in the information that treachery was attendant is not enough because the usage of such term is not a factual averment but a conclusion of law.""[^382]

> "An information alleging that treachery exists, to be sufficient, must therefore have factual averments on how the person charged had deliberately employed means, methods or forms in the execution of the act that tended directly and specially to insure its execution without risk to the accused arising from the defense that the victim might make. The Information must so state such means, methods or forms in a manner that would enable a person of common understanding to know what offense was intended to be charged."[^383]

## Definition of Murder and Imposable Penalty Basis for Damages

> "Murder is defined under Article 248 of the Revised Penal Code as the unlawful killing of a person, which is not parricide or infanticide, attended by circumstances such as treachery or evident premeditation. The presence of any one of the circumstances enumerated in Article 248 of the Code is sufficient to qualify a killing as murder. The trial court correctly ruled that appellant is liable for murder because treachery attended the killing of Norberto's two children, thus:"[^384]

> "Essentially, despite the fact that the death penalty cannot be imposed because of RA 9346, the imposable penalty as provided by the law for the crime, such as those found in RA 7569, must be used as the basis for awarding damages and not the actual penalty imposed."[^385]

## Acquittal When Prosecution Fails to Present Evidence After an Improvident Plea of Guilty

> "Therefore, in instances where an improvident plea of guilt has been entered and the prosecution was given reasonable opportunity to present evidence to establish the guilt of the accused but failed to do so, the accused is entitled to an acquittal, if only to give rise to the constitutionally guaranteed right to due process and the presumption of innocence."[^386]

## Incidental Deprivation of Liberty Merged Into Murder or Homicide

> "If the primary and ultimate purpose of the accused is to kill the victim, the incidental deprivation of the victim’s liberty does not constitute the felony of kidnapping but is merely a preparatory act to the killing, and hence, is merged into, or absorbed by, the killing of the victim. The crime committed would either be homicide or murder."[^387]

## Murder by Means of Explosion Distinguished From R.A. No. 8294

> "It must be made clear, however, that RA No. 8294 did not amend the definition of murder under Article 248, but merely made the use of explosives an aggravating circumstance when resorted to in committing “any of the crimes defined in the Revised Penal Code.” The legislative purpose is to do away with the use of explosives as a separate crime and to make such use merely an aggravating circumstance in the commission of any crime already defined in the Revised Penal Code. Thus, RA No. 8294 merely added the use of unlicensed explosives as one of the aggravating circumstances specified in Article 14 of the Revised Penal Code. Like the aggravating circumstance of “explosion” in paragraph 12, “evident premeditation” in paragraph 13, or “treachery” in paragraph 16 of Article 14, the new aggravating circumstance added by RA No. 8294 does not change the definition of murder in Article 248."[^388]

## Dismemberment as Outraging or Scoffing at the Corpse Under Paragraph 6

> "One attendant circumstance, however, is amply proved by the prosecution’s evidence which shows that the victim’s corpse was sawn by appellant into seven (7) pieces. Under Art. 248 (6) of the Revised Penal Code, “outraging or scoffing at the corpse” is a qualifying circumstance. Dismemberment of a dead body is one manner of outraging or scoffing at the corpse of the victim. In the instant case, the corpse of Victor F. Keyser was dismembered by appellant who sawed off the head, limbs, and torso. The Information categorically alleges this qualifying circumstance, when it stated that the appellant “thereafter, cut into pieces using said saw one Victor F. Keyser.” This being the case, as proved by the prosecution, appellant is guilty not just of homicide but of murder."[^389]

## Indeterminate Sentence Law Applicable to Minors Sentenced Below Death

> "In imposing an indeterminate sentence upon Baltazar Moises y Sanidad, We hereby overrule the contrary doctrine in People vs. Colman, et al., 103 Phil. 6, Resolution of March 26, 1958, pp. 19-20, holding that the Indeterminate Sentence Law (Act No. 4103, as amended by Act No. 4225) is not applicable to a case similar to that of accused Baltazar Moises y Sanidad. The penalty actually imposed upon this accused not being death, he is entitled to the benefits of the Indeterminate Sentence Law."[^390]

# Tips

## Diagnostic Checklist for Article 248 Issues

1. **Exclude parricide, infanticide and special complex crimes first:** Check whether the deceased falls within the relationships of [Article 246](art-246.md) or is a child less than three days old under [Article 255](art-255.md) ([*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678) and [*People v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53643)). Also check whether the killing occurred by reason or on the occasion of robbery or rape where generic homicide absorbs murder into a composite crime ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)).
2. **Audit the Information and pre-plea remedies:** Verify whether the Information states the ultimate facts constituting the qualifying and aggravating circumstances (or references the attached probable-cause resolution) and whether the defense filed a motion to quash under Section 3(e), Rule 117 or a motion for a bill of particulars before entering a plea ([*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742)).
3. **Test proof of the qualifying circumstance at the moment of the fatal attack:** Confirm whether an eyewitness saw how the assault began or whether an *ipso facto* rule (such as tender-age minority in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678) and [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)) or a non-treachery qualifier (evident premeditation in [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584), fire in [*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783), explosion in [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021), motor vehicle in [*People v. Mallari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48136) or dismemberment under paragraph 6 in [*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759)) applies.
4. **Separate single-impulse complex crimes from distinct counts:** Check whether multiple casualties resulted from one physical act under [Article 48](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-048.md) ([*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783) and [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021)) or from successive shots and separate blows generating multiple counts ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) and [*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532)).
5. **Match surviving victims' wounds to medical proof and match damages to the imposable penalty:** Verify medical proof of mortal injury before classifying a non-fatal attack as frustrated rather than attempted ([*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678), [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198) and [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)) and select the P100,000.00 or P75,000.00 murder damages tier under [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) according to whether death would have been imposable prior to Republic Act No. 9346.

### Common Litigation and Bar Exam Traps

> [!TIP]
> **Distinguish Complete Omission From a Defective Conclusory Averment Under [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742).** When an Information completely omits an aggravating circumstance (such as dwelling in the frustrated murder charge in [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656), cruelty in [*People v. Delos Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47339), lack of firearm license in [*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059) or positive drug use in [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)), the court cannot appreciate it to increase the criminal penalty even if proven without objection (though [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) still allows proven unpleaded aggravating circumstances to support exemplary damages in the civil aspect). By contrast, when the Information names "with treachery" or "with evident premeditation" without factual details, the accused waives that defect unless he files a motion to quash under Section 3(e), Rule 117 or a motion for a bill of particulars before arraignment ([*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) and [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)). Descriptive factual allegations also suffice without the statutory label when an Information pleads that the victim was a one-year-old minor ([*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)), suffered fatal third-degree burns from lit gasoline ([*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783)) or was cut into pieces with a saw ([*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Never Confuse Simple *Reclusion Perpetua* With *Reclusion Perpetua* Without Eligibility for Parole.** Under [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867), the qualification "without eligibility for parole" under Republic Act No. 9346 and the P100,000.00 damages tier (for civil indemnity, moral damages and exemplary damages) apply **only** when the imposable penalty under Article 248 in relation to [Article 63](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-063.md), paragraph 1 or [Article 48](../book-1-title-03-penalties/art-048.md) would have been death, meaning an ordinary aggravating circumstance (such as dwelling in [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) and [*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584) or scoffing at the corpse in [*People v. Regalario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47107)) or a complex crime ([*People v. Mercado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64783)) is present. When murder is attended only by a qualifying circumstance and no ordinary aggravating circumstance, the sentence is simple *reclusion perpetua* and the awards are P75,000.00 each for civil indemnity, moral damages and exemplary damages ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867), [*People v. Solar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65742) and [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)).

> [!TIP]
> **Check How Multiple Qualifying Circumstances Interact.** Only one circumstance is needed to qualify a killing to murder ([*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867)). Abuse of superior strength is **absorbed** in treachery and cannot serve as a separate generic aggravating circumstance ([*People v. Valdez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37532)). Conversely, independent qualifying circumstances do not absorb each other. In [*People v. Comadre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47021), explosion under paragraph 3 qualified the murder while treachery operated as a generic aggravating circumstance. In [*People v. Regalario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47107), abuse of superior strength under paragraph 1 qualified the murder while hog-tying and scoffing at the corpse under paragraph 6 operated as a generic aggravating circumstance.

### What Worked and What Failed in Decided Cases

- **Unwitnessed commencement defeats treachery even in brutal killings unless another qualifier is proved:** Defense counsel succeeded in downgrading murder convictions to homicide where no witness saw how the fatal assault began, even where the victim had been hog-tied and abducted hours earlier ([*People v. Delim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48059)) or called out for help right before being shot outside his godmother's door ([*People v. Duran*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63712)), as well as where eyewitnesses saw loud shouting and face-to-face grappling ([*People v. Abrazaldo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47695)) or public-place attacks without conscious preparation ([*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678)). By contrast, even when nobody witnessed the start of the killing, murder convictions stood where the prosecution proved pre-crime threats establishing evident premeditation ([*People v. Tubongbanua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43584)) or post-mortem dismemberment establishing scoffing at the corpse ([*People v. Guillermo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46759)).
- **Physician testimony on non-fatal wounds and exclusivity of actual and temperate damages:** Presenting a medical certificate without calling a physician to testify that the wound was mortal reduced frustrated charges to the attempted stage in [*People v. Lacaden*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53198), [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591) and [*People v. Pilen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68678), whereas proving four abdominal stab wounds, damaged intestines, surgical operation and two months' hospitalization sustained frustrated murder without physician testimony in [*People v. Tolentino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44656). On civil damages, courts cannot award actual burial damages and temperate damages together because they are mutually exclusive ([*People v. Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47833) and [*People v. Gutierrez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53591)). Instead, P50,000.00 temperate damages apply under [*People v. Jugueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61867) when no burial or funeral receipts are presented.
- **Guilty pleas and reward offers:** Entering a guilty plea to murder without prosecution evidence resulted in outright acquittal rather than remand when the prosecution squandered four subpoenaed hearing dates ([*People v. Pagal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66584)). Similarly, proving that a P3,000.00 reward was offered to identify and apprehend an extortionist rather than to kill anyone defeated both paragraph 2 qualification and inducement liability ([*People v. Gerolaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34166)).
