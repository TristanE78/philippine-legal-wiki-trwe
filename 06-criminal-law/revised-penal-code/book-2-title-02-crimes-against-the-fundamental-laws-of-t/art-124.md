> Article 124. Arbitrary detention.-Any public officer or employee who, without legal grounds, detains a person, shall suffer:
>
> 1. The penalty of arresto mayor in its maximum period to prision correccional in its minimum period, if the detention has not exceeded three days;
>
> 2. The penalty of prision correccional in its medium and maximum periods, if the detention has continued more than three but not more than fifteen days;
>
> 3. The penalty of prision mayor, if the detention has continued for more than fifteen days but not more than six months; and
>
> 4. That of reclusion temporal, if the detention shall have exceeded six months.
>
> The commission of a crime, or violent insanity or any other ailment requiring the compulsory confinement of the patient in a hospital, shall be considered legal grounds for the detention of any person.

[^revised-penal-code]

# Explanation

## Scope and elements

Article 124 punishes detention without legal grounds by a public officer or employee. In [*Benito Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078), the Supreme Court listed the elements of arbitrary detention:

1. the offender is a public officer or employee;

2. he detains a person; and

3. the detention is without legal grounds.

The Supreme Court affirmed a mayor's conviction after finding that threats and armed men prevented a government team from leaving.[^2]

The official's authority and the actual restriction of liberty require separate inquiry. In [*Ramon S. Milo v. Angelito C. Salanga*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/jul1987/gr_l-37007_1987.html), the Supreme Court explained that the public officer must possess authority to detain or order detention. It rejected the conclusion that a barrio captain fell outside the offense merely because he was not a member of the police force. Its ruling allowed the prosecution to proceed and did not determine guilt.[^3]

The absence of legal grounds must also be established on the facts. In [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291), police officers deprived three persons of liberty on mere surveillance. The Supreme Court found the elements of arbitrary detention present. Suspicion of involvement in illegal drugs did not supply legal grounds for the detentions adjudicated in that case.[^4]

## Official capacity and neighboring offenses

Public employment does not settle every case involving deprivation of liberty. The duties attached to the office and the purpose of the arrest remain material. [*Milo v. Salanga*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/jul1987/gr_l-37007_1987.html) examined a barrio captain's peacekeeping and law-enforcement duties under the Revised Barrio Charter then applicable. The Supreme Court held that these duties included authority to detain within legal limits.[^5]

In [*Pascasio Duropan and Raymond Nixer Coloma v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327), the Supreme Court examined the positions of a barangay kagawad and a barangay tanod. It distinguished their status as a person in authority and an agent of a person in authority from an official duty to arrest or detain. The conviction was for unlawful arrest under Article 269. The accused had brought the complainant to the police station without reasonable ground.[^6]

The following distinctions follow the discussion in [*Duropan v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327). Its references to Articles 124 and 125 appear in a quoted passage. Its actual conviction rests on Article 269.[^7]

| Provision | Distinction discussed in the decision |
|---|---|
| Article 124 | The quoted passage concerns a warrantless arrest without legal ground by an officer whose official duty authorizes arrest. |
| Article 125 | The quoted passage concerns failure to bring the detained person before judicial authorities within the prescribed period. |
| Articles 267 and 268 | The discussion addresses detention by persons without an official duty to arrest and detention undertaken in a private capacity. |
| Article 269 | Arrest or detention without authority or reasonable ground is undertaken for the purpose of delivering the person to the proper authorities. |

The Supreme Court did not treat the description of the office as a substitute for examining the charge and evidence. [*Duropan v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327) emphasized the prosecutor's choice of charge and the additional element of delivery to the proper authorities. The decision also explained that a public officer who lacks legal grounds for a detention may be deemed to have acted in a private capacity. Its own conviction still rested on the charge of unlawful arrest that the prosecutor chose to file.[^8]

## Restraint and proof

### Detention without physical enclosure

A victim need not be locked inside a cell. In [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078), the Supreme Court held that fear can restrict liberty when it compels a person to limit movement according to the accused's wishes. The mayor refused the team's requests to leave. Armed men then encircled the team with weapons pointed at its members. The Supreme Court relied on the effect of those acts on the team's freedom of movement.[^9]

Serving dinner did not establish freedom to leave. The witnesses denied that they had remained to enjoy the place. The Supreme Court rejected the claimed hospitality as an explanation for their continued presence in [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078).[^10]

### Failure to prove detention

Proof of association with an accused does not necessarily prove detention. In [*People of the Philippines v. Aaron Flores*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52183), the Supreme Court considered whether the CAFGU appellants had arbitrarily detained the missing person after rejecting their conviction for kidnapping and serious illegal detention. It found the prosecution's proof of confinement and intent insufficient. The established circumstance that the appellants were last seen with him did not complete the required chain of circumstantial evidence.[^11]

That conclusion concerned failure of proof. [*People of the Philippines v. Aaron Flores*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52183) rested the acquittal on the prosecution's failure to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. The Supreme Court also held that the appellants could not be convicted of kidnapping and serious illegal detention because that crime requires a private individual and the CAFGU members were public officers.[^12] The later ruling in [*Duropan v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327)[^13] should be read alongside it. There the Supreme Court stated that public officers who have no duty to arrest or detain, or who have that authority but fail to justify the arrest or detention, may be indicted for kidnapping or illegal detention.

### Witnesses and desistance

The private complainant need not supply all the incriminating testimony. In [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078), an accompanying police officer witnessed the threats and armed encirclement. The Supreme Court rejected the objection that he was not one of the private complainants. It also found that the joint affidavit of desistance did not clearly repudiate the material allegations proved at trial. No accompanying circumstances created doubt in the accepted testimony.[^14]

The same decision rejected the claim that extensive clarificatory questions established judicial partiality. The Sandiganbayan could ask questions to ascertain the truth. The Supreme Court found no basis to overturn the conviction on that ground in [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078).[^15]

## Legal grounds and distinct criminal acts

The final paragraph of Article 124 identifies commission of a crime, violent insanity and an ailment requiring compulsory hospital confinement as legal grounds for detention. None of the decisions discussed on this page involves a detention on the medical grounds.[^16]

In [*Duropan v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327), the Supreme Court rejected the claimed justification for a warrantless arrest. Harvesting nipa was not itself a crime. The officials knew of the organization authorized to harvest and could have checked the complainant's membership. No criminal overt act within their view justified the arrest. Their assertion of good faith did not overcome the circumstances identified in the decision.[^17]

An unlawful detention followed by a killing does not automatically constitute a complex crime. In [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291), the final Informations charged arbitrary detention with murder. The Supreme Court upheld separate convictions because the acts did not meet Article 48. The detention had already been consummated before the killings. The evidence did not establish that detention was a necessary means to commit murder.[^18]

The Supreme Court also rejected the challenges to the murder findings and the discharge of Brillantes as a state witness in [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291). Direct testimony established the killings of Suganob and Lomoljo. The circumstances concerning Salabas formed the chain of evidence supporting his murder conviction. The required conditions for the discharge of Brillantes were satisfied.[^19]

## Procedure and independent remedies

### Quashal and preliminary investigation

On a motion to quash for failure to charge an offense, a defense cannot ordinarily replace the allegations in the Information. In [*Milo v. Salanga*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/jul1987/gr_l-37007_1987.html), the barrio captain claimed that he merely sought police assistance and accompanied the complainant for safety. Those claims contradicted the Information and belonged at trial. The Supreme Court preserved the exceptions expressly allowed by the rules and the exception for facts admitted by the prosecution. It also rejected double jeopardy because the accused had himself obtained the dismissal.[^20]

In [*Ernesto J. San Agustin v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45685), the NBI arrested a barangay chairman when he appeared in response to a subpoena. The arrest occurred seven days after the alleged detention. The Supreme Court agreed that the arrest was unlawful and held the inquest void. It nevertheless distinguished that defect from the validity of an Information filed without preliminary investigation.[^21]

The absence of preliminary investigation did not itself invalidate the Information, remove trial-court jurisdiction or justify release in [*San Agustin v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45685). The remedy ordered was a preliminary investigation and suspension of the proceedings. Under the rules considered in that decision, entitlement depended on the offense charged in the original complaint. The later filing of an Article 124 Information did not cure the omission. In discussing paragraph (1), the Supreme Court recited and rejected the Court of Appeals' reasoning that the later, less severely punished charge dispensed with the investigation.[^22]

### Complaints against the arresting officers

A challenge to an accused's arrest serves a different purpose from proceedings against the arresting officers. In [*Junel Alaska v. SPO2 Gil M. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67595), the Ombudsman dismissed complaints for arbitrary detention and misconduct because the complainants could question their arrests in the pending criminal case. The Supreme Court rejected that ground. The criminal case against the complainants concerned their own alleged offense. The Ombudsman proceedings concerned the officers' possible criminal and administrative liability.[^23]

The Supreme Court found that the objection to arrest had in fact been raised before arraignment. It further held that even an assumed failure to object would not preclude complaints against the officers. [*Alaska v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67595) explained that the proper course was suspension pending the trial court's resolution of the arrest objection rather than dismissal. It also held that Section 20 of R.A. No. 6770 concerns administrative complaints and does not supply the invoked ground for dismissing a criminal complaint.[^24]

The remedy used to challenge the Ombudsman's rulings was itself defective. [*Alaska v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67595) distinguished Rule 65 review of administrative absolution before the Court of Appeals from Rule 65 review of the criminal disposition before the Supreme Court. It nevertheless relaxed procedure because the Court of Appeals had ruled on the merits through unsupported conclusions and serious indications of unlawful arrest and fabricated evidence remained unaddressed. The Supreme Court expressly declined to adopt the trial court's factual findings on the legality of the arrest. The remand was not a conviction of the officers.[^25]

## Duration and case-specific penalties

The statutory penalties depend on the duration of detention. Act No. 3815 took effect on January 1, 1932. Its Article 124 prescribes the following bands.[^26]

| Duration | Codal penalty |
|---|---|
| Not more than three days | Arresto mayor in its maximum period to prision correccional in its minimum period. |
| More than three days but not more than fifteen days | Prision correccional in its medium and maximum periods. |
| More than fifteen days but not more than six months | Prision mayor. |
| More than six months | Reclusion temporal. |

The application of the first band appears expressly in [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078), decided on October 1, 2003 for the detention on September 1, 1997. The Supreme Court applied Article 124(1) and the Indeterminate Sentence Law. It affirmed four months of arresto mayor as minimum to one year and eight months of prision correccional as maximum.[^27]

The later decision in [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291), promulgated on February 17, 2020, separately addressed the detentions beginning on August 31, 2003. For Suganob and Lomoljo, whose detentions did not exceed three days, it imposed two prison terms of four months to one year and eight months. For Salabas, whose detention did not exceed fifteen days, it imposed two years and four months to four years and nine months. The Supreme Court described these as the terms the Court of Appeals correctly imposed. The RTC had imposed six months to two years and four months for Suganob and Lomoljo and two years and four months to six years for Salabas.[^28]

None of the decisions discussed on this page applies clause (3) or clause (4) to a longer detention.[^29]

### Complete relief in the detention and murder case

In [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291), the Supreme Court affirmed with modifications the convictions of Dongail and Fortaleza for three counts of murder under Article 248 and three counts of arbitrary detention under Article 124. Each murder count carried reclusion perpetua. The three detention terms are stated above. The case against Natividad was dismissed because he had died.[^30]

The same February 17, 2020 decision ordered Dongail and Fortaleza to pay the following amounts jointly and severally. The civil indemnity, moral damages and exemplary damages were increased to PHP100,000 each for each victim. The decision's recital of the RTC awards had listed PHP50,000 as civil indemnity, PHP50,000 as moral damages and PHP30,000 as exemplary damages per victim. The Supreme Court tied the increase to the murder counts and to the latest jurisprudence on those awards.[^31]

| Award | Eleuterio Salabas | Ricardo Suganob | Maximo Lomoljo |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Civil indemnity | PHP100,000.00 | PHP100,000.00 | PHP100,000.00 |
| Moral damages | PHP100,000.00 | PHP100,000.00 | PHP100,000.00 |

| Loss of earning capacity | PHP4,480,080.00 | PHP2,780,512.96 | PHP400,000.00 |
| Actual damages | PHP3,599,031.82 | PHP1,523,010.70 | None specified in the final order. |
| Temperate damages | None specified in the final order. | None specified in the final order. | PHP50,000.00 |

All damages awarded in [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291) earn interest at six percent per annum from finality of that decision until full payment. The Supreme Court awarded temperate damages for Lomoljo because documentary proof of burial or funeral expenses had not been presented.[^32]

## Case Discussions

### Barrio captain and quashal

In [*Ramon S. Milo v. Angelito C. Salanga*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/jul1987/gr_l-37007_1987.html), an Information filed on October 12, 1972 charged barrio captain Juan Tuvera, Sr. and two members of the Manaoag police force with arbitrary detention. It alleged that Tuvera and private persons maltreated Armando Valdez with gun butts and fist blows and then detained him without legal grounds. The Supreme Court read the Information as charging detention for about eleven hours in the municipal jail.[^33] All the accused pleaded not guilty. Tuvera then moved to quash on the ground that the facts charged did not constitute an offense. In an order dated April 25, 1973, Judge Angelito C. Salanga of the Court of First Instance of Pangasinan granted the motion. He found that Tuvera was not a public officer who could be charged with arbitrary detention because he was not connected with the police force.[^34] The Assistant Provincial Fiscal and Valdez sought review. The Supreme Court held that the public officers liable for arbitrary detention must be vested with authority to detain or order detention. It relied on the older convictions of barrio lieutenants in *United States v. Braganza* and *United States v. Gellada* (as cited in [*Milo v. Salanga*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/jul1987/gr_l-37007_1987.html)) and on the barrio captain's duties under the Revised Barrio Charter. It concluded that a barrio captain like Tuvera can be held liable for arbitrary detention.[^35] Tuvera's claims that he only sought police help and accompanied Valdez for his safety were defenses for trial. The quashal also did not place him in double jeopardy because he had secured the dismissal himself. The Supreme Court granted the petition, set aside the order of April 25, 1973 and remanded the case for further proceedings, with no pronouncement as to costs.[^36]

### Unproved detention

In [*People of the Philippines v. Aaron Flores*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52183), Sgt. Wennie Tampioc and three CAFGU members under his supervision were charged with kidnapping and serious illegal detention before the Regional Trial Court of Kabankalan, Negros Occidental, Branch 61. Tampioc was a detachment commander of the 7th Infantry Brigade. The Information alleged that on September 29, 1992 they took and detained Samson Sayam in Ilog, Negros Occidental.[^37] Sayam had joined the accused at a store where they were drinking beer. Witnesses later saw the group walk toward the military detachment. Sayam was not seen or heard from again.[^38] In its December 8, 1993 judgment, the trial court convicted Flores, Silpao and Villeran and sentenced each to reclusion perpetua. Because there was no proof that Sayam was dead, it ordered them to pay him or his heirs P50,000.00 as damages jointly and severally. It acquitted Tampioc on reasonable doubt.[^39] On appeal, the Supreme Court held that the appellants could not be convicted of kidnapping and serious illegal detention because that crime requires a private individual and they were CAFGU members. The Solicitor General conceded the point and argued for a conviction for arbitrary detention instead.[^40] The Supreme Court found the evidence insufficient for that crime as well. Carlito Manlangit did not say that Sayam was pulled, dragged or taken at gunpoint. Jerry Manlangit saw no apprehension or detention. Nelson Golez wavered on whether there had been an argument. The events he described happened in a public place. The gunshots heard that night could not be traced to the detachment.[^41] Only one relevant circumstance was proved, that the appellants were the last persons seen with Sayam. That fact did not prove abduction or detention. The disappearance was not an element of arbitrary detention.[^42] The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the judgment, acquitted the appellants and ordered their immediate release unless held for some lawful reason. It directed the Director of Prisons to report the date and time of release within five days from notice.[^43]

### Restraint through fear

In [*Benito Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078), the Ombudsman charged Benito Astorga, Mayor of Daram, Samar, with arbitrary detention on October 28, 1998. On September 1, 1997, a DENR team sent to Daram for intelligence gathering and forest protection operations stopped to investigate boats being built near Barangay Lucob-Lucob. Two police officers, SPO3 Andres B. Cinco, Jr. and SPO1 Rufo Capoquian, escorted the team.[^44] Astorga slapped team leader Elpidio Simon twice on the shoulder, threatened the team and sent for reinforcements. A banca later arrived with ten armed men who encircled the team. The team was brought to a house for dinner and was allowed to leave only at 2:00 a.m.[^45] Simon's testimony was not completed and the team members executed a Joint Affidavit of Desistance. The prosecution relied on the two police escorts. On July 5, 2001, the Sandiganbayan convicted Astorga and sentenced him to four months of arresto mayor as minimum to one year and eight months of prision correccional as maximum. It later denied two motions for reconsideration.[^46] On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court found the first and third elements present because Astorga was mayor and admitted acting from an instinct for self-preservation. It found the second element proved by the fear produced by his refusal to let the team leave and by the armed encirclement.[^47] It held that the affidavit of desistance did not create doubt, that SPO1 Capoquian's status as a non-complainant was irrelevant and that the Sandiganbayan's clarificatory questions did not show partiality. It applied Article 124(1) because the detention had not exceeded three days. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Sandiganbayan decision in toto, with costs de oficio.[^48]

### Defective inquest

In [*Ernesto J. San Agustin v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45685), Luz Tan filed a complaint with the NBI charging Ernesto J. San Agustin, Barangay Chairman of La Huerta, Parañaque City, with serious illegal detention. The NBI's findings stated that on June 19, 2002 Ricardo Tan was mistaken for a snatcher and turned over to San Agustin at the barangay hall.[^49] San Agustin answered an NBI subpoena on June 26, 2002 and brought the barangay logbook. He was then arrested and prevented from going home. After an inquest on June 27, 2002, an Information for kidnapping and serious illegal detention with no bail recommended was filed with the Regional Trial Court of Parañaque City, Branch 258.[^50] San Agustin moved to quash. On July 24, 2002, the RTC ordered the City Prosecutor to conduct a reinvestigation. The assigned prosecutor later found probable cause for arbitrary detention. The RTC allowed the first Information to be withdrawn. On August 30, 2002, an Information for arbitrary detention was filed with the MeTC as Criminal Case No. 02-2486. San Agustin posted a cash bond of P3,000.00.[^51] The Court of Appeals dismissed his certiorari petition on April 15, 2003. The Supreme Court agreed that the warrantless arrest was unlawful because the arresting officers were not present when Tan was turned over and San Agustin appeared only to answer a subpoena seven days later. It held the inquest void under Rule 112, Section 7.[^52] The absence of a preliminary investigation did not affect the trial court's jurisdiction or the validity of the Information and did not justify release. The RTC nonetheless gravely abused its discretion by ordering a reinvestigation, which is merely a review of the prosecutor's records, instead of a preliminary investigation. The Supreme Court also rejected the Court of Appeals' view that the lighter Article 124(1) charge made a preliminary investigation unnecessary. The need for one depended on the crime charged in the NBI complaint, which was kidnapping or serious illegal detention.[^53] The Supreme Court partially granted the petition. It set aside the reinvestigation order and directed the RTC to order the City Prosecutor to conduct a preliminary investigation under Rule 112, Section 3. It ordered MeTC Branch 77 to suspend Criminal Case No. 02-2486 pending the outcome.[^54]

### Separate detention and murder

In [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291), Bacolod City police officers took Eleuterio Salabas, Ricardo Suganob and Maximo Lomoljo, Jr. on August 31, 2003. Salabas had been under surveillance on suspicion of involvement in the illegal drug trade.[^55] State witness Cecil Brillantes, a police asset, testified that the victims were blindfolded, hogtied and gagged and moved from Moonlight Lodge to Taculing Court and then to Hacienda Motel. They were interrogated and beaten. Suganob and Lomoljo were then shot on Dongail's orders.[^56] Salabas was later seen with Dongail and Natividad at Palao Beach Resort and with Dongail and Fortaleza on a pump boat to Ajuy, Iloilo. A cadaver that his family identified as his was recovered from the waters of Punta Buri, Ajuy on September 19, 2003.[^57] The cases were finally charged as arbitrary detention with murder and transferred to the RTC of Manila, Branch 27. On April 13, 2011, that court convicted the accused of murder and arbitrary detention as separate crimes. For the detentions it imposed two terms of six months to two years and four months for Suganob and Lomoljo and a term of two years and four months to six years for Salabas.[^58] The Court of Appeals affirmed on July 31, 2014. The Supreme Court held that the charges could not be complexed under Article 48. The detention was consummated when the victims were taken into custody and was not a necessary means to kill them.[^59] The murders of Suganob and Lomoljo were proved by Brillantes' direct testimony and the murder of Salabas by an unbroken chain of circumstances. The Supreme Court upheld treachery and found no impropriety in the discharge of Brillantes as a state witness.[^60] It denied the appeal and affirmed the convictions of Dongail and Fortaleza with modifications. It imposed reclusion perpetua for each murder. For the detentions it imposed two terms of four months to one year and eight months and one term of two years and four months to four years and nine months, which it described as the terms the Court of Appeals correctly imposed. It increased the civil indemnity, moral damages and exemplary damages to P100,000.00 each per victim, affirmed the other awards and dismissed the case against Natividad because of his death.[^61]

### Delivery to police

In [*Pascasio Duropan and Raymond Nixer Coloma v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327), barangay kagawad Pascasio Duropan and barangay tanod Raymond Nixer Coloma of Lincod, Maribojoc, Bohol saw William Pacis and four companions harvesting nipa on March 7, 2009. Pacis said they were members of ALIMANGO, a registered cooperative authorized since 1998 to develop and use the mangrove-nipa area.[^62] Doubting him, the two officials pushed the group onto two paddle boats and brought them to the Maribojoc police station over their protests. The police released them after finding no legal basis for the arrest.[^63] Charged with unlawful arrest under Article 269, the two were convicted by the Municipal Circuit Trial Court. The RTC of Tagbilaran City, Branch 4 affirmed and imposed two months and one day of arresto mayor and a P500.00 fine each plus costs. On October 23, 2015, the Court of Appeals affirmed and added 6 percent interest a year on the fine from finality until full payment.[^64] Quoting an earlier ruling, the Supreme Court explained that an officer whose duty gives authority to arrest becomes liable for arbitrary detention if a warrantless arrest lacks legal grounds. It then stated that public officers who have no duty to arrest or detain, or who have that authority but fail to justify the arrest or detention, may be indicted for kidnapping or illegal detention.[^65] It held that a barangay kagawad and a barangay tanod, although persons in authority and agents of persons in authority, are not public officers whose official duty is to arrest or detain. Pacis was arrested and brought to the proper authorities. No overt act within the officials' view showed a crime. The officials knew Pacis and ALIMANGO and were unsure that the land belonged to Calvin Cabalit.[^66] The Indeterminate Sentence Law did not apply because the maximum term did not exceed one year. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals' October 23, 2015 Decision and February 1, 2017 Resolution.[^67]

### Independent officer liability

In [*Junel Alaska v. SPO2 Gil M. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67595), four armed men robbed a Petron gasoline station in Brooke's Point, Palawan on September 24, 2010. They took P70,000.00 and shot an employee.[^68] Police officers arrested Junel Alaska and Adolfo Montesa in Bataraza, Palawan on September 29, 2010, after another employee exchanged text messages with a number linked to the suspects. Both were charged with robbery with homicide before the RTC of Puerto Princesa City. Before arraignment they filed an Omnibus Motion dated February 4, 2011 assailing their warrantless arrests.[^69] In 2011 they also filed complaints for misconduct and arbitrary detention against SPO2 Gil M. Garcia, PO3 Romy P. Galicia and PO2 Ruzel S. Briones with the Ombudsman. The Ombudsman dismissed both because the legality of the arrests belonged before the RTC and cited Section 20 of R.A. No. 6770. It denied reconsideration on November 23, 2012.[^70] On May 21, 2014, the RTC found the arrest and indictment without proper legal bases and noted signs that the police were ready to contrive evidence. The Court of Appeals still dismissed the Rule 43 petition on June 29, 2016. It noted the wrong remedy and ruled that the complainants had waived objections to their arrest.[^71] The Supreme Court relaxed the rules. It found that Alaska had in fact objected before arraignment. It held that the right to assail an arrest in one's own criminal case does not preclude administrative and criminal charges against the arresting officers. Section 20 of R.A. No. 6770 applies only to administrative complaints. The Ombudsman should have suspended its proceedings pending the RTC's resolution of the Omnibus Motion.[^72] The Supreme Court stated that it was not adopting the RTC's findings on the arrest. It granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' decision and resolution and remanded the case to the Ombudsman for resolution on the merits.[^73]

## Key Cases

- [*Milo v. Salanga*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/jul1987/gr_l-37007_1987.html): A barrio captain's alleged participation in detention could support an Article 124 prosecution. The Supreme Court reversed quashal and remanded without determining guilt.[^80]
- [*People of the Philippines v. Aaron Flores*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52183): The trial convictions could not stand because detention and intent were not proved. The Supreme Court acquitted the CAFGU appellants and ordered release unless held for a lawful reason.[^81]
- [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078): Threats and armed encirclement restrained the DENR team without a cell. The Supreme Court affirmed the Sandiganbayan's conviction and indeterminate sentence.[^82]
- [*San Agustin v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45685): A later arbitrary-detention Information did not dispense with the preliminary investigation required in that proceeding. The Supreme Court corrected the RTC's reinvestigation order and suspended the MeTC case pending investigation.[^83]
- [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291): Detention had been consummated before the killings and was not proved a necessary means to murder. The Supreme Court sustained separate convictions with modified relief and dismissed the case against the deceased accused.[^84]
- [*Duropan v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327): A kagawad and tanod brought a harvester to police without lawful grounds. The Supreme Court affirmed their Article 269 convictions, not convictions under Article 124.[^85]
- [*Alaska v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67595): An arrest objection in the complainant's criminal case did not foreclose complaints against officers. The Supreme Court reversed the dismissal and remanded for a merits determination.[^86]

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1:** A mayor refuses a government team's requests to leave and calls armed men who surround the team with rifles pointed at its members. He later serves dinner and argues that nobody was imprisoned in a cell. Does the absence of a cell defeat proof of detention?

**Answer:** No. In [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078), the Supreme Court found restraint through the fear produced by the mayor's refusal and the armed encirclement. The witnesses denied that the team stayed to enjoy the place. The Supreme Court affirmed the Sandiganbayan's conviction for arbitrary detention.[^87]

**Question 2:** CAFGU members were last seen with a person who later disappeared. The testimony does not credibly establish confinement or an intent to deprive him of liberty. Does the disappearance complete the prosecution's proof?

**Answer:** No. In [*People of the Philippines v. Aaron Flores*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52183), the Supreme Court held that the single proved circumstance of being last seen with the victim did not establish arbitrary detention beyond reasonable doubt. The disappearance was not an element of the crime. The Supreme Court reversed the judgment and acquitted the appellants on that failure of proof.[^88]

**Question 3:** A detainee challenges his arrest before the trial court and separately files complaints for arbitrary detention and misconduct with the Ombudsman. May the Ombudsman dismiss both complaints solely because the arrest objection can be resolved in the criminal case against the detainee?

**Answer:** No. [*Alaska v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67595) held that the two proceedings serve different purposes. The proper course was to suspend the Ombudsman proceedings pending the RTC's resolution rather than dismiss them. The Supreme Court reversed the dismissal and remanded the complaints for resolution on the merits without adopting the RTC's findings on the arrest.[^89]

# Quotes

> "Arbitrary Detention is committed by any public officer or employee who, without legal grounds, detains a person."[^90]

> "The prevailing jurisprudence on kidnapping and illegal detention is that the curtailment of the victim's liberty need not involve any physical restraint upon the victim's person. If the acts and actuations of the accused can produce such fear in the mind of the victim sufficient to paralyze the latter, to the extent that the victim is compelled to limit his own actions and movements in accordance with the wishes of the accused, then the victim is, for all intents and purposes, detained against his will."[^91]

> "Neither penal law nor the rules of evidence requires damning testimony to be exclusively supplied by the private complainants in cases of Arbitrary Detention."[^92]

> "Again, the fact of detention, whether illegal or arbitrary, was not clearly established by credible evidence."[^93]

> "In this case, the elements of arbitrary detention were present because accused-appellants were police officers who deprived the three victims of liberty on a mere surveillance and without legal grounds."[^94]

> "We also agree with the Court of Appeals that the absence of a preliminary investigation does not affect the jurisdiction of the trial court but merely the regularity of the proceedings."[^95]

> "The criminal case previously pending before the RTC against Alaska and Montesa, on the one hand, was separate and distinct from the criminal case for Arbitrary Detention and the administrative case for Misconduct against herein respondents, on the other hand."[^96]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Start with the office and its duties.** Identify the authority to arrest or order detention. Then identify the act that restricted liberty and the legal ground claimed for it. Keep these inquiries separate. [*Milo v. Salanga*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/jul1987/gr_l-37007_1987.html) decided only that a barrio captain could be prosecuted and remanded the case. [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078) decided whether the proof supported a mayor's conviction. Eligibility to be charged does not prove the other elements.

> [!TIP]
> **Identify what prevented departure.** Record the orders, threats, weapons and conduct that restricted movement. Ask what the witnesses actually perceived. In [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078), the mayor refused the team's pleas to go home and armed men encircled the team with weapons pointed at them. The dinner that followed did not show that the team was free to leave. A description of the place alone does not answer whether a person could leave.

> [!TIP]
> **Check who took the person and why.** If the person was brought to the police, examine the charge and the purpose of delivery before assuming Article 124 applies. [*Duropan v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327) affirmed a conviction for unlawful arrest under Article 269 against a kagawad and a tanod. It held that they were not public officers whose duty is to arrest or detain. Its discussion of arbitrary detention does not change the crime of conviction.

> [!TIP]
> **Test the claimed legal ground against what the arresting persons saw.** A general assignment to conduct surveillance does not justify a specific arrest. In [*Duropan v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327), harvesting nipa in plain view was not itself a crime. The officials knew Pacis and the cooperative and could have checked his membership. No overt act within their view showed a crime.

> [!NOTE]
> **Do not equate detention with a locked room.** [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078) treated fear as enough when it compels a person to limit his movements according to the wishes of the accused. The weapons mattered because of their effect on the team's freedom of movement. The meal did not undo that restraint.

> [!NOTE]
> **Do not reverse the burden when a person disappears.** In [*People of the Philippines v. Aaron Flores*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52183), the prosecution still had to prove actual confinement and intent. Its evidence could not draw strength from weaknesses in the defense. The acquittal rested on failure of proof. It was not a finding about what happened to the missing person.

> [!NOTE]
> **Watch the public-officer line between Articles 124 and 267.** [*People of the Philippines v. Aaron Flores*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52183) held that CAFGU members, as public officers, could not be convicted of kidnapping and serious illegal detention. The later decision in [*Duropan v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66327) stated that public officers with no duty to arrest, or with that authority but no legal grounds, may be indicted for kidnapping or illegal detention. Read the charge, the officer's duties and the facts together.

> [!TIP]
> **Use every competent witness.** [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078) rejected the argument that incriminating testimony had to come from the private complainants. A police escort's account of the threats and armed encirclement was enough. The affidavit of desistance did not create doubt because no other circumstance undermined the testimony accepted at trial.

> [!NOTE]
> **Keep each tribunal's action distinct.** A quashal, a remand for investigation, an administrative ruling and a criminal conviction answer different questions. The relief ordered shows the limit of each decision. [*Milo v. Salanga*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/jul1987/gr_l-37007_1987.html) ordered further proceedings and did not resolve the truth of the allegations against the barrio captain.

> [!TIP]
> **Separate inquest from preliminary investigation.** [*San Agustin v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45685) found the arrest unlawful and the inquest void. It still held that the missing preliminary investigation did not invalidate the Information or justify release. The remedy was a preliminary investigation with suspension of the MeTC case. A reinvestigation by the prosecutor was not a substitute.

> [!TIP]
> **Trace each count through the judgment.** In [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291), the Informations described complex crimes of arbitrary detention with murder. The judgments imposed separate convictions because the detention was consummated before the killings and was not a necessary means to commit them. Track the detention term for each victim and the separate disposition for the deceased accused.

> [!NOTE]
> **Match the penalty to the duration.** Article 124 fixes the penalty by how long the detention lasted. [*Astorga v. People of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47078) applied clause (1) to a detention that ended the same night. [*People of the Philippines v. P/Insp. Clarence Dongail*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66291) applied clause (2) to Salabas, whose detention did not exceed fifteen days. Compute the indeterminate sentence from the band that fits the proved duration.

> [!TIP]
> **Separate the detainee's own case from complaints against the officers.** [*Alaska v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67595) rejected the view that an arrest objection in the detainee's criminal case is the only remedy. Section 20 of R.A. No. 6770 applies only to administrative complaints. The Ombudsman should suspend rather than dismiss while the trial court resolves the arrest objection. A remand for a merits ruling does not establish the officers' liability in advance.
