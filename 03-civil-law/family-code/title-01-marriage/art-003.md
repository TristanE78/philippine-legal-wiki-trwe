> Art. 3. The formal requisites of marriage are:
>
> (1) Authority of the solemnizing officer;
>
> (2) A valid marriage license except in the cases provided for in Chapter 2 of this Title; and
>
> (3) A marriage ceremony which takes place with the appearance of the contracting parties before the solemnizing officer and their personal declaration that they take each other as husband and wife in the presence of not less than two witnesses of legal age. (53a, 55a)

[^family-code]

# Explanation

Article 3 of the Family Code enumerates the three formal requisites of a valid marriage: authority of the solemnizing officer, a valid marriage license unless exempted under Chapter 2 of Title I plus a marriage ceremony conducted before the solemnizing officer in the presence of at least two witnesses of legal age.[^2] In [*Tenebro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47053), the Supreme Court classified the statutory elements of marriage into essential requisites under Article 2 (legal capacity and freely given consent) and formal requisites under Article 3.[^3] When all essential and formal requisites under Articles 2 and 3 are satisfied, a valid marriage is contracted regardless of a party's claim that the union was intended merely as a sham.[^4]

Under Article 4 of the Family Code, the absence of any essential or formal requisite renders the marriage void *ab initio*, except as stated in Article 35(2) when either or both parties believed in good faith that the solemnizing officer had legal authority.[^5] By contrast, an irregularity in any formal requisite does not affect the validity of the marriage, although the party or parties responsible for the irregularity incur civil, criminal and administrative liability.[^6] Even when a marriage is celebrated in a Roman Catholic church, its civil and legal consequences remain governed by the Family Code rather than Canon Law. Petitions to declare its civil nullity fall within the exclusive original jurisdiction of Regional Trial Courts under Section 19(15) of Batas Pambansa Blg. 129.[^7]

## Requisite 1: Authority of the Solemnizing Officer

Article 3(1) operates together with Article 7 of the Family Code, which specifies who may solemnize marriage, including any incumbent member of the judiciary within the court's jurisdiction and any duly authorized and registered priest, rabbi, imam or minister of any church or religious sect.[^8] In [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979), the Supreme Court contrasted Article 7(1) with Article 8 on the venue of the marriage ceremony, holding that Article 8 is merely a directory provision that neither alters nor qualifies the authority of the solemnizing judge.[^9] While Justices of the Supreme Court and appellate courts hold nationwide authority to solemnize marriages, trial court judges may officiate weddings only within their respective territorial jurisdictions, just as priests may officiate only within the dioceses or areas allowed by their bishops.[^10]

In both [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979) and [*Beso v. Judge Daguman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903), Municipal Circuit Trial Court judges officiated weddings at their residences outside their territorial jurisdictions without satisfying Article 8's three exceptions (point of death, remote places under Article 29 or sworn written request of both parties).[^11] [^12] Both decisions ruled that solemnizing a marriage outside a trial judge's territorial jurisdiction creates an irregularity in the formal requisite under Article 3 that may not affect the validity of the marriage while subjecting the officiating judge to administrative liability.[^13] [^14]

### Presumption of Authority and the Article 35(2) Good-Faith Exception

A marriage contract is a public document carrying prima facie proof of the facts stated in it. The authority of the officer or clergyman shown to have performed the ceremony is presumed absent contrary showing.[^15] In [*Alcantara v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44273), the Supreme Court rejected a husband's uncorroborated claim that a fixer arranged an informal ceremony without a valid officer, noting further that a solemnizing officer need not investigate the regularity of a marriage license already issued by a competent local civil registrar.[^16]

Similarly, in [*Maliwat-Melad v. Melad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69969), a wife sought nullity of her March 23, 1990 marriage by testifying that a Rotary Club member rather than the judge named on the marriage contract actually officiated the ceremony.[^17] Dismissing the petition, the Supreme Court held that bare assertions fail the clear and convincing standard required to overcome a public marriage contract and the presumption of the officer's authority.[^18] Moreover, even assuming a non-judge had officiated, Article 35(2) still preserved the marriage because the wife believed in good faith since 1990 that the judge officiated.[^19] By contrast, where the marriage certificate itself named a Municipal Civil Registrar who holds no authority under Article 7 and defense witnesses proved no mayor or authorized officer officiated, the Supreme Court held in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) that the good faith exception of Article 35(2) was not shown to exist.[^20]

### Purely Sacramental Rites Without Civil Authority

In [*Perfecto v. Judge Esidera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60744), a complaint charged a trial court judge with misconduct that arose from a Catholic ceremony.[^21] She had married her first husband on May 7, 1987.[^22] That marriage was declared void on January 27, 1992.[^23] On March 18, 1990, while the first marriage subsisted, she underwent a purely sacramental rite called *matrimona de conciencia* with her second husband.[^24] She knew that the priest had no civil authority to solemnize marriages.[^25]

The Supreme Court could not conclude that she disobeyed the law against bigamy.[^26] Bigamy punishes a second marriage that would have been valid but for the first.[^27] Her 1990 marriage was invalid because the priest lacked authority to solemnize it.[^28] Religious marriages that lack the requirements of law are invalid and are not considered entered into.[^29] No second marriage could therefore be imputed to her while her first marriage subsisted.[^30]

The Supreme Court then turned to Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code, which penalizes a person who contracts a marriage knowing that the requirements of law have not been complied with.[^31] It said that Article 350 may be of doubtful constitutionality when applied to religious exercise.[^32] It reasoned that the provision burdens belief by penalizing ceremonies that lack the formal requisites of law.[^33] The Supreme Court declined to hold her administratively liable for taking part in the religious ceremony.[^34] Religious freedom did not shield her from liability for other misconduct.[^35] She had knowingly entered a civil marriage and had sexual relations with her second husband while it subsisted.[^36] The Supreme Court found her guilty of violating Canon 1 of the Code of Professional Responsibility and suspended her from judicial service for one month.[^37]

## Requisite 2: A Valid Marriage License

A valid marriage license represents the State's public notice and regulatory participation in every marriage. Its absence outside Chapter 2 exemptions renders the marriage void *ab initio* under Articles 3(2), 4 and 35(3).[^38] Mere private motives such as seeking nullity to evade a pending bigamy charge cannot cure or excuse the absence of a marriage license.[^39] Conversely, the issuance of a marriage license in a municipality where neither party resides or without completing the ten-day publication period constitutes a formal irregularity that leaves the marriage valid.[^40] Nor does the mere absence of a marriage record in the civil registrar's files invalidate a marriage whose celebration is established by a certified marriage contract and supporting proof.[^41]

### Proving the Absence of a Marriage License

Under the standard articulated in [*Alcantara v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44273) and applied in [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449) and [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376), proof that no marriage license existed requires either that the absence be apparent on the marriage contract or that a local civil registrar certify that no such license was issued to the parties.[^42] [^43] [^44]

Earlier jurisprudence (*Sevilla v. Cardenas*, as discussed in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555)) had required local civil registrar certifications under Rule 132, Section 28 to recite verbatim that no record existed "despite diligent search."[^45] In [*Abbas v. Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503), the Supreme Court held that omitting the phrase "despite diligent search" does not strip a local civil registrar's certification of probative weight because Rule 131, Section 3(m) presumes official duty was regularly performed, especially where Carmona's registrar located the actual Marriage License No. 9969967 and showed it had been issued on January 19, 1993 to another couple rather than to Syed Abbas and Gloria Goo on January 8, 1993.[^46] In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), the Supreme Court expressly stated that it had abandoned *Sevilla*'s rigid application of Rule 132, Section 28, crediting a San Juan registrar certification alongside the petitioner's admission against interest that no license or ceremony ever occurred.[^47]

To reconcile divergent factual outcomes (*Republic v. Court of Appeals*, [*Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503), *Kho* and *Vitangcol*, as synthesized in [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964)), [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964) established a holistic approach for evaluating local civil registrar certifications.[^48] Under this approach, courts must assess the certification together with its purpose, the cause of action and all corroborating evidence on record rather than rejecting it for lacking formulaic wording.[^49] In [*Cariaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964), Quezon City records showed that Marriage License No. 131078 appearing on the parties' November 10, 2000 marriage certificate belonged to a different couple, while the local civil registrar explained that under Act No. 3753, Section 12 and National Statistics Office Administrative Order No. 1-93, registrars maintain marriage applications and license books rather than copies of issued licenses themselves.[^50]

By contrast, in [*Pascua v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203), a husband alleging that Marriage License No. 5013850 dated January 6, 2005 was spurious relied solely on a Municipal Treasurer certification and accountable form logbook for Accountable Form No. 54.[^51] Affirming the dismissal of his nullity petition without prejudice, the Supreme Court emphasized that only certifications from the Local Civil Registrar enjoy probative value under Articles 9 and 25 of the Family Code and Rule 132, Section 28 because the Municipal Treasurer tracks only blank form batches rather than duly accomplished marriage licenses issued to specific couples.[^52] In evidentiary disputes over whether a historical ceremony occurred, [*Calimag v. Heirs of Macapaz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61955) also clarified that an unauthenticated canonical marriage certificate is a private writing and an unexcused faxed copy of a marriage contract violates the best evidence rule, although a May 25, 1955 pre-Family Code marriage could still be proven by National Statistics Office birth certificates of legitimate children, open cohabitation and the presumption *semper praesumitur pro matrimonio* under Article 220 of the Civil Code.[^53]

### Cariaga Examined: The Holistic Approach to Local Civil Registrar Certifications

Lovelle and Henry Cariaga married on November 10, 2000.[^54] Their marriage certificate listed Marriage License No. 131078 dated November 9, 2000.[^55] In 2015, Lovelle learned from the Quezon City civil registry that this license belonged to another couple.[^56] The Assistant City Civil Registrar certified that no record of the license existed for Henry and Lovelle and that it was issued to Mamerto Yambao and Amelia Parado.[^57] Lovelle then petitioned to declare the marriage void for lack of a license.[^58] The prosecutor found no collusion and Henry did not answer the petition.[^59]

The trial court denied the petition.[^60] It reasoned that the certification showed only that the listed license number belonged to another couple.[^61] It added that Lovelle had not submitted the original or a certified true copy of the certification.[^62] The Court of Appeals affirmed.[^63]

The Supreme Court reviewed its earlier decisions on civil registrar certifications.[^64] It discussed *Republic v. Court of Appeals and Castro*, a civil nullity case where a certification of due search and inability to find a license had probative value.[^65] It contrasted *Vitangcol v. People*, a bigamy prosecution where a similar certification was suspect because the accused obtained it to evade conviction.[^66] It then clarified the rule.[^67] Courts must take a holistic approach when the absence of a license is shown through a certification from the local civil registrar.[^68] The probative value of the certification cannot be divorced from its purpose, the cause of action and the other evidence.[^69] Courts assess its sufficiency with due regard to law and procedure, the attendant facts and the evidence on record.[^70]

Applying that approach, the Supreme Court found the certification sufficient.[^71] Its first part said that no record of the license existed for Henry and Lovelle, which corroborated Lovelle's testimony that she never applied for a license.[^72] Its second part said that the license belonged to Yambao and Parado. The documents from that couple's application supported the statement.[^73] The facts closely resembled [*Abbas v. Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503), where the listed license number belonged to another couple.[^74] The absence of the words despite diligent search did not by itself reduce the probative value of the certification.[^75]

The Republic argued that a license with a different number could have been issued and that a typographical error caused the mismatch.[^76] The Supreme Court treated this as bare supposition.[^77] It explained that a registrar must verify the entries in a certificate of marriage, including the license number.[^78] It noted that Lovelle had procured the certification that Rule 132, Section 28 requires and had done so on two occasions.[^79] The Republic could have called the registrar as a witness or verified the records itself.[^80] It did neither.[^81]

Last, the Supreme Court addressed unclean hands.[^82] Lovelle's testimony suggested that she accepted the help of her parents with the documents and that a spurious license may have been used.[^83] The Supreme Court held that such testimony should not by itself preclude a finding of nullity on the ground of unclean hands.[^84] It reasoned that the irregularity and any resulting liability must be determined in a separate proper case.[^85] A contrary ruling would validate marriages that the law declares void.[^86] The earlier decision in [*Alcantara v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44273) had applied the clean hands principle against a husband who sought to undo a marriage he had initiated.[^87] In that case the registrar's certification showed that a license had been issued.[^88] The Supreme Court granted the petition and declared the Cariaga marriage void under Article 35(3) of the Family Code.[^89]

### The Five-Year Cohabitation Exception Under Article 34

Chapter 2 of Title I exempts certain marriages from securing a marriage license, most litigated among which is Article 34 covering a man and a woman who have lived together as husband and wife for at least five years without any legal impediment to marry each other.[^90] In [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319), construing Article 76 of the Civil Code alongside the parallel rule in Article 34 of the Family Code, the Supreme Court held that the five-year cohabitation period must immediately precede the wedding day, remain continuous and unbroken and be marked by exclusivity with both parties capacitated to marry each other throughout the entire five-year span.[^91] Where Pepito Niñal married Norma Bayadog on December 11, 1986, roughly twenty months after his first wife's death on April 24, 1985, their prior adulterous cohabitation during the subsisting first marriage could not be counted, rendering the second marriage void *ab initio* and open to collateral attack by his heirs after his death.[^92]

Modern Family Code decisions enforce these Article 34 requisites strictly:

- **Falsified affidavit and overseas separation:** In [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), the couple married on May 8, 2009 without a license by executing an Article 34 affidavit, yet testimony showed the husband worked continuously in Israel from May 24, 2006 until May 3, 2009.[^93] The Supreme Court credited his testimony as an admission against interest exposing him to perjury under Article 183 of the Revised Penal Code and affirmed nullity because a false affidavit confers no Article 34 exemption.[^94]
- **Missing affidavit and brief courtship:** In [*Bounsit-Torralba v. Torralba*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68706), the parties became sweethearts only in December 1995 before marrying on January 26, 1996, ticked the Article 34 box on the marriage contract without attaching a sworn affidavit and had no marriage license.[^95] Rejecting a hearsay psychological report under *Tan-Andal*, the Supreme Court nonetheless declared the marriage void *ab initio* for lack of a marriage license under Articles 3(2), 4 and 35(3).[^96]
- **Minority during alleged cohabitation:** In [*OCA v. Judges Necessario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55788) and [*Cosca v. Judge Palaypayon*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/am_mtj-92-721_1994.html), solemnizing judges accepted pro forma Article 34 affidavits from parties aged 18 to 22 whose claimed five-year cohabitation would have begun when one or both were minors under a legal impediment.[^97] [^98] The Supreme Court held that solemnizing officers have a duty to scrutinize the parties' qualifications and cannot hide behind pro forma affidavits.[^99]

### Niñal Examined: The Meaning of Five Years of Cohabitation

Pepito Niñal married Teodulfa Bellones on September 26, 1974.[^100] Teodulfa died on April 24, 1985 after Pepito shot her.[^101] On December 11, 1986, Pepito married Norma Bayadog without a marriage license.[^102] The couple executed an affidavit that stated they had lived together as husband and wife for at least five years.[^103] Pepito died in a car accident on February 19, 1997.[^104] His children from the first marriage then petitioned to declare the second marriage void for lack of a license.[^105] The trial court dismissed the petition.[^106] It held that the action should have been filed before Pepito died.[^107]

Both marriages took place before the Family Code took effect, so the Civil Code governed.[^108] A valid license was a requisite of marriage. Its absence made the marriage void from the beginning.[^109] Article 76 of the Civil Code exempted a man and a woman who had lived together exclusively as husband and wife for a continuous and unbroken period of at least five years.[^110] The Supreme Court explained that the exemption spares the couple the humiliation of publicity from a license application.[^111] The only issue was what kind of cohabitation counts toward the five years.[^112]

The Supreme Court held that the five years are counted back from the wedding day.[^113] The cohabitation must be exclusive, which means that no third party was involved at any time within the period.[^114] It must also be continuous, which means unbroken.[^115] It must be a union that would have been legal had it not been for the absence of the marriage contract.[^116] Pepito's first marriage ended only about twenty months before the second marriage.[^117] Any earlier cohabitation with Norma took place while his first marriage subsisted.[^118] It was therefore not the cohabitation that the law contemplates.[^119] The second marriage was void from the beginning for want of a license.[^120]

On standing, the Supreme Court held that Article 47 of the Family Code does not govern petitions for declaration of nullity.[^121] It distinguished a voidable marriage, which is valid until annulled, from a void marriage, which is deemed never to have taken place.[^122] Pepito's death extinguished nothing because a void marriage creates no bond.[^123] A court may pass on the validity of a marriage in a case not directly brought to question it, such as one on heirship, legitimacy or settlement of estate, when validity is essential to the case.[^124] The Supreme Court granted the petition and reinstated the case.[^125]

### Aguirre Examined: Temporary Absence and the Five-Year Cohabitation Period

Boots Aguirre left for Israel to work on May 24, 2006 and stayed there until May 3, 2009.[^126] He returned to marry Alma Aguirre.[^127] The two signed an affidavit of cohabitation on May 6, 2009 that stated they had lived together as husband and wife since December 2003.[^128] They married on May 8, 2009 without a license.[^129] The officiating priest certified that the marriage fell under Article 34.[^130] Boots later petitioned to declare the marriage void on the ground that the affidavit was false.[^131] The trial court and the Court of Appeals declared the marriage void.[^132]

The Republic argued that a temporary absence abroad does not break cohabitation.[^133] It also argued that Boots was estopped from calling his own affidavit false.[^134] The Supreme Court framed the crux as the first requisite of Article 34, which is cohabitation as husband and wife for at least five years before the marriage.[^135] It listed five requisites for the exemption.[^136] They are five years of cohabitation, no legal impediment, the absence of impediment at the time of marriage, an affidavit of the parties and a sworn statement of the solemnizing officer.[^137]

The Supreme Court accepted that a temporary absence does not interrupt cohabitation.[^138] That holds when the separation is by mutual consent or for convenience, health or business and neither party intends to abandon the other.[^139] It stressed that such cases first established that the parties did live together as husband and wife.[^140] Proof of that kind includes a common residence, a joint bank account and shared expenses.[^141]

The Republic did not prove cohabitation in the five years before the marriage.[^142] From 2001 to 2004, Boots and Alma lived separately while they were dating.[^143] They shared a rented space only for a short time and together with relatives.[^144] Boots testified that they never lived together as a couple without other people around.[^145] While Boots was in Israel, his absence was not merely temporary.[^146] The Supreme Court found that he had no intention to return to live with Alma even on occasion.[^147] It found no evidence that they deported themselves as husband and wife while he was abroad.[^148]

The Supreme Court gave weight to Boots's own testimony.[^149] It reasoned that the falsity of the affidavit could expose him to perjury under Article 183 of the Revised Penal Code.[^150] His statements were therefore admissions against interest.[^151] Article 34 is an exception that must be strictly construed.[^152] A false affidavit of cohabitation renders the marriage void.[^153] The decision relied on *Republic v. Dayot*, where a fabricated affidavit could not stand in for a license.[^154] It distinguished *Laguda v. Manguardia*, where the husband regularly returned home and stayed with his wife.[^155] The petition was denied.[^156] Occasional stays in the same place did not amount to the cohabitation that Article 34 contemplates.[^157] The false affidavit was ineffectual and gave no exemption from the license requirement.[^158]

## Requisite 3: A Marriage Ceremony

Article 3(3) defines a marriage ceremony as the appearance of the contracting parties before the solemnizing officer and their personal declaration that they take each other as husband and wife in the presence of not less than two witnesses of legal age.[^159] Reading Article 3(3) with Article 6, [*Ronulo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57196) held that while Philippine law prescribes no rigid form or religious rite, a ceremony exists whenever those minimum statutory elements occur.[^160]

In [*Ronulo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57196), an Aglipayan priest conducted a wedding-style "blessing" on March 29, 2003 before witnesses after a Catholic parish priest refused to officiate for lack of a marriage license.[^161] Affirming his conviction under Article 352 of the Revised Penal Code and Section 44 of Act No. 3613 (fined `P200.00`), the Supreme Court held that the constitutional separation of church and state is preserved by Article 6's latitude on religious form and that the absence of a marriage certificate does not negate that a marriage ceremony actually transpired.[^162] By contrast, pre-signing marriage contracts before the parties appear and exchange declarations violates Article 6.[^163]

In [*People v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70088), where a municipal mayor was accused of falsifying a March 26, 2015 marriage certificate after his vice-mayor delivered marriage advice at the reception, the Supreme Court clarified that legal solemnization consists strictly of non-transferable acts by the authorized officer: personal appearance of the parties, their personal declaration of consent and proclamation by the officer.[^164] Ancillary ceremonial moments such as delivering a homily or giving marriage advice do not constitute or replace legal solemnization, leading to the mayor's acquittal on reasonable doubt.[^165]

### Ronulo Examined: When a Blessing Becomes a Marriage Ceremony

Joey Umadac and Claire Bingayen were scheduled to marry on March 29, 2003 in a Catholic parish church.[^166] The officiating priest refused after he learned that the couple had no marriage license.[^167] The couple and their guests then went to an Aglipayan church and asked Rene Ronulo, an Aglipayan priest, to perform a ceremony.[^168] Ronulo agreed even though the couple told him that they had no marriage certificate.[^169] He held a mass in front of the groom, the bride, their parents, the sponsors and the guests.[^170] The groom's mother testified that the couple declared that they took each other as husband and wife.[^171]

Ronulo was charged under Article 352 of the Revised Penal Code with performing an illegal marriage ceremony.[^172] The municipal trial court fined him P200.00 under Section 44 of Act No. 3613.[^173] The Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals affirmed the conviction.[^174]

The Supreme Court stated the elements of the offense.[^175] They are the authority of the solemnizing officer and the performance of an illegal marriage ceremony.[^176] Ronulo admitted that he had authority to solemnize marriages.[^177] The only issue was whether his blessing was an illegal marriage ceremony.[^178]

Articles 3(3) and 6 of the Family Code supplied the definition.[^179] No prescribed form or religious rite is required.[^180] The law sets two minimum requirements.[^181] The parties must appear personally before a solemnizing officer.[^182] They must declare before at least two witnesses that they take each other as husband and wife.[^183] The prosecution proved both through the testimony of the groom's mother and the admission of Ronulo.[^184]

Ronulo invoked the separation of church and State.[^185] The Supreme Court held that Article 6 already preserves the principle by leaving every religion free in the form of its rites.[^186] That freedom is subject to the core requirements of law.[^187] The Supreme Court added that the State has a paramount interest in the sanctity of marriage and may penalize acts that mock it.[^188] The blessing therefore qualified as a marriage ceremony.[^189]

The ceremony was illegal because Ronulo knew that the couple had no marriage license.[^190] His knowledge of the missing requirements negated his claim of good faith.[^191] The lack of a marriage certificate did not negate his liability because a certificate is not among the requirements of Article 3(3).[^192] The non-filing of a criminal complaint against the couple also did not negate his liability.[^193] Article 352 refers to the Marriage Law only for the penalty, so Section 44 applied and not Section 39.[^194] The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^195]

### Simulated Signings Versus Motivated Ceremonies

Where parties merely sign a marriage contract privately without a duly authorized solemnizing officer or an actual ceremony, Article 3(3) is completely absent. In [*Morigo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464), Lucio Morigo and Lucia Barrete signed an August 30, 1990 marriage contract by themselves with no solemnizing officer or ceremony before he remarried on October 4, 1992.[^196] Acquitting him of bigamy, the Supreme Court ruled that mere private signing of a contract bears no semblance to an ostensibly valid marriage.[^197] Similarly, in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), signing a blank marriage certificate on December 5, 2007 solely to facilitate a Japanese visa application without a ceremony or marriage license produced a void marriage *ab initio* under Articles 2, 3 and 4.[^198] By contrast, when parties knowingly appear before an authorized judge, obtain a license and exchange vows before witnesses, all essential and formal requisites under Articles 2 and 3 are fulfilled even if one party privately characterizes the January 7, 1994 union as a "sham marriage" to resolve an administrative charge.[^199]

## Absence Versus Irregularity, Criminal Liability and Judicial Discipline

Because an invalid marriage lacking Article 3 formal requisites is void *ab initio*, recent jurisprudence under *Pulido v. People* (as applied in [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) and [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)) permits an accused in a criminal bigamy prosecution under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code to prove the absence of Article 3 requisites collaterally without first obtaining a civil judicial declaration of nullity under Article 40.[^200] [^201] In [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376), a January 14, 2009 Imus local civil registrar certification showing no record of an application that the parties were alleged to have filed in June 2000 for her August 3, 2000 first marriage rebutted the marriage certificate and warranted her acquittal of bigamy.[^202] Earlier, in [*Tenebro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47053), the Supreme Court had confirmed that a subsequent declaration of nullity based on Article 36 psychological incapacity does not negate the presence of Article 2 essential requisites and Article 3 formal requisites at celebration and does not bar bigamy liability.[^203]

### Lapira Examined: Collateral Proof of a Void First Marriage in a Bigamy Prosecution

The prosecution of Ma. Fe Imelda Lapira began with an Information that alleged a first marriage to Takahiko Sato, a Japanese national, on August 3, 2000 in Meycauayan, Bulacan.[^204] It alleged a second marriage to Jimmy Fariscal on April 17, 2001 in Guagua, Pampanga.[^205] Lapira claimed that the first marriage was a sham meant to ease her entry into Japan.[^206] She said she signed a blank piece of bond paper and that neither Sato nor the solemnizing officer was present.[^207] She also said she never applied for a marriage license.[^208] The Local Civil Registrar of Imus testified that her office had no record of an application for a license by Sato and Lapira.[^209]

The Regional Trial Court convicted Lapira of bigamy.[^210] It reasoned that she could not unilaterally declare her first marriage invalid to avoid prosecution.[^211] The Court of Appeals affirmed.[^212] It relied on *Vitangcol v. People*, under which an accused who invoked the lack of a license remained liable because no judicial declaration of nullity preceded the second marriage.[^213]

The Supreme Court found the petition meritorious.[^214] It applied *Pulido v. People*, which abandoned the rule that a judicial declaration of nullity is needed to prove a void first marriage in a bigamy case.[^215] An accused may now raise the voidness of a first marriage as a defense without a prior declaration.[^216] The same rule applies to marriages under both the Civil Code and the Family Code.[^217] It does not extend to a voidable first marriage, which stays valid until annulled.[^218]

The Supreme Court restated the elements of bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code.[^219] A prior valid marriage is one of them.[^220] A void first marriage is inexistent, so an element of the felony is lacking.[^221] Lapira attacked the first marriage for lack of a license.[^222] She presented a certification dated January 14, 2009 from the Imus civil registrar.[^223] It stated that the office had no record of an application for a license by Sato and Lapira, who were alleged to have applied in June 2000.[^224] It added that the application records for the year 2000 were still intact in the archives.[^225] The Supreme Court held that this certification met the standard in [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964).[^226]

The prosecution relied on the marriage certificate and on the presumption that entries in public records are prima facie evidence.[^227] The Supreme Court held that the certification rebutted that presumption.[^228] It followed [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), where the rebuttal of the presumption returned to the prosecution the burden of proving each element beyond reasonable doubt.[^229] Lapira's first marriage was then left without other proof.[^230] The Supreme Court found reasonable doubt on the existence of a prior marriage.[^231] It added that penal statutes are construed strictly against the State and liberally in favor of the accused.[^232] It acquitted Lapira on the ground that the prosecution failed to prove all the elements of bigamy.[^233]

Equitable doctrines cannot override Article 3's positive statutory mandate, although criminal consequences depend on whether formal requisites ostensibly existed:

1. **Civil nullity petitions:** In [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964), the Supreme Court held that unclean hands does not by itself bar a declaration of nullity for a missing license because a contrary rule would validate unions the Family Code declares void.[^234]
2. **Criminal estoppel boundary ([*Santiago*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60934) vs. [*Genio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)):** In [*Santiago v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60934), the accused contracted a July 29, 1997 marriage to an already-married man by falsifying an Article 34 exemption on the marriage contract despite having known him only since 1993. The Supreme Court refused to let her escape bigamy conviction by invoking her own deliberate misrepresentation.[^235] Later distinguishing [*Santiago*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60934), [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) held that estoppel cannot supply missing Articles 2 and 3 requisites where the second marriage lacked an authorized solemnizing officer and ceremony, acquitting the accused of bigamy under Article 349 while convicting him under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code for knowingly contracting an illegal marriage.[^236]

Administratively, solemnizing officers face severe sanctions for ignoring Article 3. In [*Moreno v. Judge Bernabe*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/jul1995/am_mtj-94-963_1995.html), a judge who married a pregnant bride without a license on her husband's assurance that it was forthcoming was fined `P10,000.00`, the Supreme Court holding that humanitarian good faith mitigates but does not excuse violating Article 3(2).[^237] In [*Cosca v. Judge Palaypayon*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/am_mtj-92-721_1994.html), solemnizing marriages without licenses and accepting an impossible Article 34 affidavit from an eighteen-year-old groom claiming six years of cohabitation led to a `P20,000.00` fine on the judge and dismissal of the clerk of court.[^238] Likewise, in [*OCA v. Judges Necessario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55788), an audit of 643 marriage certificates (including 280 under Article 34) exposed tampered same-day licenses, missing embassy certificates under Article 21 and pro forma Article 34 affidavits by young couples aged 18 to 22, resulting in the dismissal from service of three judges (with a fourth judge already dismissed in a prior administrative case) for gross ignorance of Articles 3, 4 and 34.[^239]

## Related Provisions

- [Article 2](art-002.md): Enumerates the two essential requisites of marriage (legal capacity of a male and a female and freely given consent).
- [Article 4](art-004.md): Prescribes nullity *ab initio* for absence of any essential or formal requisite (except under Article 35(2)) and liability without invalidation for formal irregularities.
- [Article 6](art-006.md): Declares that no prescribed form or religious rite is required while mandating personal declaration before the solemnizing officer and at least two witnesses of legal age.
- [Article 7](art-007.md) and [Article 8](art-008.md): Identify authorized solemnizing officers (with territorial limits) and directory rules on marriage venue.
- [Article 9](art-009.md), [Article 23](art-023.md) and [Article 25](art-025.md): Designate the Local Civil Registrar as the proper issuer and custodian of marriage license applications and records.
- [Article 34](art-034.md): Exempts couples who have cohabited continuously and exclusively as husband and wife without legal impediment for at least five years before marriage from securing a marriage license.
- [Article 35](art-035.md): Declares void marriages solemnized without authority (subject to the Article 35(2) good-faith exception) and marriages solemnized without a valid license (Article 35(3)).

## Key Cases

- [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979) (1996): Distinguishes territorial authority under Article 7(1) from directory venue under Article 8 and holds that out-of-jurisdiction solemnization creates an irregularity in Article 3(1) warranting administrative suspension.[^252]
- [*Beso v. Judge Daguman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903) (2000): Reaffirms territorial limits on judicial solemnizing authority under Articles 3(1) and 7(1) and fines a judge `P5,000.00` for officiating outside his jurisdiction and failing to safeguard marriage contracts under Article 23.[^253]
- [*Maliwat-Melad v. Melad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69969) (2025): Requires clear and convincing proof to overcome the presumption of solemnizing officer authority and applies Article 35(2) good faith where a party believed a judge officiated.[^254]
- [*Abbas v. Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503) (2013): Relies on Rule 131, Section 3(m) to sustain an LCR certification omitting the words "despite diligent search" and voids a marriage whose listed license belonged to another couple.[^255]
- [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964) (2021): Adopts a holistic evidentiary review of local civil registrar certifications and holds that unclean hands does not by itself bar nullity for a missing license.[^256]
- [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555) (2021): Confirms abandonment of *Sevilla*'s rigid Rule 132 wording rule and declares a simulated visa marriage with a blank contract void under Articles 2, 3 and 4.[^257]
- [*Pascua v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203) (2025): Rules that Municipal Treasurer accountable form logbooks lack probative value compared to Local Civil Registrar negative certifications.[^258]
- [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319) (2000): Mandates continuous, exclusive five-year cohabitation free of legal impediments immediately preceding marriage for license exemption.[^259]
- [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449) (2026): Voids a marriage grounded on a false Article 34 affidavit because the evidence did not show five years of cohabitation, the absence abroad was not temporary and the spouse's admissions exposed him to perjury.[^260]
- [*Ronulo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57196) (2014): Holds that personal appearance and mutual vows before two witnesses turn a religious "blessing" into a marriage ceremony under Article 3(3) and Article 6.[^261]
- [*People v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70088) (2025): Separates non-transferable acts of legal solemnization from incidental ceremonial speeches like homilies or marriage advice.[^262]
- [*Morigo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464) (2004): Acquits of bigamy because privately signing a marriage contract without an officer or ceremony fails Article 3(3).[^263]
- [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) (2026): Applies *Pulido* to allow collateral proof of missing marriage license via an LCR negative certification in a criminal bigamy case.[^264]
- [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) (2024): Holds that estoppel cannot replace missing Articles 2 and 3 requisites for bigamy under Article 349 while imposing liability under Article 350.[^265]
- [*OCA v. Judges Necessario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55788) (2013): Dismisses judges for package instant marriages involving tampered licenses and pro forma Article 34 affidavits by young couples.[^266]

## Suggested Answers

### Question 1

A and B married on May 8, 2009 without a marriage license by signing an Article 34 affidavit claiming five years of cohabitation, even though A worked in Israel from May 2006 to May 2009. In a separate nullity suit, C seeks to declare his marriage void for lack of a license by presenting only a Municipal Treasurer certification that Serial No. 5013850 of Accountable Form No. 54 does not appear as issued to him and his wife. How should the court resolve A's and C's petitions?

**Answer:**

A's marriage must be declared void *ab initio*, whereas C's petition should be dismissed without prejudice for insufficiency of evidence.

Under Article 3(2) of the Family Code, a valid marriage license is a formal requisite unless exempted under Chapter 2. Its absence voids the marriage under Articles 4 and 35(3).[^267] In [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319) and [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), the Supreme Court held that Article 34 requires five full years of continuous, exclusive cohabitation immediately preceding the wedding. A false affidavit of cohabitation is ineffectual and confers no exemption, even when the falsity is proved through the petitioner's own admission against interest.[^268] [^269] Meanwhile, under [*Pascua v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203), only a Local Civil Registrar certification (or subpoena of its records) carries probative value under Rule 132, Section 28 and Articles 9 and 25 because the Municipal Treasurer tracks only blank form batches rather than issued marriage licenses.[^270] Dismissal of C's petition for lack of probative evidence does not operate as *res judicata* against a properly supported subsequent action.[^271]

### Question 2

X and Y obtain a valid marriage license and request an incumbent Municipal Circuit Trial Court judge from an adjoining municipality outside his territorial jurisdiction to solemnize their marriage in a hotel ballroom, both believing in good faith that all judges may officiate anywhere. Compare the civil validity of X and Y's marriage with a secret religious marriage ceremony (*matrimona de conciencia*) performed inside a parish office by a priest who holds no civil authorization from the National Archives.

**Answer:**

X and Y's marriage remains valid despite the judge's administrative liability, whereas the secret religious ceremony produces no civil marriage under Philippine law.

Under Articles 3(1) and 7(1) of the Family Code, incumbent trial court judges may solemnize marriages only within their territorial jurisdictions. As held in [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979) and [*Beso v. Judge Daguman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903), officiating outside that area without Article 8 compliance is a formal irregularity that subjects the judge to administrative sanctions while leaving the marriage valid (further protected by Article 35(2) where either or both parties believed in good faith that the officer had authority, as applied in [*Maliwat-Melad v. Melad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69969)).[^272] [^273] [^274] By contrast, as held in [*Perfecto v. Judge Esidera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60744), a purely religious ceremony officiated by a priest lacking civil license under Article 7(2) lacks the formal requisite under Article 3(1) and creates no civilly valid marriage.[^275]

### Question 3

Suppose D and E privately sign a marriage contract for visa convenience with no solemnizing officer or ceremony and later separate. Years later, D marries F. Upon prosecution for bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code, D offers a Local Civil Registrar negative certification showing no marriage license was ever issued for the D-E union and testifies that no ceremony occurred. Can D invoke the invalidity of the D-E union in the bigamy case without a prior civil judgment of nullity? Would the result change if D had instead celebrated an actual marriage before an unauthorized Local Civil Registrar knowing it was illegal?

**Answer:**

D can invoke the voidness of the D-E union as a defense in the bigamy trial without a prior civil nullity decree and should be acquitted of bigamy, whereas knowingly participating in a ceremony before an unauthorized officer exposes D to conviction under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code.

As applied in [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) following *Pulido*, an accused in a bigamy case need not obtain a prior judicial declaration of nullity to prove that an earlier or subsequent marriage was void *ab initio* for missing Article 3 requisites.[^276] Under [*Morigo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464) and [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), privately signing a contract without a ceremony or license means there is neither a valid marriage license nor a marriage ceremony.[^277] [^278] In the second scenario, [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) holds that estoppel cannot replace missing Articles 2 and 3 requisites to sustain bigamy under Article 349 when the officiant lacked authority outside Article 35(2). Yet the accused may still be convicted under Article 350 for knowingly contracting a marriage against the provisions of law (distinct from [*Santiago v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60934), where the parties actively falsified an Article 34 license exemption before an otherwise authorized celebration).[^279] [^280]

# Quotes

> "More importantly, the elementary principle underlying this provision is the authority of the solemnizing judge. Under Article 3, one of the formal requisites of marriage is the "authority of the solemnizing officer." Under Article 7, marriage may be solemnized by, among others, "any incumbent member of the judiciary within the court's jurisdiction." Article 8, which is a directory provision, refers only to the venue of the marriage ceremony and does not alter or qualify the authority of the solemnizing officer as provided in the preceding provision. Non-compliance herewith will not invalidate the marriage."[^281]

> "A priest who is commissioned and allowed by his ordinary to marry the faithful, is authorized to do so only within the area of the diocese or place allowed by his Bishop. An appellate court justice or a Justice of this Court has jurisdiction over the entire Philippines to solemnize marriages, regardless of the venue, as long as the requisites of the law are complied with. However, Judges who are appointed to specific jurisdictions may officiate in weddings only within said areas and not beyond. Where a judge solemnizes a marriage outside his court’s jurisdiction, there is a resultant irregularity in the formal requisite laid down in Article 3, which while it may not affect the validity of the marriage, may subject the officiating official to administrative liability."[^282]

> "Issuance of a marriage license in a city or municipality, not the residence of either of the contracting parties, and issuance of a marriage license despite the absence of publication or prior to the completion of the 10- day period for publication are considered mere irregularities that do not affect the validity of the marriage. An irregularity in any of the formal requisites of marriage does not affect its validity but the party or parties responsible for the irregularity are civilly, criminally and administratively liable."[^283]

> "However, this Court has abandoned Sevilla's inordinately stringent application of Republic v. Court of Appeals and Castro. It has since been clarified that Rule 132, Section 28 of the Rules of Court does not require a distinct, categorical statement to the effect that a diligent search was conducted. The presumption of regularity in the performance of official duty stipulated in Rule 131, Section 3(m) of the Rules of Court works to create a presumption that concerned officers have made a diligent search. Only when there is actual countervailing proof should those officers be taken as having performed their tasks less than diligently."[^284]

> "Hence, lest there be any confusion, the Court here clarifies that in cases where the absence of a marriage license is sought to be established through a certification issued by the local civil registrar, courts must take a holistic approach in resolving the case. To borrow the language in Vitangcol, the appreciation of the probative value of the certification cannot be divorced from the purpose of its presentation, the cause of action in the case, and the context of the presentation of the certification in relation to the other evidence presented in the case. Accordingly, courts must assess the sufficiency of the certification with due regard to applicable law and procedure, the attendant facts, and the evidence on record."[^285]

> "The Court stresses that in declaration of nullity of marriage cases, it is the certification issued by the Local Civil Registrar that enjoys probative value, as it is the Registrar's legal mandate to maintain records of duly accomplished sworn applications for marriage licenses. The fact that fees are required to be paid to the Municipal Treasurer before the marriage license is issued by the Local Civil Registrar does not mean that records related thereto are likewise kept by the former. To repeat, the permanent record kept by the Municipal Treasurer only relates to blank and unaccomplished forms for marriage licenses."[^286]

> "Working on the assumption that Pepito and Norma have lived together as husband and wife for five years without the benefit of marriage, that five-year period should be computed on the basis of a cohabitation as "husband and wife" where the only missing factor is the special contract of marriage to validate the union. In other words, the five-year common-law cohabitation period, which is counted back from the date of celebration of marriage, should be a period of legal union had it not been for the absence of the marriage. This 5-year period should be the years immediately before the day of the marriage and it should be a period of cohabitation characterized by exclusivity - meaning no third party was involved at any time within the 5 years and continuity - that is unbroken."[^287]

> "Even prior to the date of the enactment of Article 352 of the RPC, as amended, the rule was clear that no prescribed form of religious rite for the solemnization of the marriage is required. However, as correctly found by the CA, the law sets the minimum requirements constituting a marriage ceremony: first, there should be the personal appearance of the contracting parties before a solemnizing officer; and second, their declaration in the presence of not less than two witnesses that they take each other as husband and wife."[^288]

> "In the instant case, however, no marriage ceremony at all was performed by a duly authorized solemnizing officer. Petitioner and Lucia Barrete merely signed a marriage contract on their own. The mere private act of signing a marriage contract bears no semblance to a valid marriage and thus, needs no judicial declaration of nullity. Such act alone, without more, cannot be deemed to constitute an ostensibly valid marriage for which petitioner might be held liable for bigamy unless he first secures a judicial declaration of nullity before he contracts a subsequent marriage."[^289]

> "Estoppel is a principle of equity that may only be applied in the absence of, and never against, positive law. Relevantly, Articles 2 and 3 of the Family Code unequivocally provide the essential requisites for a valid marriage. Thus, the Court cannot apply estoppel and supplant the clear requirements of the Family Code, notwithstanding any alleged misrepresentation on the part of Rommel."[^290]

> "There being no other proof of the validity of the marriage between Takahiko and Imelda, then there is reasonable doubt as to the existence of a prior marriage, which is one of the elements of Bigamy."[^291]

> "Such falsity renders the affidavit ineffectual; it does not confer exemption from the license requirement."[^292]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> Always obtain the negative certification from the Local Civil Registrar of the place where the marriage license was purportedly issued rather than the Municipal Treasurer. Under Articles 9 and 25 of the Family Code, only the Local Civil Registrar keeps records of sworn license applications, whereas the Municipal Treasurer only tracks blank accountable form batches.

> [!TIP]
> When evaluating an Article 34 five-year cohabitation exemption, calculate backward five full continuous years from the date of celebration and verify that neither party had a subsisting marriage or minority impediment at any moment during that entire five-year period.

> [!TIP]
> Distinguish territorial authority of the solemnizing officer under Article 7(1) from venue under Article 8, and distinguish absence of a formal requisite from mere formal irregularity. Non-compliance with venue or municipal residence requirements for a marriage license does not void the marriage, whereas absence of a license outside Chapter 2 exemptions or total absence of a ceremony voids the marriage *ab initio*.
