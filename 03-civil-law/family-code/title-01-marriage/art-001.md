> **Article 1.** Marriage is a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman entered into in accordance with law for the establishment of conjugal and family life. It is the foundation of the family and an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation, except that marriage settlements may fix the property relations during the marriage within the limits provided by this Code. (52a)[^family-code]

# Explanation

Article 1 of the Family Code sets forth the statutory definition and dual juridical nature of marriage in Philippine law.[^2] Echoing the constitutional mandate that the State recognizes the Filipino family as the foundation of the nation and protects marriage as an inviolable social institution and the foundation of the family,[^3] Article 1 characterizes marriage both as a **special contract of permanent union** between a man and a woman and as an **inviolable social institution** whose nature, consequences and incidents are governed by law and not subject to private stipulation, save only for marriage settlements fixing property relations within the limits of the Family Code.[^4] [^5] [^6] [^7]

The provision contains two interconnected normative clauses that govern all subsequent chapters of Title I:

1. **The Special Contract Clause (*First Sentence*):** Marriage is defined as a *"special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman entered into in accordance with law for the establishment of conjugal and family life."*[^8] As a contract, it requires the free and genuine consent of two legally capacitated contracting parties, who must be a male and a female, given in accordance with the essential and formal requisites prescribed by law.[^9] [^10] [^11] [^12] Unlike ordinary civil contracts, however, it creates a permanent and exclusive partnership and a new legal status (*vinculum juris*) in whose maintenance the general public and the State are deeply interested.[^13] [^14] [^15] [^16]
2. **The Institutional and Non-Stipulation Clause (*Second Sentence*):** Marriage is *"the foundation of the family and an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation, except that marriage settlements may fix the property relations during the marriage within the limits provided by this Code."*[^17] Once the parties enter into marriage, the State becomes the sole third party involved in the marital bond.[^18] The spouses cannot by mutual agreement alter the nature of marriage, waive their essential marital obligations, stipulate that their marriage will be temporary or inoperative or dissolve their union on grounds not provided by law.[^19] [^20] [^21] [^22] [^23]

---

## 1. Constitutional Foundation and Dual Juridical Nature of Marriage

### A. Constitutional Mandate and State Protection

Article 1 implements Sections 1 and 2, Article XV of the 1987 Constitution, which declare that the State recognizes the Filipino family as the foundation of the nation and that marriage, as an inviolable social institution, is the foundation of the family and shall be protected by the State.[^24] [^25] [^26] [^27] In [*Leouel Santos v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/jan1995/gr_112019_1995.html) and [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319), the Supreme Court emphasized that these constitutional and statutory provisions express the basic nucleus of Philippine marriage law, under which marriage is not a mere adventure but a lifetime commitment.[^28] [^29]

Because the security and stability of the State depend upon the purity, continuity and permanence of marriage, the law surrounds the institution with substantive and procedural safeguards.[^30] [^31] As the Supreme Court declared in [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69576), *"Marriage, in turn, is the foundation of the family and an inviolable social institution, whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law."*[^32] The only *"third party"* allowed to be involved in contracts of marriage is the State.[^33]

### B. Marriage as a Special Contract and a New Relation (*Vinculum Juris*)

While ordinary contracts are governed primarily by the autonomy of the parties' wills, marriage is a *sui generis* "special contract."[^34] [^35] [^36] In [*De Silva v. De Silva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67852) and [*Alfonso v. Alfonso*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69994), the Supreme Court explained that marriage in this jurisdiction is not just a civil contract, but a new relation and an institution in the maintenance of which the public is deeply interested.[^37] [^38]

In every valid marriage lies the *vinculum juris*: the juridical bond that unites the spouses in a legally and morally binding union governed by law.[^39] This *vinculum juris* imposes upon each spouse the essential marital obligations of living together and observing mutual love, respect, fidelity and support under Article 68 of the Family Code.[^40] [^41] [^42] Moreover, as the Supreme Court highlighted in [*Quiogue, Jr. v. Quiogue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68704) and [*Republic v. Yeban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68228), marriage by its very definition under Article 1 is a binary and exclusive partnership involving only two persons, requiring the participation of both spouses and recognizing absolute faithfulness as the legal norm.[^43] [^44]

## 2. Elements of the Statutory Definition Under the First Sentence

### A. Special Contract Requiring Free and Genuine Consent

Because marriage is a special contract, consent freely given by both parties is indispensable to its existence and validity.[^45] [^46] The Supreme Court has drawn a sharp doctrinal boundary between a **totally simulated or fictitious marriage** (where consent and formal requisites are absent) and a **marriage entered into for a limited or ulterior motive** (where consent and requisites are present):

* **Totally Simulated Marriage Void *Ab Initio* (*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*):** In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), a Filipina and a Japanese national merely signed a blank marriage certificate at the Manila City Hall without a marriage license, without appearing before the solemnizing minister or witnesses in any marriage ceremony and with no intent to enter into marital relations, solely to facilitate the issuance of a Japanese visa.[^47] Citing Article 1, the Supreme Court declared the ostensible marriage void *ab initio*, holding that as a special contract, consent is by definition indispensable to marriage.[^48] Where no marriage ceremony was held, no license was issued and neither party ever intended to enter into a marital union, the marriage is completely fictitious. Insisting on its validity would lend judicial approbation to transactional fronts and work a greater affront to the institution of marriage.[^49]
* **Marriage for Citizenship or Money Not a Marriage in Jest (*Republic v. Albios*):** Conversely, in [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), a Filipina paid an American citizen USD 2,000.00 to marry her before a Metropolitan Trial Court judge in Mandaluyong City so she could acquire American citizenship, after which they never cohabited.[^50] The Supreme Court reversed the lower courts and upheld the validity of the marriage.[^51] Distinguishing the case from a "marriage in jest" (where the parties indulge in a mere dramatic play or jest with no intention of being bound), the Supreme Court ruled that both parties were personally aware of the consequences of their act and consciously consented to enter into a legally binding marital status in order to achieve their intended immigration effect.[^52] Although the avowed purpose of marriage under Article 1 is the establishment of conjugal and family life, the possibility that the parties might have no real intention to establish a life together is insufficient to nullify a marriage freely entered into with all essential and formal requisites present.[^53]
* **Consent Vitiated by Fraud (*Salva-Roldan v. Roldan* and *Republic v. Villacorta*):** When consent is present at the celebration of the marriage but was obtained through fraud under Article 45(3), the marriage is voidable rather than void *ab initio*.[^54] [^55] [^56] However, because marriage under Article 1 is a permanent union and an inviolable social institution governed by law, the Supreme Court held in [*Republic v. Villacorta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67585) that it may be terminated only for the limited grounds exclusively enumerated in Article 46 and fraudulent intent in bad faith must be established by clear and convincing evidence.[^57] Thus, a husband's post-wedding discovery that the three-year-old child born prior to their marriage was sired by another man does not fall under Article 46(2), which requires concealment of pregnancy by another man *at the time of the marriage*.[^58] Similarly, in [*Salva-Roldan v. Roldan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69917), the Supreme Court held that to annul a marriage under Article 45(3) in relation to Article 46(4) for concealment of homosexuality, the petitioner must prove by preponderance of evidence both that the spouse is homosexual and that he fraudulently concealed his homosexuality to obtain consent.[^59]

### B. Permanent Union ("Immutable" Consent)

Article 1 defines marriage as a contract of **permanent union**.[^60] In [*Malcampo-Sin v. Sin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50855), the Supreme Court explained that marriage is immutable, such that when both spouses give their consent to enter into it, their consent becomes irrevocable and remains unchanged even by their independent wills, unless the marriage is shown to be void *ab initio* or annulled on statutory grounds.[^61]

### C. "Between a Man and a Woman"

Article 1 explicitly defines marriage as a union *"between a man and a woman"*, which is reinforced by Article 2(1) requiring the contracting parties to be *"a male and a female"*.[^62] Two landmark decisions address the legal consequences and justiciability of this sex-specific statutory requirement:

* **Biological Sex Determined Visually at Birth (*Silverio v. Republic*):** In [*Silverio v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44532), a petitioner born biologically male underwent sex reassignment surgery in Thailand and petitioned the trial court to change his first name and to alter the sex entry in his birth certificate from "male" to "female" so that he could marry his male American fiancé.[^63] The Supreme Court denied the petition, holding that under the Civil Register Law, a person's sex is an essential, immutable fact determined visually and biologically at birth by the birth attendant and no law authorizes changing a birth certificate entry due to sex reassignment surgery.[^64] Citing Article 1 and Article 2(1) of the Family Code, the Supreme Court ruled that a person's sex is an essential requisite for marriage because marriage is a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman. Altering the civil registry to allow a post-operative male-to-female transsexual to marry another male would substantially reconfigure Philippine marriage and family laws without legislative authority.[^65]
* **Constitutional Challenge and Separation of Powers (*Falcis III v. Civil Registrar General*):** In [*Falcis III v. Civil Registrar General*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65744), an openly homosexual lawyer filed a direct petition for certiorari and prohibition before the Supreme Court (later joined by a petition-in-intervention) seeking to declare Articles 1 and 2 of the Family Code unconstitutional and to nullify Articles 46(4) and 55(6), without having applied for and been denied a marriage license.[^66] The Supreme Court dismissed both the petition and the petition-in-intervention for failing to satisfy the requisites of judicial review (lack of an actual case or controversy, lack of legal standing or direct injury-in-fact and violation of the hierarchy of courts).[^67] While observing that the plain text of Section 2, Article XV of the 1987 Constitution does not define marriage solely as between a man and a woman and does not bar Congress from enacting legislation recognizing same-sex unions, the Supreme Court stressed that Article 1 of the Family Code serves as the foundation of an extensive web of sex-specific civil, penal and labor statutes (governing surnames, artificial insemination, property administration, parental authority, adultery, concubinage and paternity leave).[^68] Consequently, the Supreme Court cannot impliedly amend this entire statutory architecture through a facial invalidation of Articles 1 and 2 in an abstract suit without arrogating legislative power unto itself and violating the separation of powers.[^69] The Supreme Court also found Falcis guilty of direct contempt for failing to observe the required decorum before the Court, which does not bear on the meaning of Article 1.[^70]

### D. "Entered Into in Accordance With Law": Civil vs. Religious Marriages and Formal Safeguards

Marriage under Article 1 must be *"entered into in accordance with law"*.[^71] Philippine jurisprudence establishes three core propositions from this requirement:

1. **Civil Jurisdiction Over Church-Solemnized Marriages (*Tilar v. Republic* and *Republic v. Manalo*):** In [*Tilar v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63283), a Regional Trial Court dismissed a husband's Article 36 nullity petition on the theory that because the marriage was solemnized in the Roman Catholic Church, marriage is an indissoluble religious sacrament beyond the reach of civil courts under the separation of Church and State.[^72] The Supreme Court reversed and remanded the case for trial on the merits.[^73] Quoting Article 1 of the Family Code, the Supreme Court held that although the Roman Catholic Church considers marriage a sacrament, marriage has civil and legal consequences that are governed by the Family Code.[^74] [^75] Because a petition for declaration of nullity under Article 36 assails the validity of the civil contract defined in Article 1 rather than the religious sacrament, Regional Trial Courts sitting as Family Courts possess exclusive original jurisdiction to adjudicate it.[^76]
2. **Purely Religious Ceremonies Not Entered Into in Accordance With Law (*Perfecto v. Judge Esidera*):** In [*Perfecto v. Judge Esidera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60744), a judge was administratively charged with bigamy and immorality for participating in a 1990 Catholic sacramental marriage rite (*matrimonia de conciencia*) with her second husband while her unconsummated 1987 civil marriage to her first husband was still pending judicial nullification, before eventually contracting a valid civil wedding in 1992 after the nullity decree.[^77] Citing Article 1, the Supreme Court held that the second or subsequent marriage contemplated in the crime of bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code is a marriage *"entered into in accordance with law"* that would have been valid were it not for the subsisting first marriage.[^78] [^79] Religious marriages are recognized by Philippine law only if they conform to civil legal requirements. Because the priest who officiated the 1990 religious rite had no civil license or authority to solemnize marriages, the ceremony had no legal effect and did not constitute a second marriage for purposes of bigamy.[^80] Nevertheless, the Supreme Court suspended the judge for one month for violating Canon 1 of the Code of Professional Responsibility, holding that benevolent neutrality and religious freedom cannot shield a judge who knowingly entered into a civil marriage from administrative liability for engaging in sexual relations with another man while her first civil marriage was still subsisting.[^81]
3. **Strict Observance of Statutory Requisites and Venue (*Niñal*, *Keuppers* and *Tormis*):** Because marriage is an inviolable social institution in which the State participates, its statutory requisites must be strictly observed:
   * **Marriage License and the Five-Year Cohabitation Exemption (*Niñal v. Bayadog*):** In [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319), a widower married his second wife without a marriage license only twenty months after shooting his first wife dead, executing an affidavit of five-year cohabitation.[^82] The Supreme Court declared the second marriage void *ab initio*, holding that the marriage license requirement is the State's demonstration of its involvement in every marriage.[^83] To qualify for the five-year cohabitation exemption (Article 76 of the Civil Code, now Article 34 of the Family Code), the five-year period immediately preceding the wedding must be characterized by both continuity and exclusivity and both parties must have been legally capacitated to marry each other throughout the entire five-year period, where the only missing factor was the special contract of marriage.[^84] [^85]
   * **Territorial Jurisdiction and Mandatory Venue of Solemnization (*Keuppers v. Judge Murcia*):** In [*Keuppers v. Judge Murcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64162), a Municipal Trial Court in Cities judge of the Island Garden City of Samal solemnized a marriage between a Filipina and a German national inside a travel agency office in Davao City, outside his territorial jurisdiction and without any statutory exception under Article 8, claiming he took pity on the couple because the fiancé was leaving for Germany.[^86] The Supreme Court found the judge guilty of grave misconduct and forfeited his retirement benefits, ruling that the limiting phrase *"and not elsewhere"* in Article 8 highlights the status of marriage under Article 1 as a special contract of permanent union and an inviolable social institution. Solemnizing a marriage outside one's territorial jurisdiction inside a travel agency office demeans and cheapens the institution of marriage.[^87] [^88]
   * **Prohibition Against Turning Solemnization Into a Business (*OCA v. Former Judge Tormis*):** In [*Office of the Court Administrator v. Former Judge Tormis*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62364), the Supreme Court disbarred a former Cebu City judge who connived with court employees acting as "fixers" to solemnize hundreds of marriages with missing or expired marriage licenses, fabricated Article 34 affidavits of cohabitation and foreign nationals submitting mere affidavits instead of certificates of legal capacity to marry under Article 21.[^89] Citing Article 1, the Supreme Court held that a judge and lawyer who dispenses with statutory safeguards for monetary gain makes a mockery of the inviolable social institution of marriage.[^90]

## 3. Institutional Nature and Non-Stipulation Rule Under the Second Sentence

### A. Ineffectiveness of Private Stipulations to Alter or Dissolve Marriage

Under the second sentence of Article 1, the *"nature, consequences, and incidents"* of marriage *"are governed by law and not subject to stipulation, except that marriage settlements may fix the property relations during the marriage within the limits provided by this Code."*[^91] Consequently, any private contract, compromise or notarized covenant attempting to alter the marital bond, waive marital fidelity, separate property extrajudicially during marriage or waive parental authority is void:

* **Notarized *Kasunduan* Granting Freedom to Cohabit with Others (*Acebedo v. Arquero*):** In [*Acebedo v. Arquero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48496), a Municipal Trial Court process server charged with immorality for cohabiting and having sexual relations with a married woman invoked as his defense a notarized *Kasunduan* executed by the woman and her husband, in which the spouses agreed to separate and granted each other the freedom to seek another life partner without court action.[^92] Citing Article 1, the Supreme Court rejected the defense and suspended the employee, holding that the *Kasunduan* had absolutely no force and effect on the validity of the marriage because marriage is an institution of public order and policy governed by rules established by law that cannot be made inoperative by the stipulation of the parties.[^93]
* **Agreement that Marriage is Only for Immigration (*Republic v. Albios*):** In [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the Supreme Court ruled that because the nature, consequences and incidents of marriage are governed by law and not subject to stipulation under Article 1, contracting parties who validly marry cannot stipulate that their marriage will have no legal effect or that they will be exempt from the rights and obligations of spouses.[^94]
* **Extrajudicial Separation and Property Settlement Agreement (*Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*):** In [*Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57326), a Filipino husband and his first Filipino wife executed an Agreement for Separation and Property Settlement in the Dominican Republic concurrently with a Dominican divorce decree.[^95] Citing Article 1 of the Family Code and Articles 15 and 17 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that both the foreign divorce between two Filipino citizens and their contractual agreement to dissolve and partition their conjugal partnership without competent judicial approval were void for being contrary to prohibitive laws and public policy.[^96] [^97] Even where spouses are permitted to fix their property relations in marriage settlements, such settlements must be executed *before* the celebration of the marriage and any modification must be made before the wedding and approved by the court in the instances allowed by law.[^98] [^99]
* **Legal Separation Compromise Cannot Waive Parental Authority (*Cang v. Court of Appeals*):** In [*Cang v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36249), a wife and her relatives argued that the natural father's written consent to the adoption of their three minor children was unnecessary because a compromise agreement approved in a prior legal separation case authorized the wife *"to enter into any contract or agreement with any person... without the written consent of the husband."*[^100] The Supreme Court reversed the decree of adoption, holding that the contractual authority granted to the wife in the legal separation agreement referred only to conjugal property transactions and could not be construed as a renunciation of parental authority, which is inalienable and cannot be waived or transferred by private stipulation except in cases expressly authorized by law.[^101]

### B. Prohibition of Absolute Divorce Between Filipino Citizens and the Article 26(2) Exception

Because marriage under Article 1 is a permanent union and an inviolable social institution, Philippine law does not allow absolute divorce between Filipino citizens.[^102] [^103] Under the nationality principle in Article 15 of the Civil Code, laws relating to family rights and duties, or to the status, condition and legal capacity of persons, are binding upon citizens of the Philippines even though living abroad and under Article 17(3) of the Civil Code, prohibitive laws concerning persons and public policy cannot be rendered ineffective by foreign judgments or conventions.[^104] [^105]

The Supreme Court has delineated the exact interplay between Article 1, the nationality rule and Article 26(2) of the Family Code across three situations:

1. **Foreign Divorce Between Two Filipino Citizens is Void (*Lavadia*):** Where both spouses are Filipino citizens at the time a divorce decree is obtained abroad, the foreign divorce is null and void in the Philippines as contrary to prohibitive laws and public policy under Articles 15 and 17 of the Civil Code.[^106] Thus, in [*Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57326), the Dominican Republic divorce obtained by two Filipino spouses did not dissolve their marriage, rendering the husband's subsequent marriage in the Dominican Republic bigamous and void *ab initio* and with the property relations of the second union governed by Article 144 of the Civil Code on co-ownership.[^107] Likewise, in [*Cang v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36249), a Nevada divorce obtained by a Filipino husband from his Filipino wife before he became a naturalized American citizen had no legal effect on the wife in the Philippines.[^108]
2. **Valid Foreign Divorce in a Mixed Marriage Capacitates the Filipino Spouse Regardless of Who Initiated It (*Republic v. Manalo*):** Under the second paragraph of Article 26 of the Family Code, where a marriage between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner is validly celebrated and a divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad capacitating the alien spouse to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall likewise have capacity to remarry under Philippine law.[^109] [^110] In the landmark case of [*Republic v. Manalo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64093), the Supreme Court held that although Article 1 governs the civil consequences of marriage and prohibits divorce between Filipinos, Article 26(2), construed in light of legislative intent and the constitutional guarantee of equal protection, applies whether the Filipino spouse was the respondent or the *petitioner* who initiated the foreign divorce proceeding against the Japanese spouse.[^111] Once a valid foreign divorce dissolves the marriage bond as to the alien spouse, the State can no longer bind the Filipino spouse alone to a marriage where the other party is no longer a spouse.[^112]
3. **Mandatory Proof of Both the Foreign Divorce Decree and the Governing Foreign Law (*Anido v. Republic* and *Republic v. Manalo*):** Philippine courts do not take judicial notice of foreign judgments or foreign laws. Both the foreign divorce decree and the pertinent foreign divorce law capacitating the alien spouse to remarry must be alleged and proven as facts in accordance with Sections 24 and 25, Rule 132 of the Rules of Court.[^113] [^114] In [*Anido v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69859), a Filipina married a Peruvian citizen and the Peruvian husband later obtained a divorce decree from a Kentucky court while residing in Kentucky, U.S.A.[^115] The Supreme Court clarified that under Article 26(2), the foreign law that must be proven is the law of the foreign state or domicile whose court issued the divorce decree (Kentucky law) rather than the national law of the alien spouse's country of origin (Peruvian law) and remanded the case to allow the Filipina petitioner to prove the relevant Kentucky statutes.[^116]

## 4. Procedural and Evidentiary Consequences of Inviolability

### A. Presumption of Validity (*Semper Praesumitur Pro Matrimonio*) and Burden of Proof

Because the Constitution and Article 1 of the Family Code cherish the validity of marriage and protect it from dissolution at the whim of the parties, every marriage enjoys a strong legal presumption of validity (*semper praesumitur pro matrimonio*).[^117] [^118] [^119] [^120] From this foundational premise flow three strict evidentiary rules:

1. **Burden of Proof on the Plaintiff and Resolution of Doubt in Favor of Marriage (*Molina*, *Paras*, *Cabantug-Baguio* and *Singson*):** In any action for declaration of nullity or annulment of marriage, the burden of proof (*onus probandi*) rests at all times upon the plaintiff and any doubt must be resolved in favor of the existence, continuation and validity of the marriage and against its dissolution and nullity.[^121] [^122] [^123] [^124] In [*Singson v. Singson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63850), the Supreme Court added that courts cannot take judicial notice of a petitioner's sweeping assertion that personality disorders are generally incurable.[^125]
2. **Quantum of Proof: Clear and Convincing Evidence Under Article 36 (*Tan-Andal*) and Preponderance of Evidence for Fraud Under Article 45(3) (*Salva-Roldan*):** In [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the Supreme Court En Banc established that precisely because marriage is an inviolable social institution under the Constitution and the Family Code that is presumed valid, a plaintiff in a nullity suit under Article 36 must prove psychological incapacity by **clear and convincing evidence**, a quantum of proof higher than preponderance of evidence but less than proof beyond reasonable doubt, to overcome the presumption of validity.[^126] By contrast, in [*Salva-Roldan v. Roldan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69917), the Supreme Court held that in a petition for annulment of marriage grounded on fraudulent concealment of homosexuality under Article 45(3) in relation to Article 46(4), the allegation of vitiated consent must be proven by **preponderance of evidence**.[^127]
3. **Disbarment Findings Not Conclusive of Psychological Incapacity (*Paras v. Paras*):** In [*Paras v. Paras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44139), a wife argued that the Supreme Court's factual findings in a prior disbarment decision, which suspended her lawyer-husband for forging her signature on bank loans, maintaining a concubine and abandoning the family, were conclusive proof of his psychological incapacity under Article 36.[^128] Quoting Article 1 of the Family Code, the Supreme Court denied the petition, holding that disciplinary proceedings against lawyers are *sui generis* and determine only fitness to remain a member of the Bar. Unfitness as a lawyer does not automatically establish that a husband suffered from a grave, incurable and pre-existing psychological incapacity at the inception of a marriage that started harmoniously and lasted nearly three decades.[^129]

### B. Mandatory Active Participation of the State and Prohibition of Collusion

To safeguard the inviolability of marriage under Article 1, Articles 48 and 60 of the Family Code forbid any judgment of annulment, declaration of absolute nullity or legal separation based upon a stipulation of facts or confession of judgment and require the public prosecutor and the Office of the Solicitor General to appear on behalf of the State to prevent collusion and ensure that evidence is not fabricated or suppressed.[^130] [^131] [^132] [^133]

* **Active State Participation Required Even When the Trial Court Dismisses the Petition (*Malcampo-Sin v. Sin*):** In [*Malcampo-Sin v. Sin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50855), the trial court and the Court of Appeals dismissed a wife's Article 36 nullity petition for insufficiency of evidence, even though throughout the proceedings the public prosecutor merely filed a one-page manifestation of non-collusion and never actively participated in the trial or appeal.[^134] The Supreme Court reversed and remanded the case for proper trial, holding that the dismissal of the petition did not cure the State's non-participation: *"The task of protecting marriage as an inviolable social institution requires vigilant and zealous participation and not mere pro-forma compliance. The protection of marriage as a sacred institution requires not just the defense of a true and genuine union but the exposure of an invalid one as well."*[^135]
* **Liberal Allowance of Appeal to Protect the Institution of Marriage (*Bolos v. Bolos*):** In [*Bolos v. Bolos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54656), the Supreme Court held that A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC (requiring a prior motion for reconsideration before appealing a nullity decree) applies by its plain text only to marriages celebrated during the effectivity of the Family Code on or after August 3, 1988 and not to a marriage celebrated in 1980.[^136] Quoting Article 1 verbatim, the Supreme Court emphasized that because what is at stake is the sacrosanct and inviolable institution of marriage, a spouse appealing a trial court decision declaring his marriage void should be given the fullest opportunity to establish the merits of his appeal rather than losing it on procedural technicalities.[^137]
* **Bar Against Simultaneous Legal Separation Petitions by Both Spouses (*Santos v. Santos*):** In [*Jorge Joaquin V. Santos v. Caroline H. Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70148), while a wife's first petition for legal separation against her husband was pending on appeal, the husband filed a second petition for legal separation against the wife based on her subsequent infidelity and attempt on his life.[^138] Citing Section 2, Article XV of the Constitution and Article 1 of the Family Code, the Supreme Court held that the State policy underpinning legal separation is the complete preservation of marriage as a social institution and foundation of the family, including the marital obligations suspended by a decree of legal separation.[^139] Consequently, two spouses cannot simultaneously ask for legal separation at the same time. While one spouse's legal separation case is pending trial, a subsequent legal separation petition filed by the respondent-spouse must henceforth be dismissed without prejudice for public policy reasons and any grounds existing at the time of the first suit must be raised as defenses under Article 56(4) (mutual guilt) or risk dismissal for collusion under Article 56(5).[^140] [^141]

### C. Necessity of Judicial Declaration of Nullity and Direct vs. Collateral Attack

Because marriage under Article 1 is an inviolable social institution whose consequences and incidents are governed by law and not subject to the parties' own perception, spouses cannot simply declare their own marriage void for purposes of remarriage:

* **Judicial Declaration as the Sole Basis for Remarriage and Incidental Property Liquidation (*Domingo v. Court of Appeals*):** In [*Domingo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_104818_1993.html), a husband who contracted a bigamous marriage moved to dismiss his second wife's petition for declaration of nullity of marriage and separation of property, arguing that under Article 40 of the Family Code a judicial declaration of nullity is required only if filed for purposes of remarriage.[^142] Quoting Article 1 of the Family Code, the Supreme Court denied the petition and ruled that the nullification of a marriage cannot be accomplished merely on the basis of the perception of the parties. Otherwise, this inviolable social institution would be reduced to a mockery.[^143] Interpreting Article 40, the Supreme Court held that the word *"solely"* qualifies *"final judgment declaring such previous marriage void"* rather than *"for purposes of remarriage"*, meaning a final judicial declaration of nullity is the sole acceptable basis for contracting a subsequent marriage, while a petition for declaration of absolute nullity may also be maintained for purposes other than remarriage, such as the liquidation, partition, distribution and separation of property between the erstwhile spouses in the same proceeding.[^144]
* **Direct or Collateral Attack by Heirs After a Spouse's Death (*Niñal v. Bayadog*):** In [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319), the Supreme Court distinguished voidable marriages from void marriages: whereas a voidable marriage under Articles 45 and 47 is valid until annulled, can be ratified by free cohabitation and can be assailed only in a direct proceeding by the spouses during their lifetime, a marriage that is void *ab initio* is deemed never to have taken place, cannot be ratified and is subject to an imprescriptible action or defense under Article 39.[^145] [^146] Therefore, other than for purposes of remarriage under Article 40, compulsory heirs of a deceased spouse have legal standing after their parent's death to file a direct petition to declare a void marriage inexistent, or to attack it collaterally in an estate, heirship or legitimacy proceeding, in order to protect their successional rights.[^147]

## 5. Limits of Inviolability: Psychological Incapacity, Domestic Violence and Spousal Privilege

### A. Inviolability Does Not Compel the Preservation of Void Marriages Under Article 36

The Supreme Court has repeatedly stressed that the constitutional and statutory mandate under Article 1 to protect marriage as an inviolable social institution applies to valid marriages and does not require courts to forcibly preserve a union that was void from the beginning due to psychological incapacity under Article 36:

* **Refusing to Dignify a Union Void from Inception (*Camacho-Reyes*, *De Silva*, *Yeban*, *Quiogue, Jr.*, *Alfonso* and *Ramoran-Wong*):** In [*Camacho-Reyes v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54276), the Supreme Court declared that a blind adherence to the exhortation that marriage is an inviolable social institution, by validating a void marriage despite convincing proof of a husband's antisocial personality disorder, chronic irresponsibility, substance abuse and unpaid debts, trenches upon the very reason why a marriage doomed from its inception should not be forcibly inflicted upon hapless partners for life.[^148] Similarly, in [*De Silva v. De Silva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67852), [*Republic v. Yeban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68228), [*Quiogue, Jr. v. Quiogue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68704) and [*Alfonso v. Alfonso*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69994), the Supreme Court explained that in declaring a marriage void *ab initio* under Article 36 where a spouse's durable personality structure prevented the *vinculum juris* from ever validly arising, the court is not demolishing the foundation of families, but is actually protecting the sanctity of marriage.[^149] [^150] [^151] [^152] As the Supreme Court synthesized in [*Republic v. Ramoran-Wong*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2026/feb2026/gr_276986_2026.html), while marriage is an inviolable social institution that the State is duty-bound to protect, *"Marriage, in its truest form, must be a sanctuary: a space of mutual respect, care, and emotional safety. It must never become a chain that binds a person to a relationship that is not only fundamentally flawed, but damaging."*[^153]
* **Either Spouse May Initiate an Article 36 Petition (*Clavecilla v. Clavecilla*):** In [*Clavecilla v. Clavecilla*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68752), quoting the deliberations of the Civil Code Revision Committee on the nature of marriage as a permanent union between man and woman, the Supreme Court held that the equitable doctrine of unclean hands does not bar a psychologically incapacitated spouse from filing a petition for declaration of nullity under Article 36, because a void marriage is inexistent from the very beginning and either party may petition the court to declare its non-existence.[^154]
* **Mere Refusal, Neglect, Abandonment or Incompatibility Excluded (*Santos*, *Molina*, *Paras* and *Cabantug-Baguio*):** Conversely, where the evidence shows only opposing or conflicting personalities, emotional immaturity, being a "mama's boy" or a spouse's post-wedding refusal, neglect or abandonment of marital duties without proof of a grave, juridically antecedent and legally incurable personality structure, Article 1's mandate of permanence and inviolability prevails and the petition for nullity must be dismissed.[^155] [^156] [^157] [^158]

### B. Inviolability Cannot Shield Intimate Partner Violence

The permanence and inviolability of marriage under Article 1 never shield criminal abuse within the family:

* **Psychological Violence Through Marital Infidelity Under R.A. No. 9262 (*XXX v. People*):** In [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69576), the Supreme Court affirmed a husband's conviction under Section 5(i) of Republic Act No. 9262 for causing mental and emotional anguish to his wife and children through marital infidelity, siring a child with his paramour and abandoning his family.[^159] Citing Article 1, the Supreme Court held that *"The State's commitments to upholding marriage as an inviolable social institution and to strengthening the solidarity of the family cannot be invoked to let intimate partner violence go unchecked."*[^160]

### C. Constitutional and Evidentiary Protection of the Marital Relationship Against State Surveillance

In [*In the Matter of Petition for Writ of Amparo of Vivian A. Sanchez (Sanchez v. Darroca)*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65970), police officers surreptitiously photographed and monitored a widow and her children at a funeral parlor where she identified the body of her estranged husband, a suspected member of the New People's Army killed in an armed encounter.[^161] Granting a Writ of Amparo in favor of the widow and her children, the Supreme Court cited Article 1 of the Family Code and held that in recognition of marriage as an inviolable social institution and the foundation of the family, the State grants spouses the marital disqualification and confidential marital communication privileges under Rule 130 of the Rules of Court to preserve domestic harmony and unfettered communication.[^162] These privileges, which survive even the death of a spouse as to confidential communications made during marriage, insulate a spouse and children from being targeted for warrantless police surveillance and threat of investigation solely by virtue of their marital and familial relationship to a suspected offender.[^163]

### D. Administrative Sanctions for Subverting the Sanctity of Marriage

Because the sanctity of marriage is constitutionally recognized and affirmed by Article 1 of the Family Code as a special contract of permanent union, the Supreme Court enforces strict secular morality standards upon lawyers, judges and public employees who subvert the marital bond:

* **Dalliances With Married Persons vs. Consensual Relations Between Unmarried Adults (*Radam*, *Mayor* and *Acebedo*):** In [*Anonymous v. Radam*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46428) and [*Concerned Employee v. Mayor*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46312), the Supreme Court drew a clear distinction under secular public policy:
  1. When an unmarried public employee gives birth out of wedlock to a child fathered by a man who is **himself unmarried**, she is not ordinarily liable for disgraceful and immoral conduct, because no statute penalizes consensual sexual relations between two unmarried adults and such conduct does not contravene any fundamental State policy.[^164] [^165]
  2. However, when the sexual partner is **married to another person**, engaging in extramarital relations constitutes disgraceful and immoral conduct because it deliberately subverts the institution of marriage protected by the Constitution and Article 1 of the Family Code.[^166] [^167] [^168] While a court employee's initial ignorance that her paramour was married may extenuate liability, continuing to engage in sexual congress after learning of his subsisting marriage manifests a willful subversion of the legal order warranting suspension (*Concerned Employee v. Mayor*).[^169]

## Related Provisions

* **Article XV, Sections 1 and 2 of the 1987 Constitution:** Enshrines the State policy recognizing the Filipino family as the foundation of the nation and protecting marriage as an inviolable social institution and the foundation of the family.[^184] [^185] [^186] [^187]
* **Articles 2, 3 and 4 of the Family Code (Essential and Formal Requisites):** Implement Article 1's requirement that marriage be entered into in accordance with law by prescribing the essential requisites (legal capacity of a male and a female and consent freely given before the solemnizing officer) and formal requisites (authority of the solemnizing officer, valid marriage license and marriage ceremony), the absence of any of which renders the marriage void *ab initio*.[^188] [^189] [^190] [^191] [^192]
* **Articles 7, 8, 21 and 34 of the Family Code (Solemnization Safeguards and License Exemptions):** Enforce the State's supervision over the celebration of marriage through territorial limits and mandatory public venues for solemnizing officers, certificates of legal capacity for foreign nationals and strict five-year exclusive cohabitation requirements for license-exempt marriages.[^193] [^194] [^195] [^196]
* **Article 26(2) of the Family Code and Articles 15 and 17 of the Civil Code (Nationality Rule and Foreign Divorce in Mixed Marriages):** Bind Filipino citizens to Philippine marriage laws prohibiting absolute divorce while recognizing valid foreign divorces obtained in mixed marriages between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner once the foreign decree and foreign law are duly proven.[^197] [^198] [^199] [^200] [^201]
* **Articles 35, 36, 37, 38, 39 and 40 of the Family Code (Void Marriages and Judicial Declaration of Nullity):** Define marriages that are inexistent from the beginning (where the *vinculum juris* never arose), establish the imprescriptibility of actions or defenses for absolute nullity and require a final judicial declaration of nullity as the sole basis for remarriage.[^202] [^203] [^204] [^205] [^206]
* **Articles 45, 46, 47 and 48 of the Family Code (Voidable Marriages and State Participation):** Govern marriages defective in consent at the time of celebration (including the exclusive enumeration of fraud under Article 46) and mandate active participation by the public prosecutor and the Solicitor General to prevent collusion.[^207] [^208] [^209] [^210]
* **Articles 55, 56 and 60 of the Family Code (Legal Separation):** Regulate the suspension of common marital life without severing the marital bond, barring decrees based on stipulation or collusion and prohibiting simultaneous petitions by both spouses.[^211] [^212]
* **Articles 68 to 71 of the Family Code (Rights and Obligations Between Husband and Wife):** Specify the legal incidents of the *vinculum juris*, the mutual duties to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, render mutual help and support and fix the family domicile, which cannot be waived by stipulation.[^213] [^214] [^215] [^216]
* **Articles 74 to 81, 147 and 148 of the Family Code (Marriage Settlements and Property Regimes of Void or Non-Marital Unions):** Operationalize the sole exception in the second sentence of Article 1 allowing future spouses to fix their property relations in written pre-marriage settlements within statutory limits, while governing property acquired under void marriages through Article 147 (capacitated parties) or Article 148 (incapacitated parties).[^217] [^218] [^219]

## Key Cases

* [***Republic v. Ramoran-Wong***](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2026/feb2026/gr_276986_2026.html) (G.R. No. 276986, February 3, 2026): Affirmed the declaration of nullity under Article 36 where the husband suffered from Mixed Personality Disorder (Narcissistic, Antisocial and Histrionic traits), holding that while marriage is an inviolable social institution under Article 1, it must be a sanctuary of mutual respect, care and emotional safety and must never become a chain binding a person to a fundamentally flawed and damaging union.[^220]
* [***Alfonso v. Alfonso***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69994) (G.R. No. 258705 [Formerly UDK No. 17095], July 16, 2025): Held that under Article 1, in every marriage lies the *vinculum juris* uniting the spouses in a legally and morally binding union and when one spouse was psychologically incapacitated to assume essential marital duties at the celebration of the marriage, the *vinculum juris* is deemed never to have validly arisen.[^221]
* [***Santos v. Santos***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70148) (G.R. No. 267675, May 20, 2025): Ruled that under the State policy in the Constitution and Article 1 of the Family Code preserving marriage and preventing collusion in legal separation cases, two spouses cannot simultaneously petition for legal separation. A subsequent legal separation petition filed by a respondent-spouse while the first case is pending must be dismissed without prejudice for public policy reasons.[^222]
* [***Salva-Roldan v. Roldan***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69917) (G.R. No. 268109, March 3, 2025): Held that as a special contract of permanent union under Article 1, marriage requires free consent and to annul a marriage for fraudulent concealment of homosexuality under Articles 45(3) and 46(4), both homosexuality and its fraudulent concealment must be proven by preponderance of evidence.[^223]
* [***Anido v. Republic***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69859) (G.R. No. 253527, October 21, 2024): Held that, under the nationality principle in Article 15 of the Civil Code and the exception in Article 26(2) of the Family Code, a Filipino spouse seeking recognition of a foreign divorce under Article 26(2) must prove both the divorce decree and the divorce law of the foreign state or domicile that issued the decree (Kentucky law, rather than the alien spouse's Peruvian national law).[^224]
* [***XXX v. People***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69576) (G.R. No. 252739, April 16, 2024): Held that under Article 1, the only third party allowed in a contract of marriage is the State and the State's commitment to upholding marriage as an inviolable social institution cannot be invoked to let intimate partner violence, including psychological violence through marital infidelity and abandonment under Section 5(i) of Republic Act No. 9262, go unchecked.[^225]
* [***Quiogue, Jr. v. Quiogue***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68704) (G.R. No. 203992, August 22, 2022): Held that under Article 1, the law recognizes absolute fidelity as the norm stemming from the nature of marriage as a special contract of an exclusive partnership between a man and a woman and declared a marriage void *ab initio* where the husband's chronic infidelity was rooted in a disordered personality structure antedating the wedding.[^226]
* [***Republic v. Villacorta***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67585) (G.R. No. 249953, June 23, 2021): Ruled that because marriage under Article 1 is a permanent union and an inviolable social institution governed by law, it may be annulled only on the exclusive grounds enumerated in Article 46. Pre-marital birth of a three-year-old child sired by another man does not constitute concealment of pregnancy at the time of marriage under Article 46(2).[^227]
* [***Tan-Andal v. Andal***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) (G.R. No. 196359, May 11, 2021): Reconfigured Article 36 doctrine and held that because marriage is an inviolable social institution protected by the Constitution and the Family Code and presumed valid, the petitioner must prove psychological incapacity by clear and convincing evidence.[^228]
* [***Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555) (G.R. No. 247576, March 15, 2021): Declared void *ab initio* a totally simulated marriage where the parties signed a blank marriage certificate at Manila City Hall without a marriage license, ceremony or intent to marry solely to obtain a Japanese visa, holding that consent is indispensable to marriage as a special contract under Article 1.[^229]
* [***Sanchez v. Darroca***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65970) (G.R. No. 242257, October 15, 2019): Granted a Writ of Amparo to a widow and her children subjected to police surveillance at her slain husband's wake, holding that in recognition of marriage as an inviolable social institution under Article 1, spousal testimonial and confidential communication privileges protect domestic harmony and bar warrantless police targeting of a spouse solely by virtue of the marital tie.[^230]
* [***Falcis III v. Civil Registrar General***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65744) (G.R. No. 217910, September 3, 2019): Dismissed a direct facial challenge seeking to declare Articles 1 and 2 of the Family Code unconstitutional for lack of actual case or controversy, lack of standing (no denial of a marriage license) and violation of hierarchy of courts and separation of powers, noting that Article 1 underpins a comprehensive network of gendered statutory provisions across Philippine law.[^231]
* [***Republic v. Manalo***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64093) (G.R. No. 221029, April 24, 2018): Held that while marriage has civil consequences governed by Article 1 and absolute divorce is prohibited between Filipino citizens, under Article 26(2) and equal protection a valid foreign divorce obtained abroad in a mixed marriage, whether initiated by the alien spouse or the Filipino spouse, capacitates the Filipino spouse to remarry, subject to proof of the foreign divorce decree and foreign law.[^232]
* [***Tilar v. Republic***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63283) (G.R. No. 214529, July 12, 2017): Reversed an RTC dismissal of a nullity petition involving a Catholic church wedding, holding that although marriage is a religious sacrament, its civil nature, consequences and incidents are governed by the Family Code under Article 1 and fall within the exclusive original jurisdiction of Regional Trial Courts.[^233]
* [***Perfecto v. Judge Esidera***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60744) (A.M. No. RTJ-15-2417 [Formerly known as OCA IPI No. 10-3466-RTJ], July 22, 2015): Held that under Article 1, a purely religious marriage rite (*matrimonia de conciencia*) officiated by a priest lacking civil authority is not a marriage *"entered into in accordance with law"* and does not constitute the second marriage element of bigamy, although engaging in extramarital sexual relations while a prior civil marriage subsists violates Canon 1 of the Code of Professional Responsibility.[^234]
* [***Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57326) (G.R. No. 171914, July 23, 2014): Held that under Article 1 of the Family Code and the nationality rule in Article 15 of the Civil Code, a foreign divorce and extrajudicial property settlement obtained in the Dominican Republic by two Filipino spouses are void and contrary to public policy.[^235]
* [***Republic v. Albios***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278) (G.R. No. 198780, October 16, 2013): Upheld the validity of a marriage contracted for USD 2,000.00 to acquire U.S. citizenship, holding that where free consent and all statutory requisites are present, the marriage is not a marriage in jest and under Article 1 its nature, consequences and incidents are governed by law and cannot be negated by private stipulation.[^236]
* [***Silverio v. Republic***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44532) (G.R. No. 174689, October 19, 2007): Denied a petition to change the name and birth certificate sex entry of a biological male who underwent sex reassignment surgery, holding that under Articles 1 and 2(1) of the Family Code the contracting parties to a marriage must be a man and a woman as visually and biologically determined at birth.[^237]
* [***Acebedo v. Arquero***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48496) (A.M. No. P-94-1054, March 11, 2003): Held that under Article 1, marriage is an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation, rendering void a notarized *Kasunduan* in which spouses agreed to separate and allowed each other to find new partners.[^238]
* [***Malcampo-Sin v. Sin***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50855) (G.R. No. 137590, March 26, 2001): Held that under Article 1 marriage is permanent and immutable once entered into and that protecting marriage under Article 48 requires the State's active and zealous participation, not mere *pro forma* compliance, even when the trial court dismisses the nullity petition.[^239]
* [***Niñal v. Bayadog***](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319) (G.R. No. 133778, March 14, 2000): Held that the five-year cohabitation exemption from a marriage license requires exclusivity and legal capacity to marry throughout the entire five-year period immediately preceding the marriage and that compulsory heirs may assail a void marriage directly or collaterally even after a spouse's death.[^240]
* [***Domingo v. Court of Appeals***](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_104818_1993.html) (G.R. No. 104818, September 17, 1993): Held that because marriage under Article 1 is an inviolable social institution governed by law, spouses cannot declare their own marriage void. Under Article 40, a final judgment is the sole basis for remarriage, while a petition for declaration of nullity may also be filed together with the liquidation and separation of property.[^241]

## Suggested Answers

**Q1: Hypothetical: Assume that F, a Filipino citizen, paid A, an American citizen, USD 2,000.00 to marry her before a Metropolitan Trial Court judge in Mandaluyong City solely so that F could acquire U.S. citizenship. Assume further that they complied with all formal requisites and personally declared before the judge that they took each other as husband and wife, but orally agreed prior to the wedding that they would never live together as husband and wife and never consummated the marriage. F later files a petition for declaration of nullity of marriage, arguing that their marriage was a "marriage in jest" lacking genuine consent to establish conjugal and family life under Article 1 of the Family Code. Should the petition be granted?**

No. In [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the Supreme Court held that a marriage contracted for the limited purpose of acquiring American citizenship in exchange for USD 2,000.00 is valid and is not a marriage in jest, because both contracting parties were personally aware of the consequences of their act and consciously gave their consent to create a legally binding marital status in order to achieve its intended immigration effect.[^242] While the avowed purpose of marriage under Article 1 of the Family Code is the establishment of conjugal and family life, the second sentence of Article 1 expressly ordains that the nature, consequences and incidents of marriage are governed by law and not subject to stipulation.[^243] Consequently, the parties cannot stipulate that their marriage will be inoperative or exempt from legal obligations, nor may a marriage be declared void on grounds not provided by law.[^244] (By contrast, in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), where the parties merely signed a blank marriage certificate at City Hall without a marriage license, without any marriage ceremony or witnesses and without any intent to marry, the marriage was declared void *ab initio* for total absence of consent and formal requisites.)[^245]

**Q2: Hypothetical: Assume that H and W, both Filipino citizens, executed a notarized *Kasunduan* stating that because they could no longer get along, they freely agreed to separate as husband and wife and granted each other complete freedom to cohabit with any new partner without filing any court action against each other. Relying on this notarized *Kasunduan*, P, an unmarried court process server, entered into an eight-month sexual relationship with W. When H filed an administrative complaint for immorality against P, P invoked the notarized *Kasunduan* as a defense. Is P's defense tenable?**

No. In [*Acebedo v. Arquero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48496), the Supreme Court held that a notarized *Kasunduan* executed by spouses consenting to separate and granting each other freedom to seek another partner has absolutely no force and effect on the validity or incidents of their marriage.[^246] Under Article 1 of the Family Code, marriage is an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation.[^247] Because marriage is an institution of public order and policy governed by statutory rules that cannot be rendered inoperative by private agreement of the parties, P's sexual liaison with a married woman constitutes disgraceful and immoral conduct warranting administrative suspension.[^248]

**Q3: Hypothetical: Assume that H and W were married in a Roman Catholic Church ceremony in Leyte. Years later, H filed a petition before the Regional Trial Court sitting as a Family Court to declare their marriage void *ab initio* on the ground of psychological incapacity under Article 36 of the Family Code. The Regional Trial Court *motu proprio* dismissed the petition for lack of jurisdiction, reasoning that because the marriage was solemnized in the Roman Catholic Church, marriage is an indissoluble religious sacrament and civil courts cannot annul or dissolve it without violating the constitutional separation of Church and State. Did the trial court rule correctly?**

No. In [*Tilar v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63283), the Supreme Court reversed an identical dismissal and held that although the Roman Catholic Church considers marriage a sacrament, marriage has civil and legal consequences that are governed by the Family Code.[^249] Under Article 1 of the Family Code, marriage is a special contract of permanent union entered into in accordance with law and an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences and incidents are governed by law.[^250] Because a petition for declaration of nullity under Article 36 assails the validity of the civil marriage contract defined in Article 1 rather than the religious sacrament, Regional Trial Courts have exclusive original jurisdiction to adjudicate the petition without infringing the principle of separation of Church and State.[^251]

# Quotes

> "Prefatorily, the State recognizes marriage as the foundation of the family and an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation except otherwise provided by law. Additionally, marriage in this jurisdiction is not just a civil contract, but a new relation, an institution the maintenance of which the public is deeply interested. As such, it is incumbent upon the State to provide utmost protection and preservation to uphold the sanctity of marriage."[^252]

> "As recognized under Article 1 of the Family Code, in every marriage lies the vinculum juris – the juridical bond that unites the spouses in a legally and morally binding union governed by law. This vinculum juris imposes upon each spouse the essential marital obligations of mutual love, respect, fidelity, and support. When it becomes manifest that one party is psychologically incapacitated to assume these duties, the very foundation of that bond is rendered void."[^253]

> "Marriage, in its truest form, must be a sanctuary: a space of mutual respect, care, and emotional safety. It must never become a chain that binds a person to a relationship that is not only fundamentally flawed, but damaging. While the ideal remains that individuals choose wisely in love and commitment, the reality is that human beings make mistakes. When a marriage has become irreparably broken, the spouses so deeply incompatible as to render its continued existence unjust, the Court should not stand in the way."[^254]

> "We take this opportunity to issue a reminder that the family is the basic social unit of the community. Marriage, in turn, is the foundation of the family and an inviolable social institution, whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law. The only "third party" allowed to be involved in contracts of marriage is the State."[^255]

> "The State's commitments to upholding marriage as an inviolable social institution and to strengthening the solidarity of the family cannot be invoked to let intimate partner violence go unchecked."[^256]

> "Clearly, the law itself recognizes fidelity as the norm and a spouse should not be made to settle for anything less than absolute faithfulness from the other. This stems from the nature of marriage being a "special contract" of an exclusive partnership between a man and a woman."[^257]

> "As a special contract, consent is, by definition, indispensable to marriage."[^258]

> "Marriage is a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman entered into in accordance with law for the establishment of conjugal and family life. To be valid, consent must be freely given by both parties. Thus, a marriage may be annulled when consent was obtained by fraud."[^259]

> "It bears emphasis that "[m]arriage is a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman entered into in accordance with law for the establishment of conjugal and family life. It is the foundation of the family and an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation x x x." In view thereof, it may be terminated only for limited grounds as prescribed by law."[^260]

> "The avowed purpose of marriage under Article 1 of the Family Code is for the couple to establish a conjugal and family life. The possibility that the parties in a marriage might have no real intention to establish a life together is, however, insufficient to nullify a marriage freely entered into in accordance with law. The same Article 1 provides that the nature, consequences, and incidents of marriage are governed by law and not subject to stipulation. A marriage may, thus, only be declared void or voidable under the grounds provided by law. There is no law that declares a marriage void if it is entered into for purposes other than what the Constitution or law declares, such as the acquisition of foreign citizenship. Therefore, so long as all the essential and formal requisites precribed by law are present, and it is not void or voidable under the grounds provided by law, it shall be declared valid."[^261]

> "Articles 1 and 2 of the Family Code provide a definition and spell out basic requisites, respectively. Without passing upon the validity of the definition under Article 1, this Court nonetheless observes that this definition serves as the foundation of many other gendered provisions of the Family Code and other laws."[^262]

> "Being an employee of the judiciary, respondent ought to have known that the Kasunduan had absolutely no force and effect on the validity of the marriage between complainant and his wife. Article 1 of the Family Code provides that marriage is “an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation.” It is an institution of public order or policy, governed by rules established by law which cannot be made inoperative by the stipulation of the parties."[^263]

> "The Family Code emphasizes the permanent nature of marriage, hailing it as the foundation of the family. It is this inviolability which is central to our traditional and religious concepts of morality and provides the very bedrock on which our society finds stability. Marriage is immutable and when both spouses give their consent to enter it, their consent becomes irrevocable, unchanged even by their independent wills."[^264]

> "As a matter of policy, therefore, the nullification of a marriage for the purpose of contracting another cannot be accomplished merely on the basis of the perception of both parties or of one that their union is so defective with respect to the essential requisites of a contract of marriage as to render it void ipso jure and with no legal effect — and nothing more. Were this so, this inviolable social institution would be reduced to a mockery and would rest on very shaky foundations indeed. And the grounds for nullifying marriage would be as diverse and far-ranging as human ingenuity and fancy could conceive. For such a social significant institution, an official state pronouncement through the courts, and nothing less, will satisfy the exacting norms of society. Not only would such an open and public declaration by the courts definitively confirm the nullity of the contract of marriage, but the same would be easily verifiable through records accessible to everyone."[^265]

# Tips

## 1. Distinguishing Simulated Marriages from Ulterior-Motive Marriages

> [!TIP]
> **Audit Formal Requisites and Intent to Create Legal Status (*Ado-an-Morimoto* vs. *Albios*):**
> When evaluating a "fixed" or convenience marriage contracted for immigration or visa purposes, do not argue simply that the spouses lacked the intention to live together as husband and wife or never consummated the union. Under *Republic v. Albios*, if the parties appeared before a solemnizing officer with a valid marriage license and consciously consented to acquire the legal status of marriage in exchange for money or citizenship, the marriage is valid because Article 1 bars parties from stipulating away the legal consequences of marriage. To succeed in declaring a convenience marriage void *ab initio* under *Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*, focus the evidence on the **absence of essential and formal requisites**: prove that no marriage license was issued, no marriage ceremony actually took place before the solemnizing officer and two witnesses and the parties merely signed a blank marriage certificate as a fictitious paper transaction.

## 2. Pleading and Proving Foreign Divorces in Mixed Marriages

> [!NOTE]
> **Prove the Law of the Foreign Forum That Issued the Divorce Decree (*Lavadia*, *Manalo* and *Anido*):**
> Before filing a petition for judicial recognition of a foreign divorce decree, verify two threshold requirements:
> 1. **Citizenship of the Spouses at the Time of Divorce:** Confirm that at least one spouse was a foreign citizen when the divorce was obtained. If both spouses were Filipino citizens at the time of the foreign divorce, the decree is void under Article 1 of the Family Code and Article 15 of the Civil Code (*Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*). If one spouse was a foreigner, the divorce may be recognized under Article 26(2) even if the Filipino spouse initiated the foreign divorce proceeding (*Republic v. Manalo*).
> 2. **Proof of the Issuing Forum's Divorce Law:** Under *Anido v. Republic*, when an alien spouse obtains a divorce in a third state or domicile (such as a Peruvian citizen obtaining a divorce in Kentucky, U.S.A.), do not present only the national law of the alien's country of origin; you must plead and prove in accordance with Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25 both the foreign divorce decree and the marriage/divorce law of the **specific foreign state or jurisdiction that granted the divorce** showing that it capacitated the alien spouse to remarry.

## 3. Drafting Agreements and Handling Parallel Matrimonial Suits

> [!TIP]
> **Never Rely on Extrajudicial Separation Covenants (*Acebedo*, *Lavadia* and *Cang*):**
> Because the second sentence of Article 1 prohibits stipulations on the nature, consequences and incidents of marriage (except pre-marriage property settlements), advise clients that any notarized *Kasunduan* or extrajudicial agreement between spouses to live separately, allow new partners, partition conjugal assets without court approval or waive parental authority is void *ab initio*. Relying on such an agreement will not shield a party or paramour from administrative discipline for immorality (*Acebedo v. Arquero*), will not dissolve the conjugal partnership (*Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*) and will not dispense with a parent's written consent to adoption (*Cang v. Court of Appeals*).

> [!TIP]
> **Consolidate Defenses Instead of Filing a Counter-Petition for Legal Separation (*Santos v. Santos*):**
> If a client is sued for legal separation and has independent grounds for legal separation against the petitioner-spouse, do **not** file a separate, simultaneous petition for legal separation while the first case is pending. Under *Santos v. Santos*, a second legal separation petition filed by the respondent-spouse during the pendency of the first case will be dismissed without prejudice for violating public policy under Article 1. Instead, plead all existing grounds for legal separation against the petitioner-spouse in the Answer in the first case to establish mutual guilt under Article 56(4) and avoid a finding of collusion under Article 56(5).
