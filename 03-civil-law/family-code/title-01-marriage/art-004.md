> Art. 4. The absence of any of the essential or formal requisites shall render the marriage void ab initio, except as stated in Article 35 (2).
>
> A defect in any of the essential requisites shall render the marriage voidable as provided in Article 45.
>
> An irregularity in the formal requisites shall not affect the validity of the marriage but the party or parties responsible for the irregularity shall be civilly, criminally and administratively liable. (n)

[^family-code]

# Explanation

## Statutory Architecture and the Tripartite Classification of Deficiencies

Article 4 of the Family Code (Executive Order No. 209) is a new statutory synthesis marked `(n)` in the enactment text that assigns distinct legal consequences to every deficiency in the essential requisites under Article 2 (legal capacity of a male and a female and consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer) and the formal requisites under Article 3 (authority of the solemnizing officer, a valid marriage license unless exempt under Chapter 2 of Title I and a marriage ceremony before the solemnizing officer and at least two witnesses of legal age).[^2] Its three paragraphs draw a bright line among total **absence**, substantive **defect** and formal **irregularity**.

First, the **absence** of any essential or formal requisite renders the marriage void *ab initio*, subject to the single express statutory exception in Article 35(2) where either or both parties believed in good faith that an unauthorized solemnizing officer had legal authority.[^3] Second, a **defect** in any essential requisite renders the marriage merely voidable under Article 45.[^4] Third, an **irregularity** in the formal requisites leaves the validity of the marriage intact, while exposing the party or parties responsible for the irregularity to civil, criminal and administrative liability.[^5] Because marriage is an inviolable social institution and the foundation of the family protected by the Constitution, a union entered into in compliance with all essential and formal requisites cannot be declared void or voidable except on the grounds expressly provided by law.[^6]

## Absence of Essential or Formal Requisites (Paragraph 1)

### Absence of Essential Consent, Marriages in Jest and Limited-Purpose Marriages

Under Article 2(2) in relation to the first paragraph of Article 4, freely given consent in the presence of the solemnizing officer is an essential requisite whose absence renders the marriage void *ab initio*.[^16] In [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the Supreme Court drew a sharp doctrinal line between three situations involving consent. Valid consent must be real (not vitiated by vices of consent under Articles 45 and 46) and conscious or intelligent (understanding the nature and legal consequences of marriage). A **marriage in jest** is a pretended ceremony entered into as a joke with zero intention of entering the actual marriage status or creating any legal ties whatsoever, which is void *ab initio* for a complete absence of genuine consent.[^17]

By contrast, a **limited-purpose or ulterior-motive marriage** is valid when the contracting parties willingly and intelligently intend to create a real legal marital bond so as to achieve a collateral benefit. In [*Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), Liberty Albios and American citizen Daniel Lee Fringer contracted marriage before a Metropolitan Trial Court judge so that Albios could apply for United States citizenship in exchange for 2,000 U.S. dollars. Reversing the trial court and the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court dismissed the nullity petition because both parties consciously intended to enter a valid marriage that could legally support an immigration petition. The Supreme Court explained that the possibility of having no real intention to establish a life together is insufficient to nullify a marriage freely entered into in accordance with law, because Article 1 provides that the nature, consequences and incidents of marriage are governed by law.[^18]

A totally simulated transaction where no genuine ceremony and no consent ever occurred stands on entirely different footing. In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), the petitioner met respondent Yoshio Morimoto at Manila City Hall on December 5, 2007 and signed a blank marriage certificate to simulate a marriage that would help her obtain a Japanese visa. The solemnizing officer assured them that the certificate would never be registered. She later found a registered San Juan marriage certificate that cited a license number the San Juan Civil Registrar had no record of. The Supreme Court compared the case to *People v. Santiago* (as cited in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555)) and said it was worse because no ceremony took place. The Supreme Court declared the registered marriage null and void *ab initio* because there was no consent to marry, no actual marriage ceremony and no marriage license.[^19]

### Absence of Marriage Ceremony and Authorized Solemnizing Officer

Articles 3(1), 3(3) and 6 require the personal appearance of the contracting parties before an authorized solemnizing officer under Article 7 and their mutual declaration that they take each other as husband and wife in the presence of at least two witnesses of legal age. When parties merely sign a marriage contract privately without appearing before an authorized solemnizing officer in a marriage ceremony, no marriage exists in the eyes of the law.[^20]

In [*Morigo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464), Lucio Morigo and Lucia Barrete signed a marriage contract in 1990 without any solemnizing officer or actual marriage ceremony. Reversing his bigamy conviction after a trial court later declared that first union void *ab initio*, the Supreme Court held that the mere private act of signing a marriage contract bore no semblance to a valid marriage and needed no judicial declaration of nullity. The nullity court had found that no solemnizing officer officiated and had applied Articles 3 and 4.[^21] Likewise, in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), defense witnesses testified that no Municipal Mayor attended the alleged second wedding at the woman's residence and that a municipal civil registrar merely asked whether the parties accepted each other and made them sign the marriage certificate with the expectation that the Mayor would sign later. The Supreme Court found that the second marriage lacked the essential and formal requisites. It set aside the bigamy conviction on the ground of reasonable doubt and convicted the accused under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code instead.[^22]

### Absence of a Valid Marriage License and False Article 34 Affidavits

Under Articles 3(2), 4 and 35(3), a marriage solemnized without a valid marriage license is void *ab initio* unless it falls strictly within the exceptional marriages in Chapter 2, Title I (Articles 27 to 34).[^23] Because Chapter 2 exemptions depart from the general license requirement, the Supreme Court construes them strictly and holds that any marriage outside those narrow conditions requires a valid license.[^24] [^25]

The jurisprudence establishes recurring scenarios where absence of a license voids the marriage under Article 4:

- **Spurious or mismatched marriage license numbers issued to another couple.** In [*Abbas v. Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503), the marriage contract listed a Carmona, Cavite marriage license number that the Municipal Civil Registrar certified had actually been issued to a different couple, Arlindo Getalado and Myra Mabilangan, days after the wedding.[^26] Similarly, in [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964), the Quezon City Civil Registry certified that the license number written in the parties' marriage contract had been issued to another couple, Mamerto Yambao and Amelia Parado, supported by copies of the latter couple's license application and receipts.[^27] In both cases, the Supreme Court declared the marriages void *ab initio* under Articles 3, 4 and 35(3).[^28] [^29]
- **Unrecorded or fabricated licenses.** In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), the San Juan Civil Registrar certified that no record existed of the supposed marriage license number, corroborated by a Philippine Statistics Authority certification admitting it had mistakenly certified that a marriage occurred, rendering the union void *ab initio*.[^30] In [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376), an Imus Civil Registrar certification that the office's intact year-2000 archives contained no application for a marriage license by the accused and her first partner raised reasonable doubt that she had a valid prior marriage, which is an element of bigamy.[^31] The same principle governed a 1972 marriage in [*Kho v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61927), although the Supreme Court carefully applied Articles 53(4), 58 and 80(3) of the Civil Code (the statute in force in 1972) rather than Article 4 of the Family Code invoked by the trial court, reversing the appellate ruling that omission of a license entry on the marriage certificate was a mere defect.[^32]
- **False or unsupported Article 34 affidavits of five-year cohabitation.** Under Article 34, a marriage license is unnecessary only when a man and a woman have lived together continuously and exclusively as husband and wife for at least five years before the marriage without any legal impediment and execute a sworn affidavit to that effect.[^33] In [*De Castro v. Assidao-De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44838), sweethearts who began sexual relations only in October 1994 executed a false affidavit on March 13, 1995 claiming five years' cohabitation after their earlier license application expired.[^34] In [*Bounsit-Torralba v. Torralba*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68706), the parties became sweethearts in December 1995 and married in January 1996 without a license by checking the Article 34 box on the marriage certificate without executing any affidavit.[^35] In [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), the couple signed a 2009 Article 34 affidavit claiming cohabitation since 2003 even though they had only dated exclusively and intermittently stayed in the same place while the husband worked in Israel from May 24, 2006 to May 3, 2009.[^36] The Supreme Court held the marriage void in each case. In [*De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44838), it held that the falsity of the affidavit is not a mere irregularity and called the affidavit "a mere scrap of paper."[^37] In [*Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), relying on *Diaz-Salgado v. Anson* (as cited in [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449)), which cited *Republic v. Dayot*, it held that a false affidavit of cohabitation voids the marriage because the defect is the absence of a license and not an irregularity in one.[^38] In [*Bounsit-Torralba*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68706), there was no affidavit at all. The Supreme Court reiterated *Dayot*'s warning (as cited in [*Bounsit-Torralba v. Torralba*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68706)) that marrying without a license invites fraud against innocent and unwary parties.[^39] By contrast, [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449) distinguished *Laguda v. Manguardia* (as cited in [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449)), where the petitioner gave an inconsistent account and there was proof that the husband regularly returned home and stayed with his wife whenever possible.[^40] Under the Civil Code counterpart in Article 76, [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319) likewise held that the five-year cohabitation period must be continuous, exclusive and characterized by full legal capacity to marry each other throughout the entire five-year period.[^41]
- **Marriages performed with underage Article 34 parties or blatantly irregular licenses.** In [*Office of the Court Administrator v. Judge Necessario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55788) and [*Cosca v. Judge Palaypayon, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/am_mtj-92-721_1994.html), the Supreme Court *En Banc* disciplined solemnizing judges. In [*Cosca*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/am_mtj-92-721_1994.html), the record showed a groom aged 18 years and 2 months who claimed almost six years of cohabitation. The judge was fined for solemnizing marriages without a license. In [*Necessario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55788), the Supreme Court found that judges solemnized Article 34 marriages although the joint affidavits showed that one or both parties were minors during the claimed cohabitation.[^42] [^43]

### Incurability by Ceremony, Good Faith, or Unclean Hands

When an essential or formal requisite is absent under the first paragraph of Article 4, subsequent or collateral circumstances cannot supply the missing statutory element. In [*Abbas v. Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503), the Court of Appeals refused to annul the marriage because photographs showed a wedding ceremony, the parties signed the marriage contract, they lived together as spouses, had a child and the husband allegedly sued only to evade a bigamy charge. The Supreme Court reversed and reinstated the decree of nullity, holding that conducting a wedding ceremony, signing a marriage contract, cohabiting and even harboring an impure litigation motive cannot cure the absence of a valid marriage license.[^44]

In [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964), the Office of the Solicitor General invoked the equitable doctrine of unclean hands because the wife admitted she and her husband never personally applied for a marriage license and relied on a family friend to arrange the documentary requirements. The Supreme Court rejected that defense, holding that in petitions for absolute nullity based on lack of a marriage license, a petitioner's admission of acquiescing in a spurious license does not preclude a decree of nullity. Any responsibility for the underlying irregularity must be determined in a separate proceeding rather than used to validate a marriage that Article 4 and Article 35(3) declare void *ab initio*.[^45]

## The Statutory Exception: Good Faith Belief in Solemnizing Officer's Authority Under Article 35(2)

The first paragraph of Article 4 expressly qualifies its nullity rule with the clause saving marriages covered by Article 35(2). Under Article 35(2), a marriage solemnized by a person not legally authorized to perform marriages is void from the beginning **unless** it was contracted with either or both parties believing in good faith that the solemnizing officer had the legal authority to do so.[^46]

The Supreme Court applied this interplay between Article 4 and Article 35(2) in [*Maliwat-Melad v. Melad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69969). There, the marriage contract reflected that a 1990 wedding at the Tarlac Municipal Hall was solemnized by Judge Conrado De Gracia, an incumbent judge empowered under Article 7(1). In 2018, the wife petitioned for nullity after her lawyer examined wedding photographs and claimed that a Rotary Club member named Rosalio Florendo, not Judge De Gracia, appeared to be officiating. The Supreme Court first held that the wife failed to overcome the presumption of regularity of the marriage contract and the presumption of the solemnizing officer's authority by clear and convincing evidence. Second, applying Article 4 in relation to Article 35(2), the Supreme Court held that even assuming *arguendo* that an unauthorized person officiated, the marriage remained valid because the wife genuinely believed in good faith from 1990 until 2017 that Judge De Gracia had solemnized her marriage.[^47]

Good faith under Article 35(2) turns on a genuine belief that the solemnizing officer had legal authority.[^48] [*Morigo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464) and [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555) did not decide a good faith question under Article 35(2).[^49] [^50] In [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), the Supreme Court said that the general rule is nullity when the solemnizing officer lacks authority. It added that the good faith exception "has not been shown to exist" in that case.[^51] Outside Article 35(2), Article 4 recognizes no good faith exception to the absence of a marriage license or other essential or formal requisites.[^52]

## Defect in Essential Requisites and Voidable Marriages (Paragraph 2)

Under the second paragraph of Article 4, a **defect** in any of the essential requisites renders the marriage **voidable** as provided in Article 45. In [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the Supreme Court clarified the boundary between an **absence** of consent under Article 4 (paragraph 1) and a **defect** of consent under Article 4 (paragraph 2) in relation to Articles 45 and 46. Consent is defective when it is vitiated by a vice of consent under Articles 45 and 46, such as fraud, force, intimidation or undue influence. In [*Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the Supreme Court held that mutual collusion to marry for U.S. immigration citizenship did not constitute fraud under Article 45(3) and Article 46 (which is an exclusive list and may be invoked only by the injured or innocent party under Article 47(3), whereas both spouses conspired).[^53]

In [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319) and, for the first and fourth points, [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) (quoting *Pulido v. People*), the Supreme Court underscored the sharp legal consequences separating voidable marriages (Article 4, paragraph 2) from void marriages (Article 4, paragraph 1):[^54] [^55]

1. A voidable marriage is considered **valid and subsisting** until annulled by a final judicial decree, whereas a void *ab initio* marriage is considered never to have taken place.[^56] [^57]
2. A voidable marriage may generally be ratified or confirmed by free cohabitation or extinguished by prescription, whereas a void marriage can never be ratified and the action or defense is imprescriptible.[^58]
3. A voidable marriage cannot be attacked collaterally and can be assailed only in a direct proceeding brought by the proper party during the lifetime of both spouses, whereas a void marriage may be assailed directly or collaterally even after death by any proper interested party.[^59] [^60]
4. Because a voidable prior marriage remains valid and subsisting at the celebration of a subsequent union, an accused charged with bigamy cannot interpose a subsequent decree of annulment as a defense, unlike a void *ab initio* marriage (*Pulido v. People*, as quoted in [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376)).[^61]

The curated decisions in this corpus do not adjudicate annulment petitions grounded on physical incapacity or incurable sexually transmissible disease under Articles 45(5) and 45(6).[^62]

## Irregularities in Formal Requisites and Officer Liability (Paragraph 3)

Under the third paragraph of Article 4, an **irregularity** in the formal requisites does not affect the validity of the marriage, but the party or parties responsible for the irregularity shall be civilly, criminally and administratively liable. The Supreme Court's administrative and civil jurisprudence cleanly separates non-invalidating formal irregularities from fatal absences of formal requisites:

- **Solemnizing a marriage outside a judge's territorial jurisdiction without an Article 8 exception.** Under Article 7(1), an incumbent judge is authorized to solemnize marriages within the court's jurisdiction, while Article 8 directs where ceremonies must be held (courtroom or chambers unless *articulo mortis*, remote place under Article 29 or sworn written request of both parties). In [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979), an MCTC judge for Sta. Monica-Burgos solemnized a marriage at his residence in Dapa (40 to 45 kilometers away) based on a written request signed by only one party. The Supreme Court distinguished the **directory** venue rule of Article 8 from territorial judicial authority under Article 7(1): when a judge appointed to a specific jurisdiction solemnizes a wedding outside that area, there is a resultant irregularity in the formal requisite under Article 3 that, under Article 4 (cited in footnote 5 of [*Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979)), **does not affect the validity of the marriage**, yet subjects the officiating judge to administrative liability. Judge Domagtoy was suspended for six months for gross ignorance of the law (having also solemnized a second bigamous wedding on a mere joint affidavit of absence rather than an Article 41 judicial declaration of presumptive death).[^63] Following [*Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979), the Supreme Court in [*Beso v. Judge Daguman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903) fined an MCTC judge of Sta. Margarita-Tarangnan-Pagsanjan P5,000.00 for solemnizing a marriage at his Calbayog City residence without an Article 8 ground and for negligently failing to retain a copy and register the marriage certificate under Article 23.[^64]
- **Failure to date, sign, furnish or register marriage certificates under Article 23.** In [*Cosca v. Judge Palaypayon, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/am_mtj-92-721_1994.html), the Supreme Court *En Banc* rejected a judge's claim that an undated, unfiled and unfurnished marriage contract meant no marriage occurred, holding (citing *Jones v. Hortiguela*, as cited in [*Cosca v. Judge Palaypayon, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/am_mtj-92-721_1994.html)) that failing to furnish a party a copy of the marriage certificate or failing to register it does not invalidate the marriage, though it violates Article 23.[^65] Conversely, Judge Palaypayon's act of solemnizing marriages **without a marriage license** was not excused by omitting his signature or date, so under Article 4(3) he incurred administrative liability (fined P20,000.00, without prejudice to criminal liability under the Revised Penal Code provision on illegal marriage ceremonies, which refers to the Marriage Law, as well as civil liability to affected parties).[^66]
- **Issuance of an existing marriage license outside the parties' residence, imperfect publication or clerical numbering error.** In [*Alcantara v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44273) (deciding a 1982 marriage governed substantively by the Civil Code while articulating the formal-irregularity rule), the Carmona Local Civil Registrar certified that a marriage license had actually been issued to the couple (No. 7054133, typed as 7054033 on the contract). The Supreme Court held that issuing a license in a municipality where neither party resided, issuing it without completing the 10-day publication period and committing a one-digit typographical discrepancy were mere irregularities that did not invalidate the marriage, though the responsible party incurs civil, criminal and administrative liability.[^67]
- **Solemnizing officers' duty regarding visible irregularities and court personnel liability.** While a solemnizing officer is not ordinarily duty-bound to investigate behind an issued marriage license (*People v. Jansen*, as cited in [*Alcantara v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44273) and [*Office of the Court Administrator v. Judge Necessario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55788)), the Supreme Court *En Banc* held in [*Necessario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55788) that visible superimpositions on licenses, missing Article 21 embassy certificates for foreigners and patently impossible Article 34 affidavits involving minors should alert judges, dismissing four Cebu City MTCC judges for gross inefficiency, neglect of duty and gross ignorance of the law while dismissing, suspending or admonishing court employees who facilitated open-dated marriages, falsified applications, sold pro forma Article 34 affidavits or accepted tips.[^68] [^69]

Conversely, executing a **false Article 34 affidavit** where the parties never cohabited for five years or having **no marriage license at all** is never a mere irregularity under Article 4(3), because it goes to the total absence of a formal requisite under Article 4(1) and Article 35(3).[^70] [^71]

## Evidentiary Standards, Presumptions and Burden Shifting

### Presumption of Validity, Clear and Convincing Proof and Authority of Solemnizing Officer

Philippine law presumes the validity of marriage and the regularity of official acts, resolving doubts in favor of the marriage's existence and continuation.[^72] [^73] In civil actions for declaration of nullity, the petitioner bears the burden of overcoming those presumptions by **clear and convincing evidence**, a quantum greater than a preponderance of evidence though less than proof beyond reasonable doubt.[^74] A duly registered marriage contract is a public document and constitutes *prima facie* evidence of the facts stated therein, while the authority of an officer or clergyman shown to have performed a marriage ceremony is presumed absent contrary proof. In [*Maliwat-Melad v. Melad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69969), uncorroborated testimonies from the petitioner, a wedding guest who did not know the judge's appearance and a lawyer absent from the ceremony failed to establish by clear and convincing evidence that an unauthorized layman replaced Judge De Gracia.[^75]

### Proving Non-Issuance of a Marriage License: Local Civil Registrar Certifications vs. Treasurer Records

To declare a marriage void for absence of a marriage license, the absence must be apparent on the face of the marriage contract or supported by a certification from the local civil registrar that no such license was issued to the parties.[^76] [^77] Under Section 28, Rule 132 of the Rules of Court, a written statement by the custodian of an official record that no record exists is admissible to prove lack of record.[^78] Local civil registrars keep those records under Act No. 3753, Article 25 of the Family Code and National Statistics Office Administrative Order No. 1-93.[^79] [^80]

Two critical evidentiary rules emerge from the modern cases:

1. **Abandonment of *Sevilla v. Cardenas* literalism on the diligence formula.** As explained in [*Kho v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61927) and expressly applied to Family Code Article 4 in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555) and [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964), the Supreme Court abandoned the rigid approach in *Sevilla v. Cardenas* (as cited in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555)) requiring the literal words *despite diligent search* in the registrar's certificate.[^81] [^82] [^83] Under Rule 131, Section 3(m), official duty is presumed regularly performed unless affirmative evidence of laxity or irregularity is shown, so a certification stating that no record exists or that the license number was issued to another couple shifts the burden to the party or the Republic asserting validity.[^84] [^85] [^86]
2. **Municipal Treasurer certifications and logbooks lack competence to prove non-issuance.** In [*Pascua v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203), the petitioner relied on a Municipal Treasurer certification and logbook showing that Accountable Form No. 54 with the license serial number stated on his marriage certificate was not included in the series issued by the municipality. Tracing Local Treasury Operations Manual provisions and Commission on Audit rules, the Supreme Court held that a Municipal Treasurer holds custody only over pads of **blank, unaccomplished** Accountable Form No. 54 released in batches of 50 to bonded officers for inventory purposes, without recording applicants' names or issuance dates. Consequently, a Municipal Treasurer lacks legal competence to certify whether an accomplished marriage license was issued. Only a certification from the **Local Civil Registrar** (or a subpoena *duces tecum* directed to the Registrar, corroborated if needed by the solemnizing officer's retained original license under Article 23) carries probative weight.[^87]

### Admissions Against Interest and Criminal-Case Presumptions Under Section 6, Rule 131

In nullity suits involving simulated ceremonies or false Article 34 affidavits, a petitioner's own testimony admitting participation in the ruse is not dismissed automatically as self-serving when it exposes the witness to criminal liability for falsification or perjury. In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555) and [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), the Supreme Court treated the petitioner's declarations as **admissions against interest**. In [*Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), it found them bolstered by the prosecutor's non-collusion report and the PSA certification.[^88] In [*Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), it held that the husband's statements about the false affidavit may be given probative weight because they were admissions against interest.[^89]

In **criminal prosecutions**, however, a marriage certificate's *prima facie* evidentiary presumption is strictly constrained by the constitutional presumption of innocence and Section 6, Rule 131 of the 2019 Amendments to the Revised Rules on Evidence (applied retroactively in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) as a procedural rule benefiting the accused). Under Section 6, Rule 131, if the prosecution uses a marriage certificate (the basic fact) to infer that a marriage possessed all essential and formal requisites (the presumed fact), both the basic fact and the inference must hold beyond reasonable doubt.[^90] Once defense testimony or an LCR certification presents substantial countervailing evidence (such as absence of a solemnizing officer and ceremony in [*Genio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) or absence of any marriage license application in [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376)), the presumption dissipates and the burden shifts back to the prosecution to prove every requisite beyond reasonable doubt.[^91] [^92]

## Procedural Governance: Collateral Attack, Standing and Res Judicata

### Collateral Attack and Standing to Question Void Marriages

Because a void *ab initio* marriage under the first paragraph of Article 4 is legally inexistent, no prior judicial declaration of nullity is required **except for purposes of remarriage under Article 40** and as otherwise provided by law.[^93] [^94] In [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319), children from a first marriage sued after their father's death to declare his 1986 second marriage void for lack of a license (false five-year cohabitation affidavit under Civil Code Article 76). Reversing the trial court's dismissal under Article 47, the Supreme Court held that Article 47 governs only **voidable** marriages (which only the spouses may challenge during their lifetime), whereas the Family Code imposes no such limitation on **void** marriages, allowing any proper interested party (including heirs whose successional rights are affected) to assail a void marriage directly or collaterally even after a spouse's death.[^95]

Applying [*Niñal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319) to a Family Code marriage, the Supreme Court in [*De Castro v. Assidao-De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44838) held that a trial court had jurisdiction to pass upon the nullity of a marriage **collaterally inside an action for support**, even without the participation of the public prosecutor or Solicitor General, where determining the marriage's validity was essential to adjudicate support and filiation (ruling the child illegitimate yet entitled to support from her biological father).[^96]

### Res Judicata and Splitting a Cause of Action: Mallion vs. Pascua

Although an action or defense of absolute nullity is imprescriptible, litigants cannot split one cause of action over the validity of a marriage into successive direct petitions after losing on the merits. In [*Mallion v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42362), a husband filed an Article 36 psychological incapacity petition in 1995 and lost on the merits in a final judgment. When he filed a second petition in 1999 asserting lack of a marriage license under Article 4, the Supreme Court affirmed dismissal on the ground of *res judicata* (bar by prior judgment under Rule 39, Section 47(b)) and splitting a single cause of action, holding that both suits sought the same cause (the declaration of nullity of the same marriage) and that litigating Article 36 impliedly conceded due celebration of the marriage.[^97]

By contrast, in [*Pascua v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203), the Supreme Court held that dismissal of a nullity petition strictly for **insufficiency of evidence** (offering an incompetent Municipal Treasurer certification instead of an LCR certification) is **not** a judgment on the merits determining substantive rights and liabilities. Consequently, such dismissal does not constitute *res judicata* and is without prejudice to refiling the nullity petition with competent proof.[^98]

### [*Tilar v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63283)

Jerrysus Tilar petitioned the Regional Trial Court of Baybay City to declare his marriage void for psychological incapacity under Article 36. He alleged that he and his wife married in a Catholic church with a priest as solemnizing officer. The trial court dismissed the petition for lack of jurisdiction. It reasoned that the State cannot inquire into the validity of a church marriage because of the separation of Church and State. The Supreme Court granted the petition and ordered the trial court to proceed.[^99]

The Supreme Court tied its ruling to the Family Code. It recited the rule in Article 4 that the absence of an essential or formal requisite renders the marriage void *ab initio*, except as stated in Article 35(2).[^100] It noted that Articles 7 and 8 allow a priest to solemnize a marriage in a church. A marriage solemnized by a Catholic priest in a church complies with those provisions. The Supreme Court added that, although the Catholic Church considers marriage a sacrament, marriage has civil and legal consequences governed by the Family Code.[^101]

The Supreme Court then held that a petition to declare a marriage void falls within the exclusive original jurisdiction of the Regional Trial Court over actions involving the contract of marriage and marital relations.[^102] The case shows that Article 4 and the other requisites in the Family Code apply to a church marriage in the same way as to any other marriage. The Supreme Court did not decide whether this marriage was void.

## Criminal Law Intersections: Bigamy (Article 349, RPC) and Illegal Marriage (Article 350, RPC)

The interplay between Article 4's declaration that marriages lacking essential or formal requisites are void *ab initio* and criminal liability for bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code underwent major doctrinal refinement and eventual abandonment of an earlier strict rule:

1. **Early collateral-attack prohibition and the [*Morigo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464) exception.** Under earlier jurisprudence applied in [*Capili v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55959) (2013), citing *Jarillo v. People* and *Tenebro v. Court of Appeals* (as cited in [*Capili*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55959)), the Supreme Court held that a subsequent judicial declaration of nullity of a marriage, even where the accused argued violation of Article 3 in relation to Article 4, did not bar a prosecution for bigamy because bigamy was consummated upon celebrating the second marriage without a prior judicial declaration under Article 40.[^103] Even during that period, however, [*Morigo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464) (2004) carved out a crucial Article 4 distinction: where the alleged first marriage consisted solely of privately signing a marriage contract with **no ceremony and no solemnizing officer**, no ostensibly valid marriage ever existed (unlike *Mercado v. Tan*, as cited in [*Morigo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464)), so no prior judicial declaration was needed and the accused was acquitted of bigamy.[^104]
2. **Abandonment under *Pulido v. People* (as quoted in [*Lapira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) and reiterated in [*Genio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)).** As quoted at length in [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) (2026), the Supreme Court *En Banc* in *Pulido v. People* (as cited in [*Lapira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376)) **expressly abandoned** the earlier precedents barring collateral attack in bigamy cases.[^105] [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) (2024) reiterated that an accused in bigamy may validly raise a void *ab initio* second marriage without a judicial declaration of nullity.[^106] Under the governing *Pulido* rule, a judicial declaration of absolute nullity is **not** necessary to prove that a first or subsequent marriage is void *ab initio* in a bigamy prosecution. The accused may prove its nullity by testimonial or documentary evidence (such as an LCR certification of no marriage license in [*Lapira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) or testimony proving absence of ceremony and solemnizing officer in [*Genio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)), regardless of when any civil nullity decree is obtained.[^107] [^108] Crucially, *Pulido* (as quoted in [*Lapira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376)) preserves the Article 4 distinction for **voidable** marriages: if the prior or subsequent marriage is merely voidable under Article 45, an annulment decree is not a defense to bigamy because a voidable marriage was valid and subsisting when the second marriage was celebrated.[^109]
3. **Criminal liability under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code.** Acquittal of bigamy under Article 349 does not leave an accused immune when the second ceremony was knowingly sham or unlawful. Under the variance doctrine in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), although the accused was acquitted of bigamy on the ground of reasonable doubt, because the second marriage lacked the essential and formal requisites, the Supreme Court convicted him beyond reasonable doubt of **knowingly contracting a marriage against the provisions of laws under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code** because he signed the marriage certificate and staged the sham wedding knowing his first marriage remained undissolved and without complying with Article 40.[^110]

## Related Provisions

- **[Family Code, Article 1](art-001.md)**: Defines marriage as a special contract of permanent union governed by law and not subject to stipulation, preventing parties or courts from nullifying valid marriages on unenumerated grounds such as ulterior motives ([*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278)).
- **[Family Code, Article 2](art-002.md)**: Establishes the essential requisites of legal capacity (male and female) and freely given consent in the presence of the solemnizing officer, whose absence triggers nullity *ab initio* under Article 4, paragraph 1 ([*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278) and [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555)).
- **[Family Code, Article 3](art-003.md)**: Enumerates the three formal requisites (authority of the solemnizing officer, valid marriage license unless exempt and marriage ceremony before two witnesses of legal age). Their absence triggers nullity *ab initio* under Article 4, paragraph 1 whereas a mere irregularity leaves the marriage valid under Article 4, paragraph 3 ([*Abbas v. Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503) and [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979)).
- **[Family Code, Article 7](art-007.md) and [Article 8](art-008.md)**: Designate authorized solemnizing officers (including judges within their court's jurisdiction) and specify public venue rules. Violation of a judge's territorial jurisdiction without an Article 8 exception creates a formal irregularity under Articles 3 and 4 ([*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979) and [*Beso v. Judge Daguman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903)).
- **[Family Code, Article 21](art-021.md), [Article 23](art-023.md) and [Article 25](art-025.md)**: Require diplomatic certificates of legal capacity for foreign nationals, mandate solemnizing officers to distribute and register marriage contracts within fifteen days and require local civil registrars to maintain marriage license registry books ([*Office of the Court Administrator v. Judge Necessario*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55788), [*Cosca v. Judge Palaypayon, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/am_mtj-92-721_1994.html) and [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964)).
- **[Family Code, Article 34](art-034.md)**: Exempts couples who have continuously and exclusively lived together as husband and wife for at least five years without legal impediment from the marriage license requirement. A false Article 34 affidavit does not take the place of a license, so the marriage is void *ab initio* ([*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), [*De Castro v. Assidao-De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44838) ). A claim of Article 34 exemption with no affidavit and no five-year cohabitation fails the same way ([*Bounsit-Torralba v. Torralba*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68706)).
- **[Family Code, Article 35](art-035.md)**: Lists void *ab initio* marriages, including Article 35(2) (good-faith exception to lack of solemnizing officer's authority expressly cross-referenced in Article 4) and Article 35(3) (marriages solemnized without a license outside Chapter 2) ([*Maliwat-Melad v. Melad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69969) and [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964)).
- **[Family Code, Article 36](art-036.md)**: Governs nullity due to psychological incapacity. A final judgment dismissing an Article 36 petition on the merits bars a subsequent Article 4 petition for lack of marriage license by *res judicata* ([*Mallion v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42362)).
- **[Family Code, Article 40](art-040.md) and [Article 41](art-041.md)**: Require a prior final judicial declaration of nullity or presumptive death before contracting a subsequent marriage. Absence of an Article 41 judicial declaration renders a subsequent marriage bigamous and void under Article 35(4) ([*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979)), while *Pulido v. People* (as cited in [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376)) allows collateral nullity defenses in bigamy prosecutions ([*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) and [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)). An acquittal for bigamy does not excuse a conviction under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code ([*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)).
- **[Family Code, Article 45](art-045.md), [Article 46](art-046.md) and [Article 47](art-047.md)**: Govern voidable marriages resulting from defects in essential requisites referenced in Article 4, paragraph 2, restrict fraud grounds and limit standing and prescriptive periods in direct annulment actions ([*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278) and [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319)).
- **Revised Penal Code, Articles 349 and 350**: Define criminal liability for bigamy and for knowingly contracting a marriage against the provisions of law ([*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)). The Revised Penal Code also punishes civil authorities who perform illegal marriage ceremonies in accordance with the Marriage Law, as noted in [*Cosca v. Judge Palaypayon, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/am_mtj-92-721_1994.html).

## Suggested Answers

### Question 1

B and A dated exclusively from December 2003 and only intermittently stayed in the same place. B then worked in Israel from May 24, 2006 until May 3, 2009. Upon returning to the Philippines, B and A executed a sworn Affidavit of Cohabitation on May 6, 2009 attesting that they had lived together continuously as husband and wife since December 2003 and married on May 8, 2009 without securing a marriage license. Years later, B filed a petition to declare the marriage void *ab initio* for lack of a marriage license. Opposing the petition, the Republic argues (1) that the falsity of the five-year cohabitation statement is merely an irregularity in a formal requisite that leaves the marriage valid under Article 4, paragraph 3 while contending (2) that B is barred from seeking nullity because he himself executed the false affidavit and therefore comes to court with unclean hands. Rule on both contentions.

Both contentions lack merit, so the marriage must be declared void *ab initio*.

First, under Articles 3(2), 4 and 35(3) of the Family Code, a valid marriage license is an indispensable formal requisite whose absence renders the marriage void *ab initio*, except in marriages strictly complying with Chapter 2, Title I. Article 34 exempts parties from securing a marriage license only when they have lived together continuously and exclusively as husband and wife for at least five years immediately before the marriage without any legal impediment and state those facts under oath.[^111] As held in [*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), intermittent and occasional stays in the same place do not amount to cohabitation as husband and wife. A three-year absence abroad also defeats continuous five-year cohabitation.[^112] [*De Castro v. Assidao-De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44838) and [*Bounsit-Torralba v. Torralba*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68706) likewise denied the Article 34 exemption where the parties had not lived together as spouses for five years.[^113] [^114] Contrary to the Republic's first argument, a false Article 34 affidavit is not a mere irregularity in the formal requisites under Article 4, paragraph 3, because the false affidavit cannot take the place of a license and is, in the words of [*De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44838), "a mere scrap of paper," leaving the marriage without a marriage license and therefore void *ab initio*.[^115]

Second, the Republic's invocation of the clean-hands doctrine fails. In [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964) and [*Abbas v. Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503), the Supreme Court held that a petitioner's acquiescence in a spurious or missing license or an allegedly impure motive does not preclude a declaration of absolute nullity.[^116] [^117] While B's false statement exposes him to possible perjury or irregularity liability in a separate proceeding ([*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449) and [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964)), courts cannot apply the clean-hands rule to validate a marriage that Article 4 and Article 35(3) declare void *ab initio*.[^118]

### Question 2

Judge D, the presiding judge of the Municipal Circuit Trial Court covering Municipalities S and B, solemnized the marriage of F and G (both possessing legal capacity, genuine consent and a valid marriage license, in the presence of two adult witnesses) inside Judge D's residence in Municipality D, which lies outside his court's territorial jurisdiction. Neither party was at the point of death or in a remote place. Only G signed an unsworn letter requesting that the wedding be held at Judge D's home. When F later sues to declare the marriage void *ab initio* under Article 4 for lack of authority of the solemnizing officer and also files an administrative complaint against Judge D, how should the civil validity of the marriage and Judge D's administrative liability be resolved?

The marriage between F and G remains **valid**, whereas Judge D is **administratively liable** for gross ignorance of the law.

Under Article 7(1) of the Family Code, an incumbent member of the judiciary may solemnize marriages within the court's jurisdiction, while Article 8 directs public solemnization in chambers or open court unless the marriage is contracted at the point of death, in a remote place under Article 29 or upon the sworn written request of both parties.[^119] As settled in [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979) and reiterated in [*Beso v. Judge Daguman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903), Article 8 is a directory venue provision and does not expand a municipal judge's territorial authority. Furthermore, an unsworn request signed by only one party fails to satisfy Article 8. Nevertheless, when a judge appointed to a specific court solemnizes a marriage outside his court's territorial jurisdiction where all other essential and formal requisites are present, the defect constitutes a **resultant irregularity in a formal requisite** under Article 3. Pursuant to the third paragraph of Article 4, an irregularity in the formal requisites does not affect the validity of the marriage, so F's petition to declare the marriage void *ab initio* must fail.[^120] [^121]

At the same time, Article 4, paragraph 3 mandates that the party responsible for the formal irregularity shall be civilly, criminally and administratively liable. Under [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979) and [*Beso v. Judge Daguman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903), an incumbent judge is presumed to know the territorial limits of judicial office and the requirements of Articles 7 and 8, warranting administrative sanction for gross ignorance of the law or neglect of duty.[^122] [^123]

### Question 3

R was validly married to M in 2006. While that marriage remained undissolved and without obtaining any judicial declaration of nullity, R went through a purported second marriage in 2013 with C at C's family home. No Municipal Mayor appeared, no vows or rings were exchanged and only a municipal civil registrar attended, asking R and C if they accepted each other and having them sign a Marriage Certificate listing the Municipal Mayor as solemnizing officer, which was later registered with the Philippine Statistics Authority. Charged with bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code, R presents C and two witnesses present at the house to prove the absence of an authorized solemnizing officer and marriage ceremony. May R be convicted of bigamy under Article 349, or of any other offense?

R must be **acquitted of bigamy** under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code on reasonable doubt, but he may be convicted of **knowingly contracting a marriage against the provisions of laws** under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code.

Under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code, one essential element of bigamy is that the subsequent marriage would have been valid had it not been for the existence of the first. Under Articles 3 and 4 of the Family Code, the authority of the solemnizing officer and a marriage ceremony before the solemnizing officer and two adult witnesses are formal requisites whose absence renders the marriage void *ab initio* (save for Article 35(2), whose good faith exception was not shown to exist in [*Genio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)).[^124] Applying *Pulido v. People* (as quoted in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) and [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376)) and [*Morigo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464), an accused in bigamy may collaterally raise the defense that the subsequent marriage is void *ab initio* without securing a prior judicial declaration of nullity.[^125] [^126] Further, under Section 6, Rule 131 of the Rules of Court as explained in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), once defense witnesses present substantial testimony contradicting the *prima facie* presumption of the registered Marriage Certificate, that presumption dissipates and the prosecution bears the burden of proving beyond reasonable doubt that all requisites of the second marriage existed. Absent rebuttal proof from the State, R must be acquitted of bigamy.[^127]

However, under [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), the lesser offense penalized under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code is necessarily included in a bigamy information under the variance doctrine. By signing the Marriage Certificate and staging a sham ceremony with C while knowing his 2006 marriage to M was undissolved and without complying with Article 40 (knowledge of marriage laws being presumed under Article 3 of the Civil Code), R knowingly contracted a marriage against the provisions of laws and is guilty under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code.[^128]

# Quotes

> "In ruling that Albios’ marriage was void for lack of consent, the CA characterized such as akin to a marriage by way of jest. A marriage in jest is a pretended marriage, legal in form but entered into as a joke, with no real intention of entering into the actual marriage status, and with a clear understanding that the parties would not be bound. The ceremony is not followed by any conduct indicating a purpose to enter into such a relation. It is a pretended marriage not intended to be real and with no intention to create any legal ties whatsoever, hence, the absence of any genuine consent. Marriages in jest are void ab initio, not for vitiated, defective, or unintelligent consent, but for a complete absence of consent. There is no genuine consent because the parties have absolutely no intention of being bound in any way or for any purpose."[^129]

> "The avowed purpose of marriage under Article 1 of the Family Code is for the couple to establish a conjugal and family life. The possibility that the parties in a marriage might have no real intention to establish a life together is, however, insufficient to nullify a marriage freely entered into in accordance with law. The same Article 1 provides that the nature, consequences, and incidents of marriage are governed by law and not subject to stipulation. A marriage may, thus, only be declared void or voidable under the grounds provided by law. There is no law that declares a marriage void if it is entered into for purposes other than what the Constitution or law declares, such as the acquisition of foreign citizenship. Therefore, so long as all the essential and formal requisites precribed by law are present, and it is not void or voidable under the grounds provided by law, it shall be declared valid."[^130]

> "Accordingly, in addition to there being no consent to enter into marriage and no actual marriage ceremony being performed, the registered marriage further lacks the formal requisite of a marriage license. For a multiplicity of reasons, such marriage was inexistent. Therefore, the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals erred in denying Rosario's Petition for Declaration of Nullity."[^131]

> "In the instant case, however, no marriage ceremony at all was performed by a duly authorized solemnizing officer. Petitioner and Lucia Barrete merely signed a marriage contract on their own. The mere private act of signing a marriage contract bears no semblance to a valid marriage and thus, needs no judicial declaration of nullity. Such act alone, without more, cannot be deemed to constitute an ostensibly valid marriage for which petitioner might be held liable for bigamy unless he first secures a judicial declaration of nullity before he contracts a subsequent marriage."[^132]

> "All the evidence cited by the CA to show that a wedding ceremony was conducted and a marriage contract was signed does not operate to cure the absence of a valid marriage license. Article 4 of the Family Code is clear when it says, “The absence of any of the essential or formal requisites shall render the marriage void ab initio, except as stated in Article 35(2).” Article 35(3) of the Family Code also provides that a marriage solemnized without a license is void from the beginning, except those exempt from the license requirement under Articles 27 to 34, Chapter 2, Title I of the same Code. Again, this marriage cannot be characterized as among the exemptions, and thus, having been solemnized without a marriage license, is void ab initio."[^133]

> "As the records of this case show, there is inadequate evidence proving that Alma and Boots continuously and exclusively cohabited as husband and wife for a period of five years immediately preceding the celebration of their marriage. At most, the two dated exclusively and sojourned in several places. However, the fact that they intermittently and occasionally stayed in the same place does not amount to cohabitation as husband and wife contemplated by Article 34 of the Family Code. Verily, Boots admitted signing the Affidavit of Cohabitation despite not meeting the statutory conditions. Such falsity renders the affidavit ineffectual; it does not confer exemption from the license requirement. The marriage, founded on this false declaration, is void."[^134]

> "The falsity of the affidavit cannot be considered as a mere irregularity in the formal requisites of marriage. The law dispenses with the marriage license requirement for a man and a woman who have lived together and exclusively with each other as husband and wife for a continuous and unbroken period of at least five years before the marriage. The aim of this provision is to avoid exposing the parties to humiliation, shame and embarrassment concomitant with the scandalous cohabitation of persons outside a valid marriage due to the publication of every applicant’s name for a marriage license. In the instant case, there was no “scandalous cohabitation” to protect; in fact, there was no cohabitation at all. The false affidavit which petitioner and respondent executed so they could push through with the marriage has no value whatsoever; it is a mere scrap of paper. They were not exempt from the marriage license requirement. Their failure to obtain and present a marriage license renders their marriage void ab initio."[^135]

> "That said, the Court also recognizes that in petitions to declare the absolute nullity of marriage based on the absence of a valid marriage license, testimony of this nature should not ipso facto preclude a finding of nullity on the ground that parties who come to court must do so with clean hands. To be sure, a marriage contracted despite the absence of a marriage license necessarily implies some sort of irregularity. Nevertheless, such irregularity, as well as any liability resulting therefrom, must be threshed out and determined in a proper case filed for the purpose. It is in that separate proceeding where the party or parties responsible for the irregularity would be ascertained. A contrary ruling would operate to validate marriages which the law itself declares void."[^136]

> "Thus, since petitioner failed to prove, by clear and convincing evidence, the lack of authority of the solemnizing officer, the marriage is valid. The legal presumption accorded to public documents, in favor of the authority of the solemnizing officer, and towards the validity of marriage, must be upheld."[^137]

> "Thus, petitioner's case falls under the exception of void marriages as provided in Article 35(2) of the Family Code. Petitioner's marriage with private respondent is valid owing to her genuine belief, in good faith, that the solemnizing officer had the legal authority to officiate her marriage with private respondent."[^138]

> "A priest who is commissioned and allowed by his local ordinary to marry the faithful, is authorized to do so only within the area of the diocese or place allowed by his Bishop. An appellate court Justice or a Justice of this Court has jurisdiction over the entire Philippines to solemnize marriages, regardless of the venue, as long as the requisites of the law are complied with. However, judges who are appointed to specific jurisdictions, may officiate in weddings only within said areas and not beyond. Where a judge solemnizes a marriage outside his court's jurisdiction, there is a resultant irregularity in the formal requisite laid down in Article 3, which while it may not affect the validity of the marriage, may subject the officiating official to administrative liability."[^139]

> "The fact that Judge Palaypayon did not sign the marriage contracts or certificates of those marriages he solemnized without a marriage license, there were no dates placed in the marriage contracts to show when they were solemnized, the contracting parties were not furnished their marriage contracts and the Local Civil Registrar was not being sent any copy of the marriage contract, will not absolve him from liability. By solemnizing alone a marriage without a marriage license he as the solemnizing officer is the one responsible for the irregularity in not complying (with) the formal requ(i)sites of marriage and under Article 4(3) of the Family Code of the Philippines, he shall be civilly, criminally and administratively liable."[^140]

> "Petitioner, in a faint attempt to demolish the probative value of the marriage license, claims that neither he nor respondent is a resident of Carmona, Cavite. Even then, we still hold that there is no sufficient basis to annul petitioner and respondent's marriage. Issuance of a marriage license in a city or municipality, not the residence of either of the contracting parties, and issuance of a marriage license despite the absence of publication or prior to the completion of the 10- day period for publication are considered mere irregularities that do not affect the validity of the marriage. An irregularity in any of the formal requisites of marriage does not affect its validity but the party or parties responsible for the irregularity are civilly, criminally and administratively liable."[^141]

> "The Court stresses that in declaration of nullity of marriage cases, it is the certification issued by the Local Civil Registrar that enjoys probative value, as it is the Registrar's legal mandate to maintain records of duly accomplished sworn applications for marriage licenses. The fact that fees are required to be paid to the Municipal Treasurer before the marriage license is issued by the Local Civil Registrar does not mean that records related thereto are likewise kept by the former. To repeat, the permanent record kept by the Municipal Treasurer only relates to blank and unaccomplished forms for marriage licenses."[^142]

> "Voidable and void marriages are not identical. A marriage that is annulable is valid until otherwise declared by the court; whereas a marriage that is void ab initio is considered as having never to have taken place and cannot be the source of rights. The first can be generally ratified or confirmed by free cohabitation or prescription while the other can never be ratified. A voidable marriage cannot be assailed collaterally except in a direct proceeding while a void marriage can be attacked collaterally. Consequently, void marriages can be questioned even after the death of either party but voidable marriages can be assailed only during the lifetime of the parties and not after death of either, in which case the parties and their offspring will be left as if the marriage had been perfectly valid. That is why the action or defense for nullity is imprescriptible, unlike voidable marriages where the action prescribes. Only the parties to a voidable marriage can assail it but any proper interested party may attack a void marriage. Void marriages have no legal effects except those declared by law concerning the properties of the alleged spouses, regarding co-ownership or ownership through actual joint contribution, and its effect on the children born to such void marriages as provided in Article 50 in relation to Article 43 and 44 as well as Article 51, 53 and 54 of the Family Code. On the contrary, the property regime governing voidable marriages is generally conjugal partnership and the children conceived before its annulment are legitimate."[^143]

> "Petitioner, however, forgets that he is simply invoking different grounds for the same cause of action. By definition, a cause of action is the act or omission by which a party violates the right of another. In both petitions, petitioner has the same cause - the declaration of nullity of his marriage to respondent. What differs is the ground upon which the cause of action is predicated. These grounds cited by petitioner essentially split the various aspects of the pivotal issue that holds the key to the resolution of this controversy, that is, the actual status of petitioner and respondent's marriage."[^144]

> "At the outset, the Court reiterates its ruling in Pulido v. People, that the "accused in bigamy may validly raise [the defense of] a void ab initio second or subsequent marriage even without a judicial declaration of nullity." Certainly, if the second marriage was void ab initio on grounds other than being bigamous, then it is inexistent from the beginning; thus, the element of entering into a second or subsequent marriage would be lacking, warranting the acquittal of the accused for the felony of Bigamy."[^145]

> "However, if the first marriage is merely voidable, the accused cannot interpose an annulment decree as a defense in the criminal prosecution for bigamy since the voidable first marriage is considered valid and subsisting when the second marriage was contracted. The crime of bigamy, therefore, is consummated when the second marriage was celebrated during the subsistence of the voidable first marriage. The same rule applies if the second marriage is merely considered as voidable."[^146]

> "It must be emphasized that Articles 349 and 350 of the RPC are crimes against the civil status of persons, which were enacted "to support the sanctity of the marriage relation and the welfare of society." They criminalize the act of making an "unlawful contract and the abuse of the formality which the law has enjoined as requisite to the creation of the marital relation," because of its "outrage upon public decency, its violation of the public economy, as well as its tendency to cheat one into a surrender of the person under the appearance of right." Simply, the law acknowledges that while a second marriage may be void ab initio due to the existence of a legal impediment or absence of any of the essential requisites for a valid marriage, it may still produce legal consequences in that the parties thereto may incur criminal liabilities for either Article 349 or Article 350 of the RPC."[^147]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Diagnostic Checklist for Article 4 Analysis.** Before classifying a marital deficiency under Article 4, run four sequential threshold checks:
> 1. **Identify the Governing Law by Marriage Date:** Confirm whether the marriage was celebrated on or after August 3, 1988 (Family Code) or prior thereto (Civil Code Articles 53, 58, 76 and 80, as in [*Kho v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61927), [*Alcantara v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44273) and [*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319)).
> 2. **Pinpoint the Exact Requisite (Article 2 vs. Article 3):** Determine whether the issue concerns an **essential requisite** under Article 2 (legal capacity or freely given consent) or a **formal requisite** under Article 3 (authority of solemnizing officer, marriage license unless exempt or marriage ceremony).
> 3. **Distinguish Absence, Defect and Irregularity:** Ask whether the requisite was **completely absent** (void *ab initio*, unless saved by good-faith belief in an officer's authority under Article 35(2)), whether an essential requisite was merely **defective** (voidable under Article 45, such as vitiated consent) or whether a formal requisite was only **irregular** (marriage remains valid, with civil, criminal and administrative liability on the responsible party).
> 4. **Test the Evidentiary Record and Procedural Bars:** Verify whether the petitioner holds a **Local Civil Registrar** certification rather than an incompetent Municipal Treasurer record ([*Pascua v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203)), then check whether a prior final judgment on the merits under Article 36 bars a second nullity suit under *res judicata* ([*Mallion v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42362)).

### Distinguishing Absence from Defect and Ulterior Motive

> [!NOTE]
> **Consent in Jest, Sham Signatures and Immigration Marriages ([*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278) vs. [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555)).** On the Bar Exam and in nullity litigation, never confuse **motives for marriage** with **lack of consent**:
> - If both parties knowingly appear before an authorized judge in a ceremony with a license and intend to create a real legal marital bond to obtain U.S. citizenship or financial consideration, genuine consent exists and the marriage is **valid** under Article 4 and not voidable for fraud under Articles 45(3) and 46 ([*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278)).
> - By contrast, if the parties execute a marriage in jest with zero intention to create any legal tie ([*Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278)) or merely sign a blank certificate with no ceremony ([*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555) and [*Morigo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46464)), essential consent or formal requisites are completely absent and the union is **void *ab initio***.

### Marriage License Traps and Article 34 Cohabitation

> [!TIP]
> **Spotting Fatal License Absence vs. Non-Invalidating Irregularities ([*Republic v. Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), [*Abbas v. Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503) and [*Alcantara v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44273)).** Examine what actually happened at the local civil registry:
> - **Total absence (marriage void *ab initio*):** No license issued in the parties' names, a license number belonging to another couple ([*Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503) and [*Cariaga v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964)) or a false Article 34 affidavit where the couple did not cohabit continuously and exclusively for five full years ([*Aguirre*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70449), [*De Castro v. Assidao-De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44838) and [*Bounsit-Torralba v. Torralba*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68706)). Unclean hands, wedding photos or children do not cure the absence ([*Abbas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55503) and [*Cariaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964)).
> - **Mere formal irregularity (marriage valid):** A license was actually issued to the parties, even if obtained in a city where neither habitually resided, without 10-day publication or transcribed with a one-digit clerical typo ([*Alcantara v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44273)). Likewise, an LCR certification need not recite the words *despite diligent search* ([*Cariaga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67964) and [*Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555)), whereas a **Municipal Treasurer's** logbook of blank Accountable Form No. 54 pads cannot prove non-issuance ([*Pascua v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203)).

### Solemnizing Officers, Article 35(2) and Territorial Authority

> [!NOTE]
> **Separating Article 35(2) Good Faith from Territorial Judicial Irregularities ([*Maliwat-Melad v. Melad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69969), [*Navarro v. Judge Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979) and [*Beso v. Judge Daguman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903)).** When a question tests a solemnizing officer's authority:
> - Under Article 4 in relation to Article 35(2), if an unauthorized person officiated but either or both parties genuinely believed in good faith that the officer had legal authority (as when a petitioner believed for 27 years that an incumbent judge officiated), the marriage is **valid** ([*Maliwat-Melad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69969)).
> - If an incumbent MCTC or MTC judge solemnizes a marriage outside territorial court boundaries without an Article 8 exception (only one party requested it or neither was *in articulo mortis* or in a remote place), the territorial breach is a **formal irregularity** under Article 4, paragraph 3: the marriage remains **valid**, while the judge faces suspension or fine ([*Domagtoy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33979) and [*Beso*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50903)).

### Procedural Strategy and Criminal Exposure

> [!TIP]
> **Avoiding Res Judicata Traps and Distinguishing Article 349 vs. Article 350 RPC ([*Mallion v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42362), [*Pascua v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203), [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) and [*Lapira v. Fariscal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376)).**
> - **Consolidate all nullity grounds in one petition:** Litigating Article 36 psychological incapacity alone to final judgment on the merits precludes a subsequent Article 4 petition for lack of a marriage license under *res judicata* ([*Mallion*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42362)), whereas a prior dismissal strictly for insufficiency of evidence does not bar refiling ([*Pascua*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70203)).
> - **Collateral nullity in support, estate and criminal cases:** Unlike voidable marriages, void *ab initio* marriages may be attacked collaterally in support actions ([*De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44838)), after death by interested heirs ([*Niñal v. Bayadog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53319)) and in bigamy prosecutions without a prior civil nullity decree ([*Lapira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70376) and [*Genio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)). Yet even if a sham second marriage lacks formal requisites and defeats bigamy under Article 349 RPC, an accused who knowingly contracts an unlawful second marriage without an Article 40 decree risks conviction under **Article 350 RPC** ([*Genio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309)).
