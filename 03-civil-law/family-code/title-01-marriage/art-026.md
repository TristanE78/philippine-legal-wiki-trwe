> Art. 26. All marriages solemnized outside the Philippines, in accordance with the laws in force in the country where they were solemnized, and valid there as such, shall also be valid in this country, except those prohibited under Articles 35 (1), (4), (5) and (6), 3637 and 38. (17a)
>
> Where a marriage between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner is validly celebrated and a divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law. (As amended by Executive Order 227)[^family-code]


# Explanation

Article 26 of the Family Code of the Philippines establishes the dual legal architecture governing international marital relations in Philippine private international law. The provision comprises two distinct paragraphs designed to resolve the tension between foreign marital events and domestic family policy. Paragraph 1 incorporates the ancient conflict-of-laws doctrine of lex loci celebrationis, establishing that marriages solemnized abroad in conformity with the local law of the place of celebration are valid in the Philippines, subject to explicit public policy exceptions. Paragraph 2 introduces a remedial mechanism that recognizes the residual effects of a foreign divorce obtained in mixed marriages, capacitating the Filipino spouse to remarry under Philippine law once the alien spouse is freed to contract a subsequent marriage abroad.

Together, these two paragraphs balance the State policy protecting marriage as an inviolable social institution with the constitutional imperative to prevent grave inequality and legal absurdity. Over four decades of jurisprudence, the Supreme Court has transformed Article 26 from a narrow statutory exception into an equitable charter protecting Filipino citizens trapped in dysfunctional cross-border unions.

---

## 1. Statutory Text, Legislative Genesis, and Historical Roots

### 1.1 Dual Structure and Textual Framework

Article 26 contains two distinct statutory commands addressing different phases of marital life. Paragraph 1 addresses the inception and celebration of marriage abroad, adopting the rule of validation under the principle of locus regit actum. Paragraph 2 addresses the termination and dissolution of marriage abroad, creating an express statutory gateway for the recognition of foreign divorce decrees that dissolve mixed marriages.

The first paragraph preserves the rule originally codified in Article 71 of the Civil Code of 1950, which itself derived from Article 17 of the Spanish Civil Code of 1889. Under this rule, the formal and extrinsic validity of a marriage contract is tested by the law of the place where the ceremony was performed. If valid there, the marriage is recognized as valid in the Philippines, unless it violates essential statutory prohibitions that the State deems non-negotiable.

The second paragraph serves as an explicit legislative exception to the nationality principle embodied in Article 15 of the Civil Code. While Philippine citizens remain governed by Philippine laws concerning family rights, status, and legal capacity, Paragraph 2 permits a Filipino spouse to benefit from a foreign divorce decree when an alien spouse has been freed to remarry. This prevents an anomalous legal limping marriage where an individual is considered married under one legal system but single under another.

### 1.2 The Spanish Civil Code of 1889 and Act No. 2710

The historical evolution of Philippine divorce law explains the deliberate design of Article 26. Under the Spanish Civil Code of 1889, marriage was conceived exclusively as an indissoluble religious sacrament under canon law. Canonical rules governed matrimonial ties, and secular law provided solely for relative divorce or separation from bed and board without severing the marital bond.

Following American sovereignty, the Philippine Legislature enacted Act No. 2710 on March 11, 1917, establishing a regime of absolute divorce in the Philippines. Act No. 2710 was notably restrictive, permitting absolute divorce on only two grounds: adultery on the part of the wife or concubinage on the part of the husband. Furthermore, the statute required a prior criminal conviction for adultery or concubinage before an action for absolute divorce could even be filed in court. Despite its narrow grounds, Act No. 2710 established that absolute divorce could exist under Philippine statutory law.

During the Japanese occupation, Executive Order No. 141 liberalized divorce grounds to include intentional abandonment, incurable insanity, and grave physical abuse. Upon the liberation of the Philippines in 1944, General Douglas MacArthur issued a proclamation declaring all wartime executive acts null and void, which reinstated Act No. 2710 as the governing divorce statute.

### 1.3 The Civil Code of 1950 and the Absolute Nationality Principle

The enactment of the Civil Code of the Philippines (Republic Act No. 386) on August 30, 1950, marked a sharp return to strict matrimonial indissolubility. Guided by Dean Jorge Bocobo, the Code Commission repealed Act No. 2710, abolished absolute divorce entirely, and replaced it with legal separation under Article 97. The Code Commission reasoned that the Filipino tradition regarded marriage as sacred and permanent, and that the State had a duty to defend the family against fragmentation.

To reinforce this policy, the Civil Code maintained the absolute nationality principle under Article 15. Article 15 provides that laws relating to family rights and duties, or to the status, condition, and legal capacity of persons are binding upon citizens of the Philippines, even though living abroad. Correlatively, Article 17, paragraph 3 declared that prohibitive laws concerning persons, their acts or property, and those which have for their object public order, public policy and good customs shall not be rendered ineffective by laws or judgments promulgated, or by determinations or conventions agreed upon in a foreign country.

Under this rigid statutory structure, a divorce obtained abroad by a Filipino citizen was completely null and void in the Philippines. If two Filipinos secured a divorce decree in a foreign country, Philippine law ignored the decree and treated the spouses as perpetually married. However, an acute dilemma arose when a Filipino married an alien whose national law permitted absolute divorce.

### 1.4 The Watershed Ruling in Van Dorn v. Romillo, Jr.

The Supreme Court confronted this dilemma in the 1985 landmark case of *Van Dorn v. Romillo, Jr.*[^2] In that case, Alice Reyes Van Dorn, a Filipino citizen, married Richard Upton, an American citizen, in Hong Kong. The couple subsequently established residence in the Philippines and had two children. Upton later obtained an absolute divorce decree in the State of Nevada, United States.

Following the divorce, Upton filed a civil action in the Regional Trial Court of Pasay City, claiming that Van Dorn had established a business concern in Ermita, Manila, using conjugal funds. Upton prayed that the trial court order Van Dorn to render an accounting of the business and declare him entitled to manage the conjugal property. Van Dorn moved to dismiss the complaint on the ground that Upton had already secured an absolute divorce decree abroad and was no longer her husband. The trial court denied the motion to dismiss, reasoning that Philippine law did not recognize absolute divorce.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari and ordered the dismissal of the civil suit. Writing for the Supreme Court, Justice Irene Cortes emphasized the bilateral operation of the nationality principle. While Philippine citizens are governed by Philippine law under Article 15 of the Civil Code, foreign nationals are equally governed by their own national laws. Because the Nevada divorce was valid according to Upton's national law, it completely severed his marital ties to Van Dorn.

The Supreme Court held that Upton could not claim marital rights or management of property in the Philippines after renouncing his marriage abroad. To permit Upton to assert conjugal rights would allow an alien to exploit Philippine law to harass a Filipino citizen. Crucially, the Supreme Court declared that the Filipino spouse should not be discriminated against in her own country, and that she should no longer be considered married to the alien who had validly divorced her.

### 1.5 Deliberations of the Civil Code Revision Committee

When the Civil Code Revision Committee convened under the auspices of the University of the Philippines Law Center to draft the Family Code, the committee debated how to codify the *Van Dorn* ruling. The Committee included distinguished jurists, including Justice Jose B.L. Reyes, Justice Eduardo Caguioa, Justice Ricardo Puno, Judge Alicia Sempio-Diy, and Dean Fortunato Gupit.

The Committee unanimously agreed that Paragraph 1 of Article 26 should reproduce the rule of validation for foreign marriages, while expanding the statutory exceptions to include the new grounds of psychological incapacity under Article 36 and the public policy prohibitions under Article 38. However, the inclusion of an express provision on foreign divorce sparked intense debate.

Several committee members expressed deep concern that an explicit provision permitting remarriage after a foreign divorce would serve as a back door for absolute divorce in the Philippines. They feared that wealthy Filipinos would travel abroad, marry foreign citizens, secure quick divorces, and return to the Philippines to remarry. Consequently, the Committee deleted the draft second paragraph of Article 26 from the final draft submitted to President Corazon C. Aquino. When President Aquino signed Executive Order No. 209 into law on July 6, 1987, Article 26 contained only the first paragraph.

### 1.6 Executive Order No. 227 and the Insertion of Paragraph 2

The omission of the *Van Dorn* doctrine immediately drew sharp criticism from legal scholars, practitioners, and civil registrars. Critics pointed out that omitting the second paragraph left Filipino spouses in intolerable legal jeopardy. If an alien spouse obtained a divorce abroad and remarried, the Filipino spouse remained legally chained to the dissolved marriage under Philippine domestic law, unable to remarry, unable to rebuild their life, and exposed to criminal prosecution for bigamy or adultery if they entered into a new relationship.

Responding to these pressing concerns, the Cabinet and the Code Committee reconsidered their position. Only eleven days after promulgating the original Family Code, President Aquino signed Executive Order No. 227 on July 17, 1987. Executive Order No. 227 amended several provisions of the Family Code before its effectivity date, including Articles 36, 39, and 26.

Section 1 of Executive Order No. 227 formally added the second paragraph to Article 26:
Where a marriage between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner is validly celebrated and a divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law.

Judge Alicia Sempio-Diy explained in her handbook on the Family Code that this amendment was adopted specifically to avoid the absurd and unjust situation where the Filipino spouse remained bound by the marriage while the alien spouse was free to remarry under foreign law. Because Executive Order No. 227 was signed prior to the effectivity of the Family Code on August 3, 1988, Article 26 took effect containing both paragraphs.

### 1.7 Retroactivity and Transitory Application Under Article 256

Article 256 of the Family Code provides that the Code shall have retroactive effect insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights in accordance with the Civil Code or other laws. The application of Article 26(2) to marriages celebrated prior to August 3, 1988, was addressed by the Supreme Court in *San Luis v. San Luis*[^3] (involving a foreign divorce obtained in 1973 under the Civil Code) and *Republic v. Orbecido III*[^4] (involving a marriage solemnized in 1981 prior to the Family Code).

In *San Luis*, the children of the late Governor Felicisimo San Luis argued that his marriage to Felicidad San Luis in 1974 was bigamous because his prior marriage to an American citizen had been dissolved through a foreign divorce decree in Hawaii in 1973, before the Family Code took effect. The children contended that applying Article 26(2) retroactively would impair their vested inheritance rights.

The Supreme Court rejected this contention, ruling that retroactive application of the Family Code was not even necessary to validate the marriage. The Supreme Court explained that *Van Dorn* had already settled the law under the Civil Code of 1950. The enactment of Paragraph 2 of Article 26 merely codified the rule previously established by judicial precedent. Because the alien spouse had validly obtained a divorce capacitating her to remarry under American law, Governor San Luis had acquired legal capacity to remarry under Philippine law even prior to the Family Code. Consequently, the foreign divorce doctrine applies to all mixed marriages regardless of whether the marriage or the foreign divorce took place before or after August 3, 1988.


## 2. Constitutional Framework and Public Policy Considerations

### 2.1 Constitutional Status of Marriage as an Inviolable Social Institution

The 1987 Constitution establishes marriage as an institution entitled to extraordinary State protection. Article XV, Section 2 of the Constitution provides that marriage, as an inviolable social institution, is the foundation of the family and shall be protected by the State. Complementing this, Section 1 mandates that the State shall recognize the Filipino family as the foundation of the nation, and Section 3 guarantees the right of spouses to found a family in accordance with their religious convictions and the demands of responsible parenthood.

In ordinary civil litigation, parties enjoy full freedom of contract to alter, modify, or terminate their agreements by mutual consent. In contrast, marriage is a special contract of permanent union governed by public law rather than private whim. As Article 1 of the Family Code provides, marriage is not a mere contract but an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation.

This constitutional mandate forms the core justification for the general prohibition against absolute divorce between Filipino citizens. The State possesses a vital interest in preserving marital stability, preventing reckless dissolutions, and shielding children from familial rupture.

### 2.2 The Equal Protection Guarantee and the Landmark Manalo Ruling

While the Constitution mandates the protection of marriage, that protection must coexist with the fundamental guarantees of the Bill of Rights. Article III, Section 1 of the 1987 Constitution guarantees that no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, nor shall any person be denied the equal protection of the laws.

The constitutional tension between the protection of marriage and the equal protection clause formed the decisive analytical foundation of the Supreme Court En Banc ruling in *Republic v. Manalo*.[^5] In *Manalo*, the Office of the Solicitor General argued that Paragraph 2 of Article 26 must be strictly construed according to its literal text, which states that divorce must be "validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse." The government contended that if the Filipino spouse initiated the foreign divorce proceeding, the resulting decree could not be recognized in the Philippines because Article 15 of the Civil Code strictly prohibits Filipinos from securing divorces.

The Supreme Court En Banc rejected this argument as constitutionally untenable. Writing for the majority, Justice Diosdado M. Peralta held that the equal protection clause requires all persons similarly situated to be treated alike, both in privileges conferred and liabilities imposed. To create a legal distinction between a Filipino spouse whose alien partner filed for divorce and a Filipino spouse who initiated the foreign proceeding produces an arbitrary classification with no rational basis in law or policy.

The Supreme Court observed that in both instances, the foreign decree produces the exact same legal reality abroad. The alien spouse is legally freed from the marriage and capacitated to remarry under foreign law. In both instances, the Filipino spouse is left without a functioning marital partner. To grant legal freedom to one Filipino while keeping the other bound solely based on who filed the foreign petition constitutes arbitrary discrimination. The Supreme Court declared that such a distinction exalts literalism over substance and inflicts severe injustice on Philippine nationals.

### 2.3 The Protection of Human Dignity and the Right to Marry

The constitutional framework also safeguards individual human dignity and the fundamental liberty to seek personal happiness. In *Republic v. Manalo*, the Supreme Court emphasized that the right to marry is a basic civil right that touches the core of human liberty. While the State regulates marriage, it cannot exercise its regulatory authority in a manner that degrades human dignity by compelling an individual to remain legally joined to a non-existent union.

When an alien spouse obtains a divorce abroad, that spouse is at liberty to contract another marriage. If Philippine law refuses to recognize the decree simply because the Filipino spouse participated in the foreign proceedings, the Filipino spouse is condemned to perpetual legal limbo. That spouse cannot remarry in the Philippines, cannot establish a legitimate second family, and risks criminal prosecution if they attempt to move on with their life.

The Supreme Court highlighted that such an interpretation turns the law into an instrument of oppression. By recognizing foreign divorces regardless of which spouse initiated the petition, Article 26(2) aligns statutory family law with the constitutional guarantee of human dignity and liberty.

### 2.4 Inviolable Social Institution Versus Hollow Legal Fiction

The Supreme Court has repeatedly clarified that the constitutional protection accorded to marriage applies to real and living unions, not to hollow legal shells. When a marriage has been dissolved by a foreign decree validly recognized under the foreign spouse's national law, the marital union is irrevocably dead. No domestic legislation can force the foreign spouse to return to the Philippines, resume cohabitation, observe fidelity, or provide mutual emotional support.

To insist that the marriage continues to exist for the Filipino spouse while it has ceased to exist for the alien spouse transforms the constitutional protection of marriage into an absurd legal fiction. As the Supreme Court observed in *Manalo*, a prohibitive view of Paragraph 2 of Article 26 does more harm than good. If the law disallows a Filipino from remarrying after an alien spouse has been freed abroad, the law actively discourages legal marriages and instead forces abandoned Filipinos into illicit and informal relationships.

Furthermore, any children born to the Filipino spouse in a subsequent relationship would be branded illegitimate under Philippine law, despite the fact that their parents acted in good faith after a foreign divorce. By providing a clear avenue for the recognition of foreign divorces, Article 26(2) preserves public order and ensures that subsequent families formed by Filipino citizens are legitimate and stable.

### 2.5 Secular Matrimonial Jurisdiction and Public Policy Against Domestic Divorce

The application of Article 26 operates strictly within secular legal jurisdiction and does not compromise the State policy against establishing a domestic divorce law. The Supreme Court emphasized in *Manalo* and *Republic v. Ng*[^6] that Philippine courts do not grant absolute divorces. The power to grant a divorce belongs exclusively to the foreign sovereign acting within its own territory under its own laws.

Article 26(2) functions merely as a private international law rule of recognition. It does not introduce divorce into the domestic legal system; rather, it extends recognition to the legal effects of a foreign judgment already validly rendered abroad. The Supreme Court underscored that this limited recognition does not undermine the constitutional family policy because the policy against domestic divorce was designed to govern marriages between Filipino citizens. When a marriage involves an alien, the legal status of that union is necessarily subject to the personal laws of two distinct sovereigns. Article 26 reconciles these conflicting sovereign jurisdictions with justice and equity.


## 3. Doctrinal Evolution Across Historical Eras

The jurisprudence governing Article 26 spans four decades, evolving across three distinct doctrinal eras: the Foundational Era (1985–2005), the Restrictive Era (2006–2017), and the Modern Governing Era (2018–2026). This evolution reflects a steady progression from strict literalism to equitable, purposive statutory interpretation grounded in constitutional equal protection.

### 3.1 The Foundational Era (1985–2005)

The foundational era established the fundamental premise that a foreign divorce obtained by an alien spouse releases both parties from marital obligations. Beginning with *Van Dorn v. Romillo, Jr.* in 1985, the Supreme Court recognized that the nationality principle cannot be applied in an asymmetrical manner that penalizes Filipino citizens.

In *Pilapil v. Ibay-Somera* (1989),[^7] the Supreme Court applied the *Van Dorn* doctrine to criminal law. A German husband obtained an absolute divorce against his Filipino wife in Germany and later filed criminal charges for adultery against her in the Philippines. The Supreme Court ordered the dismissal of the criminal complaint, ruling that under Article 344 of the Revised Penal Code, a criminal action for adultery can only be commenced by the offended spouse. Because the foreign divorce had severed the marital relationship, the German national was no longer a husband and had no legal standing to institute criminal adultery proceedings.

In *Garcia v. Recio* (2001),[^8] the Supreme Court established the foundational evidentiary rules governing foreign divorce. The Supreme Court held that Philippine courts cannot take judicial notice of foreign laws and judgments. A party pleading a foreign divorce decree must allege and prove both the fact of the divorce and the foreign law capacitating the foreign spouse to remarry under Rule 132 of the Rules of Court.

In *Roehr v. Rodriguez* (2003),[^9] the Supreme Court recognized that while foreign divorce decrees affecting marital status are entitled to recognition, ancillary matters such as child custody, support, and property division remain subject to independent judicial scrutiny under Philippine law.

The foundational era culminated in the landmark En Banc ruling in *Republic v. Orbecido III* (2005). In that case, two Filipino citizens were married in the Philippines in 1981. In 2000, the wife became a naturalized American citizen, obtained a divorce decree in California, and remarried an American. The Filipino husband petitioned for authority to remarry under Article 26(2). The Office of the Solicitor General opposed, arguing that Article 26(2) applies only to marriages that were mixed at the time of solemnization.

The Supreme Court En Banc rejected the government's position. Writing for the unanimous Court, Justice Leonardo A. Quisumbing formulated the classic twin elements of Article 26(2): (1) a valid marriage celebrated between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner; and (2) a valid divorce obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry. The Supreme Court held that the reckoning point for foreign citizenship is not the date of marriage celebration, but the date when the valid divorce is obtained abroad. However, on the record before it, the Supreme Court set aside the trial court's decision because Cipriano had presented no competent evidence proving his wife's naturalization, the foreign divorce decree, and the foreign law capacitating her to remarry, establishing that judicial declaration of capacity could only be granted upon formal proof of those facts.

Shortly before *Orbecido*, the Supreme Court decided *Republic v. Iyoy* (2005).[^10] In *Iyoy*, the Filipino wife left for the United States, obtained a foreign divorce in 1984 or 1985, and married an American citizen. However, in her answer before the trial court, she admitted that she became a naturalized American citizen only in 1988. The Supreme Court made an express factual finding that at the time she obtained the divorce, she was still a Filipino citizen. Pursuant to the nationality principle in Article 15 of the Civil Code, she remained bound by Philippine laws prohibiting divorce; hence, the foreign divorce was void in the Philippines and Article 26(2) could not apply. The Supreme Court also ruled that abandonment and foreign divorce do not per se constitute psychological incapacity under Article 36.

### 3.2 The Restrictive Era (2006–2017)

Following *Orbecido*, jurisprudence entered a restrictive phase characterized by strict literal interpretation of the phrase "divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse." During this period, trial courts, appellate courts, and the Office of the Solicitor General took the position that Paragraph 2 of Article 26 provided relief *only* if the foreign spouse was the sole moving party who initiated and procured the divorce decree abroad.

Under this restrictive doctrine, if a Filipino spouse filed for divorce abroad, or if the spouses obtained an administrative divorce by mutual agreement (such as the Japanese *kyogi rikon*), Philippine courts dismissed the recognition petition. The courts reasoned that allowing a Filipino spouse to initiate or consent to a divorce abroad violated Article 15 of the Civil Code.

Despite this prevailing restriction, the Supreme Court achieved important procedural breakthroughs during this era. In *Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas* (2010),[^11] a naturalized Canadian citizen filed a petition to recognize his Canadian divorce in the Philippines so he could remarry another Filipina. The trial court dismissed the petition, ruling that Article 26(2) is available only to Filipino spouses. The Supreme Court qualified this ruling. Justice Arturo D. Brion explained that while Article 26(2) confers substantive capacity to remarry solely upon the Filipino spouse, an alien spouse possesses undeniable legal standing to petition for the recognition of a foreign divorce decree under Rule 39, Section 48 of the Rules of Court. The Supreme Court also established that an action for recognition of a foreign judgment may be consolidated with a petition for cancellation or correction of entries in the civil registry under Rule 108.

In *Fujiki v. Marinay* (2013),[^12] the Supreme Court En Banc resolved whether a husband in a prior, subsisting marriage had legal standing to petition for the judicial recognition of a foreign judgment from a Japanese family court declaring his wife's subsequent marriage to another Japanese national void on the ground of bigamy. The trial court had dismissed the petition under A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, which limits petitions for declaration of absolute nullity to husbands and wives of the challenged marriage. Writing for the unanimous Court, Senior Associate Justice Antonio T. Carpio held that A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC applies strictly to direct actions for declaration of nullity under Articles 35, 36, and 45 of the Family Code, and has no application to petitions for recognition of foreign judgments under Rule 39, Section 48. The Supreme Court confirmed that any interested party may petition for recognition of a foreign judgment, including a foreign decree declaring a marriage void, and that such recognition may be litigated within a special proceeding under Rule 108.

In *Medina v. Koike* (2016),[^13] a Filipino wife and her Japanese husband jointly obtained an administrative divorce by mutual agreement before the Mayor of Ichinomiya City, Aichi Prefecture, Japan. Seeking annotation of the divorce on her marriage certificate, the wife petitioned the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City for judicial recognition of foreign divorce and declaration of capacity to remarry. The trial court denied the petition outright on the ground that the petitioner failed to prove Japanese divorce law under Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25. On direct appeal under Rule 45, the Supreme Court recognized that the appeal raised questions of fact, but pursuant to Rule 56, Section 6, the Supreme Court referred the case to the Court of Appeals for appropriate action, including the reception of evidence to determine and resolve the factual issues, rather than dismissing the appeal, establishing the policy of liberality to prevent substantive injustice.

### 3.3 The Modern Governing Era / The Watershed Paradigm Shift (2018–2026)

The modern governing era began on April 24, 2018, when the Supreme Court En Banc promulgated its landmark decision in *Republic v. Manalo*. Marelyn Tanedo Manalo, a Filipino citizen, married Yoshino Minoro, a Japanese national, in the Philippines in 1998. Manalo later filed for divorce in Japan and secured a Japanese court judgment decreeing the dissolution of their marriage. Manalo then filed a petition in the Regional Trial Court of Dagupan City for judicial recognition of the foreign divorce decree and cancellation of her marriage entry.

The trial court denied the petition on the ground that Article 26(2) applies only when the alien spouse obtains the divorce. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that the provision should be interpreted broadly to avoid injustice. The Office of the Solicitor General elevated the case to the Supreme Court En Banc.

The Supreme Court En Banc affirmed the Court of Appeals and established the watershed doctrine. The Supreme Court held that Paragraph 2 of Article 26 applies to all mixed marriages where a valid foreign divorce has been obtained capacitating the alien spouse to remarry, **regardless of who initiated the divorce proceeding**. The Supreme Court expressly rejected the restrictive literal interpretation, holding that:
1. The text of Article 26(2) does not mandate that the alien spouse must be the petitioner; the word "obtained" should be understood broadly to mean that a valid divorce exists under the alien's national law;
2. Applying the statute to permit remarriage only when the alien files the petition discriminates against Filipino citizens without a rational basis, violating the equal protection clause of the Constitution;
3. The purpose of the law is to remedy the anomaly of an abandoned Filipino spouse tied to a non-existent marriage while the foreign partner is free to remarry abroad; and
4. Whether the Filipino spouse filed the divorce, the alien spouse filed it, or both spouses agreed to it, the legal consequence abroad is identical: the foreign spouse is capacitated to remarry.

Following *Manalo*, the Supreme Court unleashed a consistent stream of decisions applying and expanding the new doctrine. In *Racho v. Tanaka* (2018),[^14] the Supreme Court ruled that a Japanese divorce by agreement (*kyogi rikon*) falls squarely within Article 26(2), recognizing the agency of the Filipino spouse to agree to an amicable out-of-court dissolution. In *Morisono v. Morisono* (2018),[^15] *Juego-Sakai v. Republic* (2018),[^16] and *Nullada v. Civil Registrar of Manila* (2019),[^17] the Supreme Court consistently granted recognition to foreign divorces initiated or agreed to by Filipino citizens.

In *Galapon v. Republic* (2020),[^18] the Supreme Court, speaking through Justice Alfredo Benjamin S. Caguioa, synthesized the modern doctrine into the authoritative **Tripartite Formulation**. The Supreme Court declared that pursuant to *Manalo*, Article 26(2) applies to mixed marriages where the foreign divorce decree is:
1. Obtained solely by the foreign spouse;
2. Obtained jointly by the Filipino and foreign spouse; or
3. Obtained solely by the Filipino spouse.

In *Kondo v. Civil Registrar General* (2020),[^19] the Supreme Court underscored that substantive justice must prevail over procedural technicalities, directing the liberal reception of evidence proving foreign divorce laws. In *Abel v. Rule* (2021),[^20] the Supreme Court reaffirmed the *Orbecido* doctrine within the modern *Manalo* framework, confirming that a former Filipino who naturalizes abroad and secures a divorce frees the remaining Filipino spouse to remarry.

In *Johansen v. Office of the Civil Registrar General* (2021),[^21] the Supreme Court reaffirmed that an action for recognition of a foreign judgment under Rule 39 and a petition for cancellation of civil registry entries under Rule 108 may be joined in a single judicial proceeding. In *Republic v. Bayog-Saito* (2022),[^22] the Supreme Court affirmed the recognition of a consensual Japanese divorce obtained by mutual agreement.

In *Tsutsumi v. Republic* (2023),[^23] the Supreme Court addressed modern proof of foreign public documents, holding that an Apostille certificate issued under the 1961 Hague Apostille Convention fully satisfies the authentication requirements of the Rules of Court, replacing consular authentication.

In *Republic v. Ng* (2024),[^6] the Supreme Court En Banc delivered another milestone ruling. Speaking through Justice Japar B. Dimaampao, the Supreme Court confirmed that Paragraph 2 of Article 26 applies to administrative out-of-court divorces obtained by mutual agreement (*kyogi rikon*). However, the Supreme Court reversed the trial court's recognition order and remanded the case because the petitioner had submitted unauthenticated photocopies of the Japanese Civil Code, failing to satisfy Rule 132. The Court also held that while the compilation in OCA Circular No. 157-2022-A is a helpful reference tool, it does not dispense with the requirement to formally plead and prove foreign law in compliance with Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25.

Finally, in *De Munari v. Asprec* (2025),[^24] the Supreme Court clarified the operation of Paragraph 1 of Article 26, holding that Philippine Family Courts possess subject-matter jurisdiction to declare the nullity of foreign-celebrated marriages involving Filipino citizens where intrinsic legal capacity is violated.

## 4. Paragraph 1: Marriages Solemnized Abroad (Lex Loci Celebrationis) and Public Policy Exceptions

### 4.1 The General Rule of Validation: Lex Loci Celebrationis

Paragraph 1 of Article 26 codifies the classic conflict-of-laws doctrine of lex loci celebrationis, also known as the rule of validation. The statute declares that all marriages solemnized outside the Philippines, in accordance with the laws in force in the country where they were solemnized, and valid there as such, shall also be valid in this country.

The policy underpinning this rule is international stability and the protection of marital expectations. When individuals travel across borders and celebrate marriage under the authority of local foreign sovereigns, private international law presumes that the union is legally binding everywhere. To hold otherwise would disrupt family stability, cast uncertainty upon legitimacy, and create chaotic legal relations across international frontiers.

Under this principle, the formal and extrinsic validity of a marriage is tested strictly by the law of the place of celebration. If the foreign law where the ceremony occurred does not require a marriage license, dispenses with formal witnesses, or authorizes marriage solemnization by ship captains or public notaries, Philippine law recognizes the marriage as formally valid, even though such formalities would be defective if the wedding took place within the Philippines.

### 4.2 Explicit Statutory Exceptions Under Paragraph 1

The general rule of validation under Paragraph 1 is not absolute. The statute contains an express reservation subjecting foreign marriages to the overriding public policy of the Philippines. Specifically, Paragraph 1 provides that foreign marriages shall not be recognized as valid in the Philippines if they fall under any of the following prohibitive provisions:

1. **Underage Marriages (Article 35, Paragraph 1):** Marriages contracted by any party below eighteen years of age, even with parental consent. While certain foreign jurisdictions may authorize marriage at sixteen or seventeen years of age, Philippine law considers age sixteen or seventeen an absolute impediment for Filipino citizens under the nationality principle.
2. **Bigamous or Polygamous Marriages (Article 35, Paragraph 4):** Bigamous or polygamous marriages not falling under the exceptional exception of presumptive death in Article 41. Even if a marriage is celebrated in a country that recognizes polygamy (such as certain Islamic or traditional legal systems), a polygamous marriage involving a non-Muslim Filipino citizen is void ab initio in the Philippines.
3. **Mistake as to Identity (Article 35, Paragraph 5):** Marriages contracted through mistake by one contracting party as to the physical identity of the other.
4. **Failure to Record Partition and Legitimes (Article 35, Paragraph 6):** Subsequent marriages contracted by a person whose prior marriage was annulled or declared void, but who failed to record the judgment of annulment, partition of property, and delivery of children's presumptive legitimes in the civil registry as required by Article 53.
5. **Psychological Incapacity (Article 36):** Marriages contracted by any party who, at the time of the celebration, was psychologically incapacitated to comply with the essential marital obligations of marriage, even if such incapacity manifests after solemnization.
6. **Incestuous Marriages (Article 37):** Marriages between ascendants and descendants of any degree, whether legitimate or illegitimate, and between brothers and sisters, whether of the full or half blood.
7. **Marriages Void for Public Policy (Article 38):** Void collateral unions, including marriages between collateral blood relatives within the fourth civil degree (such as first cousins); step-parents and step-children; parents-in-law and children-in-law; adopting parent and adopted child; the surviving spouse of the adopting parent and the adopted child; the surviving spouse of the adopted child and the adopter; adopted children of the same adopter; and parties where one, with the intention to marry the other, killed that other person's spouse or their own spouse.

### 4.3 Interplay with the Nationality Principle

The exceptions enumerated in Paragraph 1 must be read in close conjunction with Article 15 of the Civil Code. Under the nationality principle, Philippine laws relating to family rights and duties, status, condition, and legal capacity follow Filipino citizens wherever they may go.

When a Filipino citizen marries abroad, the formal requirements of the ceremony (such as the solemnizing officer, marriage ceremony, and marriage license) are governed by the foreign lex loci celebrationis. However, the substantive legal capacity of the Filipino citizen to marry is governed exclusively by Philippine law.

Consequently, if a Filipino citizen marries their first cousin abroad in a jurisdiction that permits first-cousin marriages (such as certain states in the United States), that marriage is completely void ab initio under Philippine law pursuant to Article 38(1) in relation to Article 26(1). The foreign lex loci celebrationis cannot override the explicit personal law of the Filipino citizen.

### 4.4 Intrinsic Validity Versus Extrinsic Formalities: De Munari v. Asprec

The Supreme Court delivered an authoritative clarification on the distinction between extrinsic formalities and intrinsic validity in *De Munari v. Asprec* (2025). In that case, Gianni de Munari, an Italian citizen, filed a complaint for declaration of absolute nullity of marriage against Thelma Gagui Asprec, a Filipino citizen, before the Family Court of Puerto Princesa City. The couple had celebrated their marriage abroad in Italy.

The Regional Trial Court motu proprio dismissed the complaint for lack of jurisdiction, ruling that under Article 17 of the Civil Code, the forms and solemnities of contracts are governed by the laws of the country where they are executed. The trial court reasoned that because the marriage was celebrated in Italy, Italian courts had exclusive jurisdiction to determine its validity.

The Supreme Court reversed the trial court and reinstated the complaint. Writing for the Third Division, Justice Japar B. Dimaampao explained that the trial court confused formal validity with intrinsic validity. The Supreme Court declared that when a marriage is celebrated elsewhere, its validity does not depend fully on foreign law. While accepted in the jurisdiction where it is celebrated, it may be held invalid in the Philippines when it violates public policy exceptions, such as bigamy.

Irrespective of the place of solemnization, Philippine laws bind the contracting Filipino citizen with respect to family rights and legal capacity. The Supreme Court held that Philippine Family Courts possess subject-matter jurisdiction to adjudicate petitions for declaration of nullity of foreign marriages involving Filipino citizens, and directed the trial court to proceed with the trial on the merits.

### 4.5 Same-Sex Marriages Celebrated Abroad

A prominent contemporary application of Paragraph 1 involves same-sex marriages validly solemnized in foreign countries. In jurisdictions such as the United States, Canada, and various European nations, same-sex marriage is fully lawful.

However, under Philippine domestic law, marriage is expressly defined in Article 1 of the Family Code as a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman. Correlatively, Article 2 of the Family Code lists legal capacity of the contracting parties, who must be a male and a female, as an indispensable essential requisite. Under Article 4 of the Family Code, the absence of an essential requisite renders the marriage void ab initio.

When a Filipino citizen enters into a same-sex marriage abroad, that marriage cannot be recognized in the Philippines. Under Article 15 of the Civil Code and the public policy reservation of Article 17, paragraph 3, the Filipino citizen lacks the substantive legal capacity to enter into a matrimonial union with a person of the same sex. In *Falcis v. Civil Registrar General* (2019),[^25] the Supreme Court reaffirmed that the definition and institutional framework of marriage under the Family Code remain anchored on heterosexual unions until amended by the Legislature.

### 4.6 Consular Marriages Under Article 10

Article 26(1) must also be distinguished from consular marriages solemnized abroad under Article 10 of the Family Code. Article 10 provides that marriages between Filipino citizens celebrated abroad may be solemnized by a consul-general, consul, or vice-consul of the Republic of the Philippines.

In a consular marriage, both contracting parties must be Filipino citizens. Because the ceremony is solemnized abroad by a Philippine diplomatic or consular officer empowered by domestic statute, the marriage is governed not by the foreign lex loci celebrationis, but solely and exclusively by Philippine domestic law. The consular officer performs all the duties of the local civil registrar, including the issuance of the marriage license and the formal recording of the marriage.


## 5. Paragraph 2: Substantive Requisites of the Modern Manalo Doctrine

### 5.1 The Two Substantive Requisites

In *Republic v. Orbecido III* (2005), the Supreme Court En Banc formulated the authoritative two-part test governing the application of Paragraph 2 of Article 26:
1. There is a valid marriage that has been celebrated between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner; and
2. A valid divorce is obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry.

Following the landmark En Banc ruling in *Republic v. Manalo* (2018) and its reaffirmation in *Galapon v. Republic* (2020), the second requisite was definitively recalibrated. The governing doctrine no longer requires that the foreign divorce be obtained exclusively by the alien spouse. Instead, the modern rule requires that a valid divorce has been obtained abroad that dissolves the marital tie and capacitates the alien spouse to remarry under their national law, irrespective of which spouse filed or procured the decree.

### 5.2 Requisite 1: Valid Marriage Between a Filipino and a Foreigner

The first requisite requires the existence of a valid marriage between a Filipino citizen and an alien. The marriage may have been solemnized either within the Philippines or in a foreign country. Article 26(2) contains no restriction regarding the geographical place of celebration. If solemnized in the Philippines, the formal validity of the marriage is governed by Philippine law. If solemnized abroad, its formal validity is governed by the foreign lex loci celebrationis under Paragraph 1.

The crucial inquiry under the first requisite is the citizenship of the parties. At the time of the foreign divorce, one spouse must be a Filipino citizen and the other must be an alien.

### 5.3 Post-Marriage Naturalization: The Orbecido Principle

A foundational question resolved by the Supreme Court En Banc in *Republic v. Orbecido III* was whether Article 26(2) applies if both parties were Filipino citizens when they got married, but one spouse subsequently acquired foreign citizenship prior to the divorce.

The Supreme Court ruled that Article 26(2) applies with full force to post-marriage naturalization scenarios. Writing for the En Banc Court, Justice Quisumbing explained that the reckoning point for foreign citizenship is not the citizenship of the parties at the time of the marriage ceremony, but their citizenship at the time the valid foreign divorce is obtained abroad. However, on the record before it in *Orbecido*, the Supreme Court set aside the trial court's decision declaring Cipriano capacitated to remarry because the record lacked competent proof of the wife's naturalization, the foreign divorce decree, and the foreign law allowing it, establishing that capacity to remarry cannot be declared on bare allegations without formal proof.

The Supreme Court reasoned that the legislative purpose of Article 26(2) is to prevent the absurdity where a Filipino spouse remains bound to an alien partner who is legally single under foreign law. When a Filipino spouse naturalizes abroad, that spouse sheds Philippine citizenship and becomes an alien subject to a foreign personal law. If that naturalized alien subsequently obtains a valid foreign divorce, the former marriage is dissolved under the alien's national law. The remaining Filipino spouse suffers the identical hardship as a Filipino who married a foreigner from the outset. To deny relief based solely on the date of naturalization would sanction injustice and contravene the clear intent of the law.

This doctrine was reaffirmed in *Abel v. Rule* (2021). In *Abel*, two Filipinos married in Manila in 1999. In 2008, the wife became a naturalized United States citizen. In 2014, she secured a divorce decree in Nevada. The Filipino husband petitioned for recognition under Article 26(2). The Supreme Court granted recognition, confirming that post-marriage naturalization followed by a foreign divorce fully triggers the capacity of the Filipino spouse to remarry under Philippine law.

### 5.4 Alien Spouse Procuring Divorce Bound by National Law and Estopped: The Bayot Doctrine

The legal effect of a foreign divorce obtained by an alien spouse who was a foreign citizen at the time of marriage was addressed in *Bayot v. Court of Appeals* (2008).[^26]

In *Bayot*, Vicente Bayot (a Filipino) and Rebecca Makapugay were married in Mandaluyong in 1979. On its face, the Marriage Certificate identified Rebecca as an American citizen born in Agaña, Guam, to American parents. In 1996, Rebecca initiated divorce proceedings before the Dominican Republic court, which rendered Civil Decree No. 362/96 dissolving the marriage and Civil Decree No. 406/97 approving their property settlement agreement. In 2001, Rebecca filed a petition in the Regional Trial Court of Muntinlupa City for declaration of absolute nullity of marriage and support pendente lite against Vicente, claiming she was a Filipino citizen and that the Dominican divorce had no legal effect.

The Supreme Court dismissed Rebecca's petition for declaration of nullity and support. Writing for the Second Division, Justice Presbitero J. Velasco, Jr. held that Rebecca was an American citizen at the time of the marriage and when she procured the Dominican divorce. Under Article 15 of the Civil Code, Rebecca's legal status and capacity were governed by her American personal law, which recognized the absolute dissolution of the marriage. Rebecca was legally estopped by her representations before the foreign court from impeaching the validity of the foreign divorce decree that she herself had sought and obtained. Having dissolved the marriage bond, she could no longer maintain an action for declaration of nullity or demand spousal support in Philippine courts against her former Filipino husband.

### 5.5 Both Spouses Remaining Filipino at the Time of Divorce: Inapplicability

In sharp contrast, Paragraph 2 of Article 26 does NOT apply if both spouses remain Filipino citizens at the time the foreign divorce decree is obtained. This principle was firmly settled in *Republic v. Iyoy* (2005) and reiterated in *Republic v. Manalo* (2018).

In *Iyoy*, the Filipino wife obtained a divorce decree in the United States while still holding Philippine citizenship. The Supreme Court ruled that the divorce decree was completely void and without effect in the Philippines. Under Article 15 of the Civil Code, laws relating to legal capacity and status bind Filipino citizens even when residing abroad. Because Philippine domestic law does not permit absolute divorce, two Filipino citizens cannot confer jurisdiction upon a foreign court to dissolve their marital bond.

If both parties are Filipino citizens at the time of the foreign divorce, the decree is an absolute nullity under Philippine law. Neither spouse acquires capacity to remarry, and any subsequent marriage contracted by either spouse in the Philippines or abroad constitutes bigamy.

### 5.6 Dual Citizenship Under Republic Act No. 9225

The enactment of the Citizenship Retention and Re-acquisition Act of 2003 (Republic Act No. 9225) introduced the concept of dual citizenship. Under Republic Act No. 9225, natural-born Filipinos who become naturalized citizens of a foreign country may reacquire or retain Philippine citizenship upon taking the prescribed oath of allegiance.

The interaction between dual citizenship and Article 26(2) depends on the exact date of reacquisition:
1. If a natural-born Filipino naturalizes abroad, obtains a valid foreign divorce while holding foreign citizenship, and subsequently reacquires Philippine citizenship under Republic Act No. 9225, the foreign divorce remains valid. The subsequent reacquisition of Philippine citizenship does not retroactively undo the legal effects of a divorce that was validly obtained while the party was a foreign national.
2. Conversely, if a naturalized citizen reacquires Philippine citizenship first, and thereafter obtains a foreign divorce abroad, that divorce cannot be recognized under Article 26(2) because the party was a Filipino citizen at the time the divorce was procured, subject once again to the absolute prohibition of Article 15.

### 5.7 Requisite 2: Valid Foreign Divorce Capacitating Remarriage

The second requisite requires proof of two distinct legal elements:
1. The foreign divorce decree must be valid according to the foreign law governing the proceedings; and
2. The legal effect of the foreign divorce must be to dissolve the marital bond and capacitate the alien spouse to remarry under their national law.

As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Garcia v. Recio* (2001), presentation of the divorce decree alone is insufficient. The party seeking recognition must demonstrate that the foreign divorce is an absolute divorce (*a vinculo matrimonii*) that fully severs the marital tie, rather than a limited divorce or legal separation (*a mensa et thoro*) that merely suspends cohabitation while preserving the marital bond.

If the foreign decree is a conditional decree nisi, or if the foreign law imposes a waiting period before remarriage is permitted, the Filipino spouse does not acquire capacity to remarry until the foreign divorce becomes final, absolute, and operative to capacitate the alien spouse under foreign law.


## 6. The Scope of Foreign Divorces Recognized: Judicial, Administrative, and Consensual

### 6.1 Judicial Divorce Decrees

The most common form of foreign divorce recognized under Article 26(2) is an adversarial judicial decree rendered by a competent court of law in a foreign jurisdiction. Judicial divorces are typical in common law jurisdictions, such as the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and Australia.

In a judicial divorce, a formal judgment or decree of divorce is issued by a judge following notice and hearing. Philippine courts examine whether the foreign court had jurisdiction over the subject matter and the parties, and whether the defendant was afforded procedural due process. Under Rule 39, Section 48(b) of the Rules of Court, a foreign judicial judgment upon the title to a thing or the status of a person is presumptive evidence of a right as between the parties and their successors in interest, subject to impeachment only on grounds of want of jurisdiction, want of notice, collusion, fraud, or clear mistake of law or fact.

### 6.2 Administrative and Non-Judicial Divorces: Republic v. Ng (2024)

A major jurisprudential question that arose after *Manalo* was whether Article 26(2) is limited strictly to court judgments, or whether it extends to administrative and non-judicial dissolutions recognized as valid under foreign personal laws.

In *Republic v. Ng* (2024),[^6] the Supreme Court En Banc directly resolved this issue. Ruby Cuevas Ng, a Filipino citizen, and Akihiro Sono, a Japanese national, contracted marriage in Quezon City on December 8, 2004. They subsequently moved to Japan. On August 31, 2007, they obtained a divorce decree by mutual agreement in Japan, as attested by a Divorce Certificate issued by the Embassy of Japan and recorded in the Family Registry of Nakano-Ku, Tokyo. On May 28, 2018, Ng filed a petition for judicial recognition of foreign divorce and declaration of capacity to remarry before the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City. The trial court granted the petition in 2019. The Republic, through the Office of the Solicitor General, appealed, contending that Article 26(2) applies strictly to divorces decreed by a court of competent jurisdiction and cannot encompass divorces obtained by mere mutual agreement.

Writing for the Supreme Court En Banc, Justice Japar B. Dimaampao confirmed that Article 26(2) encompasses administrative out-of-court divorces. The Supreme Court conducted a rigorous textual and purposive analysis, holding that the statute requires only that the divorce be "validly obtained abroad." To insist that the divorce be decreed through an adversarial judicial trial inserts an unwritten qualification into the law. Applying the canon *ubi lex non distinguit nec nos distinguere debemos* (where the law does not distinguish, we ought not to distinguish), the Supreme Court ruled that foreign divorces validly effected through municipal registration or administrative recording are fully cognizable under Article 26(2).

Crucially, however, the Supreme Court REVERSED the trial court's judgment and REMANDED the case for further proceedings. While Ng had proved the fact of the divorce through consular and civil registry certificates, she failed to prove the Japanese law on divorce because she merely proffered an unauthenticated photocopy of the Japanese Civil Code and its English translation. Reaffirming the strict evidentiary requirements of Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25, the Supreme Court held that foreign divorce laws cannot be established by unauthenticated photocopies or by sole reliance on the compilation in OCA Circular No. 157-2022-A. Consistent with the policy of liberality in mixed-marriage cases, the Supreme Court remanded the case to allow Ng the opportunity to formally prove Japanese law.

### 6.3 Japanese Divorce by Mutual Agreement (Kyogi Rikon)

The recognition of non-judicial divorces has its most frequent application in marriages involving Japanese nationals. Japanese law recognizes four types of divorce:
1. Divorce by agreement (*kyogi rikon*), where the spouses execute a joint divorce notification and register it with the municipal office without court intervention;
2. Divorce by conciliation (*chotei rikon*), conducted before the Japanese Family Court;
3. Divorce by determination (*shimpan rikon*), rendered by the Family Court when conciliation fails but agreement is close; and
4. Divorce by judgment (*saiban rikon*), an adversarial court trial.

Over ninety percent of divorces in Japan are accomplished through divorce by agreement (*kyogi rikon*). Prior to 2018, the Office of the Solicitor General routinely objected to the recognition of *kyogi rikon*, arguing that consensual administrative divorces violate Philippine public policy.

The Supreme Court systematically dismantled this objection across a series of landmark rulings. In *Racho v. Tanaka* (2018), the Supreme Court recognized a Japanese divorce by agreement, holding that our courts cannot impose Philippine procedural litigation models on foreign legal systems. In *Morisono v. Morisono* (2018), *Juego-Sakai v. Republic* (2018), *Nullada v. Civil Registrar of Manila* (2019), and *Republic v. Bayog-Saito* (2022), the Supreme Court repeatedly confirmed that Japanese divorce by mutual agreement falls squarely within Article 26(2), provided that the divorce is proved through the Acceptance Certificate of Divorce (*Juri Shomeisho*) or the Family Register (*Koseki Tohon*) and the relevant provisions of the Japanese Civil Code are established according to our rules of evidence.

### 6.4 Mutual Consent Versus Domestic Collusion

A central justification previously advanced against recognizing consensual divorces was the rule against collusion. Under Article 48 of the Family Code and Rule on Declaration of Absolute Nullity (A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC), Philippine courts are strictly prohibited from rendering judgments based on stipulations of facts or confessions of judgment, and public prosecutors are mandated to investigate whether collusion exists between the spouses.

In *Republic v. Ng* (2024), the Supreme Court clarified that the domestic prohibition against collusion has no application to foreign divorces involving mixed marriages. The Supreme Court observed that the policy against collusion was instituted to prevent Filipino spouses from circumventing the statutory grounds for legal separation and nullity within the domestic legal system.

However, that policy finds no rational application when one spouse is an alien whose national law expressly permits divorce by mutual consent. In foreign jurisdictions that allow no-fault or consensual divorce, mutual agreement represents an enlightened, civilized method of resolving marital breakdown without public acrimony. A divorce by mutual agreement validly obtained under foreign law cannot be branded as unlawful collusion in the Philippines. As Chief Justice Alexander G. Gesmundo observed in his separate concurring opinion in *Ng*, the legal safeguard against collusion in domestic actions seeks to protect the institution of marriage between Filipinos, not to shackle a Filipino to an alien whose national law has already dissolved the bond.


## 7. Evidentiary Standards and Modes of Proof Under the Rules of Court

### 7.1 Prohibition Against Judicial Notice of Foreign Law and Judgments

Philippine courts do not take judicial notice of foreign laws and foreign judgments. As the Supreme Court held in *Garcia v. Recio* (2001) and reiterated in *Arreza v. Toyo* (2019)[^27] and *Republic v. Ng* (2024), foreign laws and foreign judgments are treated as questions of fact that must be properly pleaded and proved in accordance with the Philippine Rules of Court.

The doctrine of processual presumption governs when foreign law is involved. Under this doctrine, if a party fails to properly plead and prove a foreign law, Philippine courts will presume that the foreign law is identical to Philippine domestic law. Because Philippine domestic law does not provide for absolute divorce, the application of the processual presumption would lead to the dismissal of the petition. Consequently, the petitioner carries the affirmative burden of establishing two separate and distinct factual elements:
1. The fact of the foreign divorce decree or dissolution; and
2. The foreign national law of the alien spouse allowing divorce and capacitating that spouse to remarry.

### 7.2 Proving the Foreign Divorce Decree

To prove a foreign divorce decree, the petitioner must comply with Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25 of the Rules of Court. Section 24 governs the proof of official records of a foreign country, providing that a foreign public document may be evidenced by:
1. An official publication printed under governmental authority; or
2. A copy attested by the officer having the legal custody of the record, accompanied by a certificate that such officer has the custody.

Under Section 25, the attestation must state that the copy is a correct copy of the original or a specific part thereof, and must be under the official seal of the attesting officer or court.

If the foreign document is written in a foreign language (such as Japanese, Spanish, or German), it must be accompanied by an official English translation duly certified by a competent diplomatic or consular official or an authorized certified translator under Rule 132, Section 33.

### 7.3 Proving Foreign Law: Standards and Acceptable Materials

Proving the foreign law requires establishing the specific statutory provisions that empower the foreign court or authority to dissolve the marriage and authorize the spouses to remarry.

Philippine case law accepts several modes of proving foreign law:
1. **Official Publication:** An official statute book, gazette, or code published under the authority of the foreign government (Rule 132, Section 24);
2. **Attested Copy with Consular Certification or Apostille:** A copy of the foreign statute attested by the legal custodian of the foreign archives, accompanied by consular or Apostille authentication;
3. **Expert Witness Testimony:** Testimony of an attorney, legal scholar, or expert licensed to practice law in the foreign jurisdiction, who testifies under oath to the authenticity and contemporary interpretation of the foreign statutory provisions; or
4. **Official Foreign Government Certification:** A certificate issued by the foreign embassy, consulate, or ministry of justice attesting to the prevailing marriage and divorce laws of that country.

In *Arreza v. Toyo* (2019), the petitioner submitted a photocopy of the English translation of the Civil Code of Japan published by a private publishing firm. The Supreme Court held this evidence insufficient because a private publication is not an official government publication and was not attested by the legal custodian. The Supreme Court emphasized that foreign statutes cannot be proved through unofficial internet printouts or commercial compendiums without official certification.

### 7.4 The 1961 Hague Apostille Convention: Tsutsumi v. Republic (2023)

A major modernization in the evidentiary proof of foreign public documents occurred on May 14, 2019, when the 1961 Hague Convention Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents (the Apostille Convention) entered into force for the Philippines.

Prior to the Apostille Convention, a foreign public document had to undergo the multi-tiered legalization process popularly referred to as red ribbon authentication. The document had to be certified by the local foreign custodian, authenticated by the foreign ministry of foreign affairs, and finally legalized by the Philippine Embassy or Consulate in that foreign country.

Under the Apostille Convention, the requirement of diplomatic or consular legalization is completely abolished among contracting states. In *Tsutsumi v. Republic* (2023), the Supreme Court confirmed that for public documents originating from Apostille member countries (such as Japan, the United States, Australia, and European Union member states), an Apostille Certificate issued by the designated competent foreign authority (such as the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan or the Secretary of State in the United States) fully satisfies Rule 132, Section 24. Once an Apostille is affixed, Philippine courts must admit the foreign divorce decree or foreign statute without requiring consular authentication.

### 7.5 Status of OCA Circular No. 157-2022 and OCA Circular No. 157-2022-A

In 2022, the Office of the Court Administrator issued OCA Circular No. 157-2022, directing family courts to take judicial notice of a compilation of foreign marriage and divorce laws published on the Supreme Court website. This circular caused widespread confusion because it appeared to dispense with Rule 132 proof of foreign law.

In *Republic v. Ng* (2024), the Supreme Court resolved this confusion by tracing the administrative history of the circular. The Supreme Court noted that shortly after its release, OCA Circular No. 157-2022 was formally superseded and withdrawn by **OCA Circular No. 157-2022-A**, issued on July 7, 2022.

OCA Circular No. 157-2022-A eliminated the directive to take judicial notice. The amended circular clarifies that the compilation of foreign laws on marriage and divorce serves merely as an internal reference tool for judges in resolving recognition petitions, and remains strictly subject to prevailing jurisprudence and the evidentiary requirements of the Rules of Court. The Supreme Court held in *Ng* that the circular did not amend Rule 132, and parties must still formally plead and offer proof of foreign law during trial.

### 7.6 The Supreme Court's Generous Remand Policy

Recognizing the practical and financial hardships faced by ordinary Filipino citizens in securing foreign public documents and overseas authentications, the Supreme Court has adopted a notably generous remand policy in Article 26(2) cases.

When a Filipino petitioner presents prima facie evidence of a foreign divorce (such as an unauthenticated decree or an unofficial translation of foreign law) and the fact of the divorce is not contested by the Republic, the Supreme Court does not dismiss the petition with prejudice. Instead, the Supreme Court routinely remands the proceedings to the Regional Trial Court for the formal reception of properly authenticated evidence of the foreign decree and foreign law.

This policy of liberality was applied in *Medina v. Koike* (2016) (referring an improper Rule 45 appeal to the Court of Appeals under Rule 56, Section 6 for the reception of evidence on foreign law), as well as *Moraña v. Republic* (2019),[^28] *Kondo v. Civil Registrar General* (2020), and *Basa-Egami v. Bersales* (2022)[^29] (remanding to the trial court). In *Basa-Egami*, the Supreme Court lamented that the noble intent of the framers of Article 26(2) was often defeated by strict evidentiary rules, leaving Filipinos locked in marital limbo. The Supreme Court instructed trial courts to exercise flexibility and afford litigants every reasonable opportunity to complete the evidentiary record in the interest of substantial justice.

### 7.7 Evidentiary Weight of Notarized Documents: Anido v. Republic (2024)

In *Anido v. Republic* (2024),[^30] the Supreme Court addressed the evidentiary sufficiency of foreign statutory copies supported by notarized attestations. Rosary Kristine Anido, a Filipino physician, married Enrique Martin Gomez Pomar, a Peruvian citizen, in Parsippany, New Jersey, in 2012. After relocating their residence to Kentucky, Enrique petitioned for and obtained a decree of absolute divorce from the Fayette County Circuit Court of Kentucky in 2015. When Anido petitioned for judicial recognition in the Philippines, she submitted a certified copy of the divorce decree, together with copies of the Peru Civil Code and Kentucky marriage statutes accompanied by self-authenticating notarized attestations, contending that expert testimony was unnecessary. The trial court granted recognition, but the Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed her petition outright for failure to prove foreign law under Rule 132.

Writing for the Third Division, Justice Henri Jean Paul B. Inting affirmed that Anido's evidence failed to satisfy the required mode of proof. The Supreme Court explained that while notarized documents enjoy a presumption of regularity under Rule 132, Section 23 as to the fact of execution and date of notarization, a notary public does not possess legal custody of foreign statutes and cannot attest to their official status, ongoing validity, or substantive content. Because the divorce was decreed by a Kentucky state court pursuant to Kentucky law, Anido was obligated to prove the pertinent laws of Kentucky capacitating Enrique to obtain the divorce and remarry under Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25. Crucially, however, the Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals' outright dismissal. Applying the policy of liberality in mixed-marriage cases, the Supreme Court remanded the proceedings to the Court of Appeals for the reception of evidence on the pertinent Kentucky laws on marriage and divorce.


## 8. Procedural Governance: Actions for Recognition and Civil Registry Remedies

### 8.1 Procedural Vehicles for Invoking Article 26(2)

Under established Philippine jurisprudence, a party may invoke Article 26(2) through three distinct procedural vehicles:
1. **Special Civil Action for Recognition of Foreign Judgment:** A petition filed pursuant to Rule 39, Section 48 of the Rules of Court, seeking a judicial declaration that a foreign divorce judgment is entitled to full force and effect in the Philippines;
2. **Special Proceeding for Cancellation or Correction of Entries:** A petition filed pursuant to Rule 108 of the Rules of Court, seeking the cancellation or correction of civil registry entries relating to the marital status of the parties; or
3. **Collateral Invocation as an Affirmative Defense:** The invocation of the foreign divorce decree as an integral aspect of a claim or defense in a pending civil, criminal, or special proceeding, such as in a bigamy prosecution (*Sarto v. People*) or an estate settlement proceeding (*San Luis v. San Luis*).

### 8.2 Consolidation and Joinder of Rule 39 and Rule 108

For many years, procedural uncertainty persisted regarding whether a litigant was required to file two consecutive lawsuits: first, a petition under Rule 39 to recognize the foreign divorce decree, and second, an independent petition under Rule 108 to direct the civil registrar to cancel the marriage record.

The Supreme Court definitively eliminated this procedural burden in *Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas* (2010), *Fujiki v. Marinay* (2013), *Republic v. Cote* (2018),[^31] and *Johansen v. Office of the Civil Registrar General* (2021). The Supreme Court ruled that litigants are NOT required to file separate actions. The recognition of a foreign divorce decree and the cancellation or correction of civil registry entries may be joined and litigated in a single proceeding under Rule 108.

The Supreme Court explained that under Rule 1, Section 3 of the Rules of Court, a special proceeding is a remedy by which a party seeks to establish a status, a right, or a particular fact. Rule 108 provides an adversarial proceeding by which facts of public consequence, including marital status, are rectified. Because the recognition of a foreign divorce decree serves precisely to establish the change in civil status of the spouses, a Rule 108 petition before the Regional Trial Court serves as the ideal procedural vehicle to determine both the recognition of the foreign judgment and the corresponding registration in the civil registry.

### 8.3 Jurisdiction and Venue

Jurisdiction over petitions for recognition of foreign judgments affecting marital status resides exclusively in the Regional Trial Courts acting as Family Courts pursuant to Republic Act No. 8369 (The Family Courts Act of 1997). Section 5 of Republic Act No. 8369 confers upon Family Courts exclusive original jurisdiction over petitions for declaration of nullity, annulment of marriage, and matrimonial civil status actions.

Venue is governed strictly by Rule 108, Section 1 of the Rules of Court, which provides that the verified petition for cancellation or correction of entries must be filed in the Regional Trial Court of the province or city where the corresponding civil registry is located:
1. **Marriage Celebrated in the Philippines:** If the marriage was solemnized within the Philippines, the petition must be filed in the Regional Trial Court having territorial jurisdiction over the Local Civil Registry where the marriage certificate was originally recorded;
2. **Marriage Celebrated Abroad:** If the marriage was celebrated abroad, the foreign marriage is reported to and recorded with the Philippine Embassy or Consulate, and subsequently transmitted to the Philippine Statistics Authority through the City Civil Registry of Manila. In such cases, because the corresponding official civil registry record is deposited with the City Civil Registrar of Manila, venue properly lies before the Regional Trial Court of Manila.

### 8.4 Legal Standing of the Alien Spouse

While the substantive text of Article 26(2) explicitly mentions the capacity of the *Filipino spouse* to remarry, the Supreme Court has ruled that legal standing (*locus standi*) to petition for recognition of a foreign divorce is NOT limited to Filipino citizens.

In *Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas* (2010), the Supreme Court ruled that a foreign citizen possesses legal standing to petition for the recognition of a foreign divorce decree under Rule 39, Section 48. The Supreme Court reasoned that foreign judgments are entitled to presumptive validity under international comity. A foreigner who secured a valid divorce abroad has a direct, material, and substantial legal interest in ensuring that the foreign decree is recognized in the Philippines, particularly if the alien intends to marry another Filipino citizen.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Fujiki v. Marinay* (2013), where a Japanese national (the husband in a prior, subsisting marriage) was recognized as having legal standing to petition for the recognition of a foreign judgment from a Japanese family court declaring his Filipino wife's subsequent marriage to another Japanese national void on the ground of bigamy. Similarly, in *Ambrose v. Suque-Ambrose* (2021),[^32] the Supreme Court affirmed that an American spouse possesses legal personality to seek judicial recognition of an American divorce decree in Philippine courts.

### 8.5 Indispensable Parties and Mandatory Notices

Under Rule 108, Section 3 of the Rules of Court, all persons who have or claim any interest which would be affected by the petition must be made parties to the proceeding. In a petition for recognition of foreign divorce and cancellation of civil registry entries, the following are indispensable parties:
1. **The Former Spouse:** The foreign ex-spouse (or the other Filipino spouse in post-naturalization cases) must be impleaded as a party respondent and served with summons;
2. **The Local Civil Registrar:** The local civil registrar of the municipality or city where the marriage is registered; and
3. **The Civil Registrar General:** The Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA), represented by the Civil Registrar General.

Furthermore, under Section 4 of Rule 108, the trial court must issue an order fixing the time and place for hearing, and cause reasonable notice thereof to be given to the persons named in the petition. The court must also cause the order to be published once a week for three consecutive weeks in a newspaper of general circulation in the province.

Pursuant to Section 5 of Rule 108 and Section 35, Chapter 12, Title III, Book IV of the 1987 Administrative Code (Executive Order No. 292), service of the petition, court orders, and pleadings upon the **Office of the Solicitor General (OSG)** is mandatory. The OSG represents the Republic of the Philippines as the defender of the marital institution. The trial court must also direct the local public prosecutor to appear and participate in the proceedings on behalf of the State.

### 8.6 Inapplicability of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC

In *Fujiki v. Marinay* (2013), the Supreme Court established that the Rule on Declaration of Absolute Nullity of Void Marriages and Annulment of Voidable Marriages (A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC) does NOT govern petitions for recognition of foreign judgments.

The Supreme Court explained that A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC was promulgated to regulate actions seeking an original domestic declaration of nullity under Articles 35, 36, and 45 of the Family Code. Sections 2(a) and 3(a) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC limit the personality to sue exclusively to the husband or the wife of the union recognized by law.

In contrast, a petition to recognize a foreign judgment (whether dissolving a marriage or declaring it void ab initio) does not ask a Philippine court to render an original domestic declaration of nullity. The foreign marriage has already been dissolved or declared void by a foreign sovereign. The Philippine court acts solely as a court of recognition under Rule 39, Section 48 in relation to Rule 108. Therefore, the restrictive procedural rules of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC (such as the ban on third-party petitioners, mandatory pre-trial investigations for collusion, and strict residency rules) do not apply to recognition proceedings under Rule 39 and Rule 108.

### 8.7 Registration Requirements and Annotation in the Civil Registry

A foreign divorce decree cannot be directly annotated on a Philippine marriage certificate by administrative request. The Supreme Court emphasized in *Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas* that local civil registrars possess no quasi-judicial authority to determine the legal validity of a foreign court decree.

Direct registration of a foreign divorce without a court order is legally void. To effectuate the divorce on the civil register, the petitioner must complete the following sequential steps:
1. Secure a final and executory judgment from a Philippine Regional Trial Court recognizing the foreign divorce decree and ordering the civil registrar to annotate the recognition;
2. Secure a Certificate of Finality from the Regional Trial Court;
3. Present the court judgment, Certificate of Finality, and the authenticated foreign divorce decree to the Local Civil Registry having custody of the marriage record for registration under Act No. 3753 (The Civil Register Law);
4. Secure a Certificate of Registration from the Local Civil Registrar; and
5. Submit the registered court decree and local registration to the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) to annotate the marriage certificate and update the National Multi-Purpose Identification System / PSA database.

While substantive capacity to remarry arises from the valid foreign divorce upon final judicial recognition, the marginal annotation on the PSA Certificate of Marriage serves as the indispensable official record required by local civil registrars to issue a marriage license under Article 9 of the Family Code or a Certificate of No Marriage Record (CENOMAR) for subsequent nuptials.


## 9. Comparative Distinctions with Related Matrimonial Remedies

To understand the precise juridical placement of Article 26(2), it is necessary to contrast it with the domestic remedies available under the Family Code. While Article 26(2) operates exclusively within the realm of private international law and recognition of foreign acts, other remedies represent domestic substantive causes of action governed by strict statutory criteria.

### 9.2 Threshold Triggers and Nature of Cause

The fundamental distinction between Article 26(2) and domestic remedies lies in the source of dissolution. In an action under Article 36 (psychological incapacity), Article 45 (annulment), or Article 55 (legal separation), the cause of action arises entirely under Philippine domestic law. The petitioner must establish facts occurring at the time of marriage celebration (Articles 36 and 45) or during cohabitation (Article 55), proving fault, incapacity, or vice of consent before a Philippine judge.

In contrast, Article 26(2) involves no evaluation of marital fault or psychological dysfunction by Philippine courts. The threshold trigger is purely the existence of an external legal fact: that a foreign sovereign acting within its legitimate jurisdiction has dissolved the marital bond of an alien spouse. The Philippine court does not re-try the grounds of the divorce; it merely inspects the foreign decree to ensure that it was validly rendered and that the alien is capacitated to remarry under their personal law.

### 9.3 Status of the Marital Bond

Under Article 36, the judicial declaration of nullity operates retroactively to the date of celebration. In legal contemplation, the marriage was void from the beginning and never existed as a valid legal contract.

Under Article 45, an annulment operates prospectively. The marriage was valid from the wedding day until the decree of annulment became final and executory.

Under Article 55, legal separation does not sever the marital knot. The spouses are authorized to live separately, and their conjugal property is liquidated, but the marriage continues to exist. Neither spouse can remarry, and sexual intercourse with a third party continues to constitute adultery or concubinage.

Article 26(2) occupies a unique intermediate position. The marriage was validly celebrated and validly existed under Philippine law until the moment the foreign divorce decree became effective abroad. The decree operates prospectively to sever the marital tie, freeing the Filipino spouse from the duties of cohabitation, support, and fidelity, and restoring their capacity to contract a new marriage.

### 9.4 Parties with Legal Standing

In domestic nullity and annulment suits, A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC strictly confines legal standing to the husband or the wife. Under Section 2(a) of that Rule, third parties (including parents, children, heirs, or prior spouses) are barred from filing an original petition for declaration of absolute nullity of a void marriage.

In actions for recognition of foreign judgments affecting marital status, the Supreme Court in *Fujiki v. Marinay* (2013) (recognition of a foreign judgment declaring a bigamous marriage void) and *Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas* (2010) (recognition of a foreign divorce decree) explicitly held that the restrictive standing rules of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC do not apply. Any party who has a direct, material, and legal interest in the status of the marriage may petition for recognition of the foreign judgment under Rule 39, Section 48. This includes the alien spouse, the Filipino spouse, or a prior spouse whose subsisting marriage is aggrieved by a subsequent bigamous union.

### 9.5 Governing Prescriptions and Defenses

Domestic annulment suits under Article 45 are subject to strict five-year statutes of limitations under Article 47 of the Family Code. Similarly, an action for legal separation must be filed within five years from the occurrence of the cause under Article 57, and is subject to defenses of condonation, consent, connivance, mutual guilt, and prescription under Article 56.

In contrast, a petition for recognition of foreign divorce under Article 26(2) is imprescriptible. An action affecting civil status and legal capacity is not barred by the statute of limitations. A Filipino spouse may petition for recognition five, ten, or twenty years after the foreign divorce decree was rendered abroad. Furthermore, domestic defenses such as condonation or consent have no bearing on Article 26(2) because the foreign decree has already conclusively dissolved the union under foreign law.


## 10. Substantive Legal Consequences and Ancillary Effects

### 10.1 Restoration of Capacity to Remarry

The paramount statutory consequence of judicial recognition under Article 26(2) is the complete restoration of the Filipino spouse's legal capacity to remarry under Philippine law. Once the foreign divorce decree is judicially recognized by a Philippine Family Court, the marital bond is deemed severed in the eyes of Philippine law and the Filipino spouse is restored to the status of an unmarried individual with full capacity to remarry.

The Filipino spouse is legally restored to the status of an unmarried individual capable of contracting a subsequent marriage. When applying for a subsequent marriage license under Article 9 of the Family Code, the Filipino spouse presents the annotated Certificate of Marriage from the Philippine Statistics Authority and the judicial decree of recognition in lieu of an annulment or nullity decree.

### 10.2 Civil Status and Surname of the Filipino Spouse: Asilo v. Gonzales-Betic (2024)

Under Philippine law, marriage does not strip a woman of her maiden name; it merely grants her the legal option to use her husband's surname during the subsistence of the marriage under Article 370 of the Civil Code. In *Remo v. Secretary of Foreign Affairs* (2010),[^33] the Supreme Court confirmed that a married woman is not legally obligated to adopt her husband's surname, and retains the right to revert to her maiden name upon the termination of the marital bond.

In *Asilo v. Gonzales-Betic* (2024),[^34] the Supreme Court addressed whether a Filipino wife whose foreign divorce had been recognized under Article 26(2) could validly pray for the cancellation of her married surname and reversion to her maiden name in her Rule 108 petition. The trial court had granted the recognition of the Japanese divorce but refused to order the civil registrar to change her surname, claiming that a change of name required a separate proceeding under Rule 103.

The Supreme Court reversed the trial court and granted the reversion of surname. Writing for the Third Division, Justice Ramon Paul L. Hernando held that once a foreign divorce decree is recognized, the marital tie is dissolved, and the legal basis for the woman using her former husband's surname ceases to exist. Directing the civil registrar to record the reversion of the woman to her maiden name is a necessary, logical, and ancillary consequence of the judicial recognition of the foreign divorce, fully cognizable within the same Rule 108 proceeding.

### 10.3 Dissolution and Liquidation of Property Regimes

The recognition of a foreign divorce operates to dissolve the property regime of the spouses, whether Absolute Community of Property (Article 75) or Conjugal Partnership of Gains (Article 105). Upon the finality of the divorce decree abroad, the conjugal or community property is terminated and must be liquidated, partitioned, and distributed.

In *Roehr v. Rodriguez* (2003) and *Bayot v. Court of Appeals* (2008), the Supreme Court addressed the division of assets following an overseas divorce. Where the foreign divorce court incorporated an agreed property settlement executed by the parties, Philippine courts will generally enforce that settlement under principles of comity, provided that it does not violate Philippine public policy or prejudice the legitimes of common children.

However, where property situated within the Philippines is contested and was not validly adjudicated by the foreign tribunal, Philippine courts exercise exclusive in rem jurisdiction over the immovable assets. Under Article 16 of the Civil Code, real property as well as personal property is subject to the law of the country where it is situated (lex rei sitae). The liquidation and distribution of properties situated in the Philippines must be conducted in accordance with the Family Code rules on partition.

### 10.4 Filiation and Legitimacy of Children

The judicial recognition of a foreign divorce has no adverse effect on the filiation and legitimacy of children conceived or born prior to the dissolution of the marriage. Under Article 54 of the Family Code, children conceived or born of a valid marriage remain legitimate children of both parents, with full rights of support, succession, and custody.

Similarly, children conceived or born to the Filipino spouse in a subsequent marriage contracted after the recognition of the foreign divorce are legitimate children of that second marriage. This directly vindicates the constitutional purpose highlighted in *Manalo* and *Ng*: protecting innocent children from the stigma and legal disadvantages of illegitimacy.

### 10.5 Child Custody, Care, and Support: Roehr and Dacasin

While a foreign divorce decree dissolves the marital bond between the husband and the wife, it does not divest Philippine courts of jurisdiction over the custody, care, and support of minor children residing within the Philippines.

In *Roehr v. Rodriguez* (2003), the German court granted absolute divorce and awarded parental custody of the minor children to the German father. When the German father sought to enforce the custody award in the Philippines, the Filipino mother objected, asserting that the trial court could not grant custody without hearing her evidence. The Supreme Court sustained the mother. The Supreme Court held that while the status decree of divorce was entitled to recognition, the ancillary award of child custody was subject to independent evaluation by Philippine courts to determine whether the mother had been afforded due process.

In *Dacasin v. Dacasin* (2010),[^35] an American husband and a Filipino wife obtained a divorce in Illinois. The divorce decree incorporated an agreement granting sole custody to the mother. Later, the parents executed an agreement in Manila providing for joint custody. When the husband sought to enforce joint custody, the trial court dismissed the suit, citing lack of jurisdiction. The Supreme Court ruled that Philippine Family Courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over child custody cases involving residents. The Supreme Court established that under Article 213 of the Family Code, no child under seven years of age shall be separated from the mother unless the court finds compelling reasons. Agreements or foreign decrees conflicting with this statutory mandate cannot be enforced against the best interests of the child.

### 10.6 Unavailability of Moral or Exemplary Damages

A recurring question in matrimonial litigation is whether an abandoned Filipino spouse can recover moral and exemplary damages from an alien spouse who procured a foreign divorce.

Under the doctrine established in *Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals* (2005)[^36] and applied by analogy to Article 26(2), the Supreme Court has consistently held that damages are unavailable in actions arising from the dissolution of marriage. Marriage is a covenant of mutual affection, not an ordinary civil contract whose termination gives rise to damages in tort or breach of contract. A foreign citizen who exercises a legitimate legal right under their national law to seek a foreign divorce does not commit an unlawful act under Articles 19, 20, or 21 of the Civil Code. Consequently, moral and exemplary damages cannot be awarded merely because an alien spouse obtained a foreign divorce decree.

## 12. Intersections with Other Branches of Law

### 12.1 Criminal Law and the Crime of Bigamy: Sarto v. People (2018)

The most hazardous intersection of Article 26(2) occurs with criminal law, specifically the crime of bigamy penalized under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code. Article 349 imposes the penalty of prision mayor upon any person who contracts a second or subsequent marriage before the former marriage has been legally dissolved, or before the absent spouse has been declared presumptively dead by a judgment.

In *Sarto v. People* (2018),[^37] the Supreme Court established that a foreign divorce decree does NOT have automatic extraterritorial effect in the Philippines. A Filipino spouse who contracts a second marriage after an alien spouse obtains a foreign divorce, but **before securing a judicial decree of recognition from a Philippine court**, is criminally liable for bigamy.

Redante Sarto married Maria Socorro in 1984. Maria Socorro later naturalized as a Canadian citizen and obtained a divorce decree in British Columbia, Canada. Relying on the Canadian divorce, Sarto married Fe in the Philippines in 2000 without first petitioning for judicial recognition of the Canadian decree. When Sarto was subsequently prosecuted for bigamy, he asserted in defense that his first marriage was already dissolved by the Canadian divorce under Article 26(2) and the *Orbecido* doctrine.

The Supreme Court affirmed Sarto's conviction for bigamy. Justice Samuel R. Martires explained that like any foreign judgment, a foreign divorce decree must be presented to a Philippine court so that the court can inspect its authenticity, jurisdiction, and compliance with foreign law. Until a Philippine court renders a judgment recognizing the foreign decree, the first marriage remains subsisting in the eyes of Philippine criminal law. The subsequent marriage contracted without prior judicial recognition is bigamous.

The Supreme Court distinguished this rule from the defense of good faith. Under Article 3 of the Civil Code, ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. Sarto's mistaken belief that a foreign divorce operates automatically in the Philippines was an error of law, which cannot negate criminal intent in a malum in se offense.

### 12.2 Distinguishing Sarto from the En Banc Doctrine in Pulido v. People (2021)

It is critical to distinguish the *Sarto* doctrine from the landmark En Banc ruling in *Pulido v. People* (2021).[^38] In *Pulido*, the Supreme Court abandoned earlier jurisprudence and held that an accused in a bigamy case may raise the nullity of a prior or subsequent void ab initio marriage as a complete defense even in the absence of a prior judicial declaration under Article 40 of the Family Code.

The rule in *Pulido* applies strictly and exclusively to marriages that are **void ab initio** under Articles 35, 36, 37, and 38 of the Family Code. Because a void marriage is non-existent from the moment of celebration, there was no valid first marriage to support the charge of bigamy.

In contrast, a marriage dissolved by a foreign divorce under Article 26(2) was a perfectly **valid** marriage at its inception. It was celebrated with all legal requisites and remained valid until the foreign divorce was granted abroad. Because the dissolution of an initially valid marriage depends on the recognition of an external foreign judgment, *Pulido* does not exempt the party from obtaining a judicial recognition decree. Contracting a second marriage prior to judicial recognition of a foreign divorce remains punishable as bigamy under *Sarto*.

### 12.3 Crimes Against Chastity: Extinguishment of Standing in Adultery and Concubinage

Article 26(2) also intersects with the prosecution of private crimes under Title XI of the Revised Penal Code. Under Article 344 of the Revised Penal Code, crimes of adultery and concubinage cannot be prosecuted except upon a criminal complaint filed by the offended spouse.

In *Pilapil v. Ibay-Somera* (1989), the Supreme Court ruled that once an alien spouse secures a valid foreign divorce dissolving the marriage, that alien spouse ceases to be an offended spouse under criminal law. The status of an offended spouse is a jurisdictional prerequisite that must exist at the time the criminal complaint is filed in court. If the foreign spouse files a criminal complaint for adultery after having secured an absolute divorce abroad, the complaint must be dismissed for lack of legal standing, even if the alleged acts of infidelity occurred while the marriage was still subsisting.

### 12.4 Law on Succession and Estate Settlement: San Luis v. San Luis (2007)

Article 26(2) exerts profound effects on succession and the administration of estates under the Civil Code and the Rules of Special Proceedings. Under Section 6, Rule 78 of the Rules of Court, the surviving spouse enjoys statutory preference in the issuance of letters of administration over the estate of a deceased person.

In *San Luis v. San Luis* (2007), the children of a deceased governor argued that his surviving second wife had no legal personality to petition for letters of administration because the governor's prior marriage to an American citizen had been dissolved through a foreign divorce decree in Hawaii rather than a domestic court proceeding. The children contended that the second marriage was bigamous and void, leaving the second wife without legal standing.

The Supreme Court held that because the American wife had validly obtained a foreign divorce in Hawaii that dissolved the marriage and capacitated her to remarry under American law, the Filipino husband acquired capacity to remarry under Philippine law. The subsequent marriage to Felicidad was valid, and she was the lawful surviving spouse entitled to file the petition for letters of administration and claim her hereditary legitime under Article 999 of the Civil Code.

### 12.5 Private International Law and Conflict of Laws Principles

Article 26 represents a sophisticated harmonizing mechanism within Philippine private international law. It navigates the boundaries between four cardinal conflict-of-laws principles:
1. **The Nationality Principle (Lex Nationalii):** Preserved in Article 15 of the Civil Code, which binds Filipino citizens to Philippine personal laws, and respected by recognizing that alien spouses are equally governed by their own personal laws;
2. **The Rule of Validation (Lex Loci Celebrationis):** Codified in Article 26(1), which validates foreign marriages according to the law of the place of execution, subject to fundamental public policy exceptions;
3. **The Principle of Comity and Reciprocal Recognition:** Embodied in Rule 39, Section 48, which extends presumptive validity to foreign status judgments to avoid international friction and ensure that individuals do not suffer conflicting legal statuses across national borders; and
4. **The Public Policy Exception (Ordre Public):** Preserved in Article 17, paragraph 3 of the Civil Code, ensuring that foreign marriages and divorces that violate core societal imperatives (such as incest, non-age, or purely domestic divorces between Filipino citizens) are denied effect in the Philippines.


## 13. Comprehensive Jurisprudential Survey (Milestone Cases 1985–2025)

The following chronological survey synthesizes the decisive landmark rulings of the Supreme Court shaping the interpretation and enforcement of Article 26 across four decades:

### 13.1 Van Dorn v. Romillo, Jr., G.R. No. 68470, October 8, 1985
* **Ponente:** Justice Irene R. Cortes (First Division)
* **Facts:** Alice Reyes Van Dorn, a Filipino citizen, married Richard Upton, an American citizen, in Hong Kong in 1972. They established residence in the Philippines and had two children. Upton subsequently obtained an absolute divorce decree against Van Dorn in Nevada, United States. In 1983, Upton filed a civil suit in Pasay City, claiming that Van Dorn's business concern in Ermita, Manila, was conjugal property and demanding an accounting and management rights.
* **Issue:** Whether a foreign absolute divorce validly obtained abroad by an alien spouse can be recognized in the Philippines to bar that alien from asserting marital property rights against the Filipino spouse.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court dismissed Upton's complaint. The Supreme Court held that under Article 15 of the Civil Code, only Philippine nationals are bound by the domestic prohibition against absolute divorce. Under his personal law, Upton was validly divorced and was no longer the husband of Van Dorn. He possessed no legal standing to assert rights over conjugal assets. The Filipino spouse should not be discriminated against in her own country if justice is to be served.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Established the foundational principle that a foreign divorce obtained by an alien spouse releases both parties from marital ties, serving as the direct legislative impetus for Executive Order No. 227 and Paragraph 2 of Article 26.

### 13.2 Pilapil v. Ibay-Somera, G.R. No. 80116, June 30, 1989
* **Ponente:** Justice Florenz D. Regalado (Second Division)
* **Facts:** Imelda Pilapil, a Filipino citizen, married Erich Geiling, a German national, in Germany in 1979. In 1983, Geiling initiated divorce proceedings in Germany, and an absolute divorce decree was granted in 1986. More than five months after the divorce decree became final, Geiling filed two criminal complaints for adultery against Pilapil in Manila, alleging that she committed infidelity during the marriage.
* **Issue:** Whether an alien ex-spouse who obtained a valid foreign divorce has legal standing to file a criminal complaint for adultery as the offended spouse under Article 344 of the Revised Penal Code.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court ordered the criminal cases dismissed. The Supreme Court ruled that under Article 344 of the Revised Penal Code, a criminal prosecution for adultery requires a complaint filed by the offended spouse. The status of an offended spouse is an indispensable jurisdictional prerequisite that must exist at the time the criminal action is commenced. Because the German divorce had severed the marriage, Geiling was no longer the husband when he filed the suit.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Applied the foreign divorce doctrine to criminal law, confirming that dissolution of the marriage terminates the alien's status as an offended spouse for crimes against chastity.

### 13.3 Garcia v. Recio, G.R. No. 138322, October 2, 2001
* **Ponente:** Justice Artemio V. Panganiban (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Rederick Recio, a Filipino who became a naturalized Australian citizen, married Editha Samson in Australia in 1987. Samson obtained an Australian divorce in 1989. In 1994, Recio married Grace Garcia in the Philippines, declaring himself single. In 1998, Garcia filed a complaint for declaration of nullity on the ground of bigamy, alleging that Recio's prior marriage was still subsisting because the Australian divorce was not validly recognized in the Philippines.
* **Issue:** Whether a foreign divorce decree can be taken judicial notice of by Philippine courts without formal proof of foreign law.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court's dismissal and remanded the case. The Supreme Court held that Philippine courts cannot take judicial notice of foreign laws and foreign divorce decrees. The party pleading a foreign divorce must prove the decree as a fact and prove the alien's national law capacitating remarriage under Rule 132. Because Recio failed to present competent proof of Australian divorce law, the case was remanded for the reception of evidence.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Formulated the cardinal evidentiary rule that both the foreign divorce decree and the national law of the alien capacitating remarriage must be alleged and proved as facts.

### 13.4 Roehr v. Rodriguez, G.R. No. 142820, June 20, 2003
* **Ponente:** Justice Leonardo A. Quisumbing (Second Division)
* **Facts:** Wolfgang Roehr, a German national, married Maria Carmen Rodriguez, a Filipina, in Germany in 1980. They had two children. In 1997, Roehr obtained a decree of absolute divorce from a German court, which also awarded him parental custody of the children. Rodriguez subsequently filed a petition for declaration of nullity and child custody in Makati City. Roehr moved to dismiss, citing the German divorce decree. The trial court dismissed the action regarding the marital tie but granted partial reconsideration regarding child custody.
* **Issue:** Whether the recognition of a foreign divorce decree automatically extends to ancillary awards of child custody rendered by the foreign court.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court affirmed the trial court. The Supreme Court held that while the foreign divorce decree dissolving the marriage bond was valid and entitled to recognition, the ancillary award of child custody remained subject to independent determination by Philippine courts. Before giving res judicata effect to a foreign custody judgment under Rule 39, Section 48, the court must ensure that the adverse party was afforded due process and that the award satisfies the best interests of the child.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Separated status recognition under Article 26(2) from ancillary awards of child custody and support, confirming that Philippine courts retain sovereign jurisdiction over children residing in the country.

### 13.5 Republic v. Iyoy, G.R. No. 152577, September 21, 2005
* **Ponente:** Justice Minita V. Chico-Nazario (Second Division)
* **Facts:** Crasus L. Iyoy and Fely Ada Rosal-Iyoy, both Filipino citizens, were married in Cebu City in 1961 and had five children. In 1984, Fely left for the United States, obtained a divorce decree from an American court in 1984 or 1985, and married an American citizen in 1985. In her Answer before the trial court, Fely admitted that she became a naturalized American citizen only in 1988. In 1997, Crasus filed a petition for declaration of nullity of marriage under Article 36 of the Family Code, asserting Fely's abandonment, foreign divorce, and subsequent foreign remarriage as evidence of psychological incapacity.
* **Issue:** Whether Paragraph 2 of Article 26 applies to a foreign divorce obtained by a spouse who was still a Filipino citizen at the time of the divorce, and whether abandonment, infidelity, and foreign remarriage constitute psychological incapacity under Article 36.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the lower courts and sustained the validity and existence of the marriage. The Supreme Court made an express factual finding that at the time Fely obtained the foreign divorce decree, she was still a Filipino citizen, having naturalized only years later. Pursuant to the nationality principle in Article 15 of the Civil Code, she was strictly bound by Philippine laws prohibiting absolute divorce; hence, the foreign divorce had no legal effect in the Philippines and Article 26(2) was inapplicable. Furthermore, Fely's abandonment, infidelity, and bigamy warranted at most an action for legal separation under Article 55, but did not per se establish psychological incapacity under Article 36.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Confirmed that Article 26(2) does not apply where the spouse who procured the foreign divorce was still a Filipino citizen when the decree was obtained, firmly anchoring the nationality principle.

### 13.6 Republic v. Orbecido III, G.R. No. 154380, October 5, 2005
* **Ponente:** Justice Leonardo A. Quisumbing (En Banc)
* **Facts:** Cipriano Orbecido III and Lady Myros M. Villanueva, both Filipino citizens, were married in Ozamiz City in 1981 and had two children. In 1986, the wife left for the United States, was naturalized as an American citizen, and in 2000 obtained an absolute divorce decree in California, subsequently marrying an American. Cipriano filed before the Regional Trial Court of Molave, Zamboanga del Sur, a petition for authority to remarry under Article 26(2). The trial court granted the petition and declared him capacitated to remarry. The Republic, through the Office of the Solicitor General, petitioned the Supreme Court, contending that Article 26(2) applies exclusively to marriages that were mixed at the time of celebration.
* **Issue:** Whether Paragraph 2 of Article 26 applies to a marriage celebrated between two Filipino citizens when one spouse subsequently naturalizes as an alien and validly obtains a divorce abroad, and whether Cipriano could be declared capacitated to remarry on the record presented.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court En Banc held that Article 26(2) applies to post-marriage naturalizations. The reckoning point of citizenship is not the celebration of marriage, but the time a valid divorce is obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry. However, the Supreme Court GRANTED the Republic's petition and SET ASIDE the trial court's decision. The Court held that the record was completely bereft of competent evidence duly submitted concerning the naturalization of the wife, the foreign divorce decree, and the foreign law allowing it. A declaration of capacity to remarry could only be made upon respondent's formal proof of those essential facts.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Formulated the landmark twin elements of Article 26(2) and established the naturalization reckoning point, while underscoring that capacity to remarry cannot be granted on bare allegations without competent proof.

### 13.7 San Luis v. San Luis, G.R. No. 133743, February 6, 2007
* **Ponente:** Justice Consuelo Ynares-Santiago (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Governor Felicisimo San Luis of Laguna married Virginia (who died), then Merry Lee (an American citizen who obtained an absolute divorce in Hawaii in 1973), and finally Felicidad in 1974. Following the governor's death in 1992, Felicidad filed a petition for letters of administration as the surviving spouse. The governor's children moved to dismiss, arguing that the 1974 marriage was bigamous because the Hawaiian divorce took place under the Civil Code of 1950 prior to the Family Code.
* **Issue:** Whether a Filipino spouse divorced by an alien spouse abroad prior to the effectivity of the Family Code was capacitated to remarry under Philippine law.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court affirmed Felicidad's legal personality. The Supreme Court held that under the *Van Dorn* precedent, the Hawaiian divorce dissolved the marital tie as to the American wife. Consequently, Governor San Luis was no longer married to her and was capacitated to remarry even under the Civil Code. The enactment of Article 26(2) merely codified existing judicial precedent. Felicidad was the lawful surviving spouse entitled to seek administration of the estate.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Established the retroactive availability of the foreign divorce remarriage principle to pre-Family Code marriages based on the *Van Dorn* Civil Code doctrine.

### 13.8 Bayot v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 155635, November 7, 2008
* **Ponente:** Justice Presbitero J. Velasco, Jr. (Second Division)
* **Facts:** Vicente Bayot and Rebecca Makapugay were married in Mandaluyong in 1979. On its face, the Marriage Certificate identified Rebecca, then 26 years old, as an American citizen born in Agaña, Guam, to American parents. In 1996, Rebecca initiated divorce proceedings before the Dominican Republic court, which issued Civil Decree No. 362/96 dissolving the marriage and Civil Decree No. 406/97 approving their property settlement agreement. In 2001, Rebecca filed a petition in the Regional Trial Court of Muntinlupa City for declaration of absolute nullity of marriage and support pendente lite against Vicente, claiming she was a Filipino citizen and that the Dominican divorce was void as to her.
* **Issue:** Whether an alien spouse who sought and obtained an absolute divorce decree abroad is legally estopped from petitioning Philippine courts for declaration of nullity and spousal support against the former Filipino spouse.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court dismissed Rebecca's petition for declaration of nullity and support. The Court held that Rebecca was an American citizen when she married Vicente and when she procured the Dominican divorce. Under Article 15 of the Civil Code, her marital status was governed by her American personal law. She was legally estopped by her representations before the foreign court from impeaching the validity of the foreign divorce decree that she herself had sought and obtained. Having dissolved the marriage bond, she could no longer maintain an action for declaration of nullity or demand spousal support in Philippine courts against her former Filipino husband.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Established that an alien spouse who procures an absolute divorce abroad is bound by that decree under their personal law, and is legally estopped from seeking a declaration of nullity or spousal support against the Filipino ex-spouse in Philippine courts.

### 13.9 Dacasin v. Dacasin, G.R. No. 168785, February 5, 2010
* **Ponente:** Justice Antonio T. Carpio (Second Division)
* **Facts:** Herald Dacasin, an American citizen, and Sharon Del Mundo Dacasin, a Filipina, married in Manila in 1994. In 1999, the wife obtained an absolute divorce in Illinois, which awarded sole custody of their daughter to the mother. In 2002, the parties executed an agreement in Manila for joint custody. When the husband sued to enforce the joint custody agreement, the trial court dismissed the case for lack of jurisdiction, ruling that Illinois retained jurisdiction.
* **Issue:** Whether a Philippine Family Court has subject-matter jurisdiction to resolve child custody disputes between divorced parents residing in the Philippines.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court and asserted jurisdiction. The Supreme Court held that Philippine Family Courts have exclusive original jurisdiction over child custody cases involving domestic residents. Under Article 213 of the Family Code, agreements providing for joint custody of a child under seven years of age are void if they conflict with the statutory maternal preference, unless compelling reasons exist.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Clarified that foreign divorce decrees cannot oust Philippine courts of sovereign jurisdiction to protect minor children residing in the country under the doctrine of parens patriae.

### 13.10 Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas, G.R. No. 186571, August 11, 2010
* **Ponente:** Justice Arturo D. Brion (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Gerbert Corpuz, a naturalized Canadian citizen, married Daisylyn Sto. Tomas, a Filipina, in Pasig City in 2005. Corpuz later obtained a divorce decree from the Superior Court of Justice in Ontario, Canada. Corpuz filed a petition in the Regional Trial Court of Laoag City for judicial recognition of the foreign divorce decree so he could marry another Filipina. The trial court dismissed the petition, ruling that Article 26(2) is available exclusively to Filipino spouses.
* **Issue:** Whether an alien spouse has legal standing to file a petition for judicial recognition of a foreign divorce decree in the Philippines.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court modified the trial court's ruling. The Supreme Court held that while Article 26(2) substantively confers capacity to remarry solely upon the Filipino spouse, an alien spouse possesses undeniable legal standing to petition for the recognition of a foreign divorce decree under Rule 39, Section 48 of the Rules of Court. The foreign divorce decree serves as presumptive evidence of right. Furthermore, the Supreme Court held that recognition under Rule 39 may be joined with a petition for cancellation of civil registry entries under Rule 108 in a single proceeding.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Landmark procedural ruling recognizing the legal standing of alien spouses under Rule 39 and authorizing the joinder of Rule 39 recognition actions with Rule 108 civil registry proceedings.

### 13.11 Fujiki v. Marinay, G.R. No. 196049, June 26, 2013
* **Ponente:** Senior Associate Justice Antonio T. Carpio (En Banc)
* **Facts:** Minoru Fujiki, a Japanese national, married Maria Paz Marinay, a Filipina, in the Philippines in 2004. In 2008, Marinay married another Japanese national, Shinichi Maekawa, in Quezon City without her prior marriage to Fujiki being dissolved. In 2010, Maekawa obtained a judgment from the Nagoya Family Court in Japan declaring his marriage to Marinay void on the ground of bigamy. Fujiki then filed a petition in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City under Rule 108 to recognize the Japanese family court judgment declaring the second marriage void for bigamy, and to cancel the entry of that bigamous marriage in the civil registry. The trial court motu proprio dismissed the petition, ruling under A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC that only a husband or wife of the challenged marriage could file a petition for declaration of absolute nullity.
* **Issue:** Whether A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC applies to petitions for recognition of foreign judgments, and whether a prior spouse has legal standing to petition for the judicial recognition of a foreign judgment declaring a subsequent marriage void on the ground of bigamy.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court En Banc reversed the trial court. The Supreme Court held that A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC applies strictly to direct domestic actions for declaration of nullity of void marriages under Articles 35, 36, and 45 of the Family Code, and does NOT govern petitions for recognition of foreign judgments under Rule 39, Section 48. Any interested party who has a material legal right in the marital status—including a prior spouse whose subsisting marriage is aggrieved by a bigamous subsequent union—possesses legal standing to petition for recognition. Furthermore, the Supreme Court confirmed that recognition of a foreign judgment affecting civil status can be litigated in a special proceeding for cancellation or correction of entries under Rule 108.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Established that A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC does not bar interested prior spouses from petitioning to recognize foreign judgments declaring marriages void, and solidified Rule 108 as a proper procedural vehicle for foreign judgment recognition.

### 13.12 Medina v. Koike, G.R. No. 215723, July 27, 2016
* **Ponente:** Justice Estela M. Perlas-Bernabe (First Division)
* **Facts:** Doreen Grace Parilla Medina, a Filipina, married Michiyuki Koike, a Japanese citizen, in Quezon City in 2005. In 2012, they obtained a divorce by mutual agreement before the Mayor of Ichinomiya City, Aichi Prefecture, Japan. Seeking annotation of the Divorce Certificate on her marriage certificate, Medina filed a petition for judicial recognition of foreign divorce and declaration of capacity to remarry before the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City. The trial court denied the petition outright on the ground that while the divorce certificate was an official Japanese document, Medina failed to prove the Japanese law on divorce under Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25, through duly authenticated copies or qualified expert testimony. Medina filed a direct petition for review on certiorari under Rule 45 to the Supreme Court.
* **Issue:** Whether the RTC erred in denying the petition outright, and whether an improper Rule 45 appeal raising questions of fact on foreign law should be dismissed or referred to the Court of Appeals.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court held that the validity of the foreign divorce and the existence of Japanese divorce law are questions of fact that cannot be resolved in a Rule 45 petition. However, invoking Section 6, paragraph 2, of Rule 56 of the Rules of Court, the Supreme Court held that procedural rules must not override substantial justice. The Supreme Court REFERRED the case to the Court of Appeals for appropriate action, including the reception of evidence to determine and resolve the pertinent factual issues, preventing an outright forfeiture of the Filipino spouse's statutory right under Article 26(2).
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Established that when an appeal from an RTC ruling on foreign divorce recognition involves questions of fact, the Supreme Court may, pursuant to Rule 56, Section 6, refer the case to the Court of Appeals for reception of evidence rather than dismiss the appeal outright, harmonizing strict Rule 132 proof with substantial justice.

### 13.13 Sarto v. People, G.R. No. 206284, February 28, 2018
* **Ponente:** Justice Samuel R. Martires (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Redante Sarto married Maria Socorro in Camarines Sur in 1984. Maria Socorro naturalized as a Canadian citizen and obtained a divorce decree in British Columbia, Canada, in 1998. Relying on that divorce, Sarto married Fe in 2000 in the Philippines, declaring himself single. Sarto was charged with bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code. In defense, Sarto argued that his first marriage was dissolved by operation of Article 26(2) and the *Orbecido* doctrine.
* **Issue:** Whether a foreign divorce decree operates automatically in the Philippines to shield a Filipino spouse from criminal liability for bigamy.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court affirmed Sarto's conviction for bigamy. The Supreme Court held that a foreign divorce decree does not have automatic extraterritorial effect in the Philippines. A foreign divorce must first be judicially recognized by a competent Philippine court under Rule 39 or Rule 108 before the Filipino spouse can validly contract a subsequent marriage. Sarto's failure to secure a judicial recognition decree prior to his second marriage rendered his second marriage bigamous under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Established the mandatory criminal law rule that prior judicial recognition of a foreign divorce is an indispensable condition precedent to remarrying; contracting a subsequent marriage without prior judicial recognition constitutes the crime of bigamy.

### 13.14 Republic v. Cote, G.R. No. 212860, March 14, 2018
* **Ponente:** Justice Andres B. Reyes, Jr. (Second Division)
* **Facts:** Florie Grace Cote married Rhomel Gagarin, a Filipino, in Quezon City in 1995. Gagarin naturalized as an American citizen and obtained an absolute divorce decree in Hawaii in 2002. Cote filed a petition in the Regional Trial Court under Rule 108 for the judicial recognition of the foreign divorce decree and cancellation of marriage record. The Office of the Solicitor General moved to dismiss, claiming that Rule 108 does not confer jurisdiction to recognize a foreign divorce.
* **Issue:** Whether the Regional Trial Court has jurisdiction to recognize a foreign divorce decree within a Rule 108 special proceeding.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court denied the government's petition. The Supreme Court held that a foreign judgment dissolving a marriage may be recognized in a Rule 108 proceeding because Rule 108 is the designated procedural vehicle to establish the civil status of a person. The trial court had full jurisdiction to recognize the Hawaiian divorce decree and order the cancellation of the marriage entry.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Reaffirmed the procedural validity of combining Rule 39 foreign judgment recognition with Rule 108 cancellation of civil registry entries.

### 13.15 Republic v. Manalo, G.R. No. 221029, April 24, 2018
* **Ponente:** Justice Diosdado M. Peralta (En Banc)
* **Facts:** Marelyn Tanedo Manalo, a Filipina, married Yoshino Minoro, a Japanese citizen, in the Philippines in 1998. Manalo filed for divorce in Japan and obtained a court judgment decreeing the dissolution of their marriage. Manalo petitioned the Regional Trial Court of Dagupan City for judicial recognition of the Japanese divorce and cancellation of her marriage record under Article 26(2). The trial court dismissed the petition, ruling that Article 26(2) applies only when the alien spouse secures the divorce. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that the provision should be interpreted broadly.
* **Issue:** Whether Paragraph 2 of Article 26 of the Family Code applies to cases where the foreign divorce was initiated and obtained solely by the Filipino spouse.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court En Banc affirmed the Court of Appeals. The Supreme Court held that Article 26(2) applies to all mixed marriages where a valid foreign divorce has been obtained capacitating the alien spouse to remarry, regardless of which spouse initiated the divorce. The word "obtained" must be construed purposively rather than literally. To deny recognition when the Filipino files for divorce creates an arbitrary classification violating the equal protection clause of the Constitution, exalting form over substance.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** The watershed En Banc ruling that dismantled the restrictive doctrine, establishing that foreign divorces initiated solely by Filipino spouses fall within Article 26(2).

### 13.16 Racho v. Tanaka, G.R. No. 199515, June 25, 2018
* **Ponente:** Justice Marvic M.V.F. Leonen (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Rhodora Ilumin Racho, a Filipina, married Seiichi Tanaka, a Japanese national, in Las Piñas City in 2001. In 2009, they mutually agreed to divorce and obtained a Certificate of Acceptance of Notification of Divorce (*Juri Shomeisho*) from the mayor of a Japanese municipality. Racho petitioned for judicial recognition in Las Piñas. The trial court dismissed the petition, holding that Article 26(2) does not apply to administrative divorces by mutual agreement.
* **Issue:** Whether a Japanese administrative divorce by mutual agreement (*kyogi rikon*) falls within the contemplation of Article 26(2).
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court. The Supreme Court held that pursuant to *Manalo*, Article 26(2) applies to consensual divorces obtained by agreement abroad. The narrow interpretation of the Solicitor General disregards the agency of the Filipino spouse and presumes that all divorces must be protracted, bitter litigations. Because Racho submitted the Divorce Certificate and a certificate of the Japanese Civil Code from the Philippine Embassy in Tokyo, the foreign divorce and foreign law were sufficiently proved.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Confirmed that Japanese administrative divorces by mutual agreement (*kyogi rikon*) are fully recognizable under Article 26(2).

### 13.17 Morisono v. Morisono, G.R. No. 226013, July 2, 2018
* **Ponente:** Justice Estela M. Perlas-Bernabe (Second Division)
* **Facts:** Luzviminda Dela Cruz Morisono, a Filipina, married Ryoji Morisono, a Japanese national, in Quezon City in 2009. Morisono subsequently filed for divorce in Japan, and a divorce decree by mutual agreement was registered. Morisono petitioned for judicial recognition in Quezon City. The trial court denied the petition, holding that under *Orbecido*, the alien spouse must be the one who procured the divorce.
* **Issue:** Whether a divorce obtained abroad upon the initiative of the Filipino spouse is recognizable in Philippine courts.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court, directly applying the newly minted *Manalo* doctrine. The Supreme Court ruled that whether the foreign divorce was initiated by the foreign spouse or the Filipino spouse is immaterial. The essential inquiry is whether the divorce was valid under foreign law and capacitated the alien to remarry. The case was remanded for formal proof of the Japanese Civil Code.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Prompt application of the *Manalo* doctrine reversing trial court decisions grounded on the obsolete restrictive interpretation.

### 13.18 Juego-Sakai v. Republic, G.R. No. 224015, July 23, 2018
* **Ponente:** Justice Andres B. Reyes, Jr. (Second Division)
* **Facts:** Stephen Juego-Sakai, a Filipina, married Toshiharu Sakai, a Japanese citizen, in Quezon City in 2000. In 2007, they jointly obtained a divorce decree by mutual agreement in Japan. The trial court granted recognition, but the Court of Appeals reversed, holding that consensual divorces do not fall under Article 26(2).
* **Issue:** Whether a foreign divorce obtained by agreement of both spouses can be recognized under Article 26(2).
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals. Applying *Manalo*, the Supreme Court held that the second paragraph of Article 26 applies to divorces obtained by mutual agreement of the spouses. The case was remanded to the trial court for formal presentation of the Japanese law on divorce.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Reaffirmed the recognizability of joint, consensual foreign divorces under the *Manalo* doctrine.

### 13.19 Nullada v. Civil Registrar of Manila, G.R. No. 224548, January 23, 2019
* **Ponente:** Justice A. Reyes, Jr. (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Marlyn Monton Nullada, a Filipina, married Akira Ito, a Japanese citizen, in Tokyo in 1997. They mutually agreed to divorce in 2009. Nullada registered the divorce with the Philippine Embassy in Tokyo and filed a petition for recognition in Manila. The trial court dismissed the petition, ruling that the Filipino spouse cannot initiate a divorce abroad.
* **Issue:** Whether a mutual agreement divorce registered abroad is recognizable under Article 26(2).
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court, ruling that *Manalo* is controlling precedent. Article 26(2) covers foreign divorces obtained by the Filipino spouse or by mutual consent. The case was remanded for the reception of evidence regarding the Japanese Civil Code.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Solidified the En Banc *Manalo* doctrine across all divisions of the Supreme Court.

### 13.20 Arreza v. Toyo, G.R. No. 213198, July 1, 2019
* **Ponente:** Justice Marvic M.V.F. Leonen (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Genevieve Rosal Arreza, a Filipina, married Tetsushi Toyo, a Japanese national, in Quezon City in 1991. They obtained a divorce decree in Japan in 2011. Arreza petitioned for judicial recognition under Rule 108, submitting a copy of the divorce decree and a photocopy of the English translation of the Japanese Civil Code published by a private publisher. The trial court dismissed the petition for failure to prove foreign law under Rule 132.
* **Issue:** Whether a photocopy of a private publication of a foreign statute is sufficient to prove foreign law in an Article 26(2) proceeding.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's dismissal without prejudice. The Supreme Court held that foreign laws must be proved as facts by official publications or certified copies attested by the legal custodian under Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25. A private translation does not satisfy statutory evidentiary requirements.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Enforced strict evidentiary standards for proving foreign law and established that private commercial translations are inadmissible without official certification.

### 13.21 Moraña v. Republic, G.R. No. 227605, December 5, 2019
* **Ponente:** Justice Rosmari D. Carandang (First Division)
* **Facts:** Juliet Rendora Moraña, a Filipina, married Minuro Takahashi, a Japanese national, in San Juan, Metro Manila, in 2002. They obtained a divorce decree in Japan. Moraña petitioned for recognition in Pasig City, presenting a Divorce Report and an English copy of the Civil Code of Japan obtained from the Japan Information Center. The trial court dismissed the petition for lack of official authentication.
* **Issue:** Whether an unauthenticated copy of foreign law requires outright dismissal of an Article 26(2) petition.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court's dismissal and remanded the case. The Supreme Court ruled that while foreign law was not properly proved under Rule 132, procedural rules should not be applied mechanically when substantial justice would be frustrated. The case was remanded to the trial court to give the petitioner an opportunity to formally prove the foreign law.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Reaffirmed the equitable remand policy in foreign divorce cases to prevent substantive rights from being defeated by technical evidentiary deficiencies.

### 13.22 Galapon v. Republic, G.R. No. 243722, January 22, 2020
* **Ponente:** Justice Alfredo Benjamin S. Caguioa (First Division)
* **Facts:** Cynthia Galapon, a Filipina, married Park Bo Shik, a South Korean citizen, in Manila in 2012. Park later filed for divorce before the Cheongju District Court in South Korea, and a confirmed divorce decree was issued. Galapon petitioned for judicial recognition in Nueva Ecija. The trial court dismissed the petition, and the Court of Appeals affirmed, finding that Galapon had consented to the divorce.
* **Issue:** Whether a divorce decree obtained abroad with the consent or agreement of the Filipino spouse is recognizable under Article 26(2).
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the lower courts. Justice Caguioa formulated the authoritative Tripartite Rule: pursuant to *Manalo*, Article 26(2) applies where the foreign divorce decree is: (1) obtained solely by the foreign spouse; (2) obtained jointly by the Filipino and foreign spouse; and (3) obtained solely by the Filipino spouse. The inquiry into whether the Filipino consented is completely immaterial.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Crystallized the Tripartite Formulation of the *Manalo* doctrine, establishing that the source or initiator of the foreign divorce is legally irrelevant.

### 13.23 Kondo v. Civil Registrar General, G.R. No. 223628, March 4, 2020
* **Ponente:** Justice Amy C. Lazaro-Javier (First Division)
* **Facts:** Edna Kondo, a Filipina, married Katsuhiro Kondo, a Japanese citizen, in Manila in 1991. They obtained a divorce by mutual agreement in Japan in 2000. Kondo petitioned for recognition in Manila. The trial court dismissed the petition for failure to present authenticated copies of the foreign law and divorce decree.
* **Issue:** Whether trial courts should dismiss recognition petitions outright when foreign public records are unauthenticated.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court set aside the dismissal and remanded the case. The Supreme Court emphasized that trial courts must exercise liberality and active case management in Article 26(2) petitions. Where the existence of the foreign divorce is undisputed, courts must provide litigants reasonable opportunities to cure evidentiary defects through remand.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Established that substantive justice requires trial courts to afford petitioners an opportunity to complete documentary proof rather than dismiss petitions with prejudice.

### 13.24 Abel v. Rule, G.R. No. 234457, May 12, 2021
* **Ponente:** Justice Rosmari D. Carandang (First Division)
* **Facts:** Raemark Abel and Mindy Rule, both Filipino citizens, were married in Manila in 1999. In 2008, Rule naturalized as a United States citizen. In 2014, Rule obtained an absolute divorce decree in Clark County, Nevada. Abel petitioned the Regional Trial Court of Manila for recognition under Article 26(2). The trial court granted the petition. The Office of the Solicitor General appealed, arguing that *Orbecido* required the foreign spouse to initiate the divorce.
* **Issue:** Whether a foreign divorce obtained by a former Filipino who naturalized abroad is entitled to recognition under the modern *Manalo* doctrine.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court affirmed the trial court. The Supreme Court held that under *Orbecido*, post-marriage naturalization brings the marriage within Article 26(2). Furthermore, under *Manalo*, the fact that the naturalized spouse procured the divorce frees the remaining Filipino spouse to remarry under Philippine law.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Reaffirmed the harmonious convergence of the *Orbecido* naturalization doctrine with the *Manalo* parity principle.

### 13.25 Johansen v. Office of the Civil Registrar General, G.R. No. 256951, November 29, 2021
* **Ponente:** Justice Alfredo Benjamin S. Caguioa (First Division)
* **Facts:** Marietta Pangilinan Johansen, a Filipina, married a Norwegian citizen in Norway. A divorce decree was subsequently granted by the County Governor of Oslo, Norway. Johansen petitioned the Regional Trial Court in the Philippines to recognize the foreign divorce decree and direct the civil registrar to cancel her marriage entry under Rule 108. The trial court dismissed the petition, ruling that an action for recognition under Rule 39 cannot be joined with a Rule 108 proceeding.
* **Issue:** Whether an action for recognition of a foreign divorce judgment may be joined with a petition for cancellation or correction of entries in the civil registry under Rule 108.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court. Justice Caguioa reiterated the *Corpuz* doctrine, holding that the differences between an action for recognition of foreign judgment and a petition under Rule 108 do not preclude joining both causes of action in a single judicial proceeding. The trial court was ordered to hear the consolidated petition on the merits.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Reaffirmed the procedural efficiency of joinder between Rule 39 recognition and Rule 108 civil registry cancellation.

### 13.26 Republic v. Bayog-Saito, G.R. No. 247297, August 17, 2022
* **Ponente:** Justice Ramon Paul L. Hernando (First Division)
* **Facts:** Helen Bayog-Saito, a Filipina, married Toru Saito, a Japanese citizen, in Pasay City in 2000. In 2013, they obtained a divorce decree by mutual agreement in Japan. Bayog-Saito filed a petition for recognition in Pasay City. The trial court granted recognition. The Office of the Solicitor General appealed, contending that consensual divorces fall outside Article 26(2).
* **Issue:** Whether a foreign divorce decree jointly obtained by spouses abroad is entitled to recognition in the Philippines.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court affirmed the trial court. The Supreme Court held that under *Manalo* and *Galapon*, Article 26(2) covers foreign divorces obtained jointly by the spouses. Because the dissolution of the marriage was valid under the laws of Japan and capacitated the alien spouse to remarry, there was no legal ground to deprive the Filipino spouse of her capacity to remarry under Philippine law.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Reaffirmed the full recognizability of joint and mutual agreement divorces under the *Galapon* tripartite framework.

### 13.27 Tsutsumi v. Republic, G.R. No. 258130, April 17, 2023
* **Ponente:** Justice Henri Jean Paul B. Inting (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Regie David Tsutsumi, a Filipina, married a Japanese national and subsequently obtained a divorce decree in Japan. In her petition for recognition, she submitted foreign public records authenticated with an Apostille Certificate issued by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan pursuant to the 1961 Hague Apostille Convention, which entered into force in the Philippines on May 14, 2019. The trial court questioned whether an Apostille satisfies Rule 132 in lieu of consular legalization.
* **Issue:** Whether an Apostille Certificate issued under the Hague Apostille Convention satisfies the authentication requirements of Rule 132, Section 24 of the Rules of Court.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court held that an Apostille Certificate issued by a competent foreign authority in an Apostille member state completely satisfies Rule 132, Section 24. Diplomatic or consular legalization is no longer required for public documents originating from contracting states.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Modernized evidentiary practice by confirming that an Apostille Certificate replaces consular authentication in proving foreign divorce decrees and statutes.

### 13.28 Republic v. Ng, G.R. No. 249238, February 27, 2024
* **Ponente:** Justice Japar B. Dimaampao (En Banc)
* **Facts:** Ruby Cuevas Ng, a Filipino citizen, and Akihiro Sono, a Japanese national, were married in Quezon City on December 8, 2004, and subsequently had a child and resided in Japan. On August 31, 2007, the spouses obtained a "divorce decree by mutual agreement" in Japan, as shown by a Divorce Certificate issued by the Embassy of Japan and recorded in the Family Registry of Nakano-Ku, Tokyo. On May 28, 2018, Ng filed before the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City a petition for judicial recognition of foreign divorce and declaration of capacity to remarry under Article 26(2). The trial court granted the petition in 2019. The Republic, represented by the Office of the Solicitor General, appealed, arguing that Article 26(2) requires an adversarial judicial decree from a foreign court and cannot recognize a divorce obtained by mere mutual agreement, and that Ng failed to prove Japanese divorce law under Rule 132.
* **Issue:** Whether Paragraph 2 of Article 26 extends to foreign administrative divorces obtained by mutual agreement (*kyogi rikon*), and whether Ng sufficiently proved the Japanese law on divorce to warrant judicial recognition.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court En Banc held that Article 26(2) encompasses administrative out-of-court divorces obtained by mutual agreement. The statute requires only that the divorce be "validly obtained abroad," without distinguishing between judicial and non-judicial dissolutions. Applying *ubi lex non distinguit nec nos distinguere debemos*, the Court ruled that mutual-consent divorces fall squarely within Article 26(2) and do not violate domestic policies against collusion. However, the Supreme Court GRANTED the Republic's petition, REVERSED the trial court's decision, and REMANDED the case to the trial court for further proceedings and reception of evidence of the pertinent Japanese law on divorce. The Court found that Ng merely submitted unauthenticated photocopies of the Japanese Civil Code, failing to satisfy Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25. The Court also held that OCA Circular No. 157-2022-A is an internal reference compilation that does not dispense with formal proof of foreign law.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Authoritative En Banc decision confirming that administrative and consensual foreign divorces (*kyogi rikon*) are cognizable under Article 26(2), while reaffirming that failure to prove foreign law under Rule 132 necessitates a remand rather than outright recognition.

### 13.29 Asilo v. Gonzales-Betic, G.R. No. 232269, July 10, 2024
* **Ponente:** Justice Ramon Paul L. Hernando (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Shela Bacaltos Asilo married a Japanese national and later obtained an administrative divorce in Japan. In her Rule 108 petition, she prayed for the judicial recognition of the Japanese divorce and the cancellation of her married surname to revert to her maiden name. The trial court granted the recognition of the divorce but refused to direct the civil registrar to record her reversion to her maiden name, ruling that change of name required a separate proceeding under Rule 103.
* **Issue:** Whether a Filipino woman whose foreign divorce is recognized under Article 26(2) may obtain the cancellation of her married surname and reversion to her maiden name in the same Rule 108 proceeding.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court and ordered the civil registrar to record the reversion to her maiden name. The Supreme Court held that under Article 370 of the Civil Code, a married woman's right to use her husband's surname is an option that terminates when the marital bond is dissolved. Reversion to her maiden name is a necessary legal consequence of the recognition of the divorce, fully cognizable within the Rule 108 proceeding without requiring a separate Rule 103 suit.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Authoritative ruling establishing that reversion to the maiden name may be granted as an ancillary prayer in a Rule 108 foreign divorce recognition petition.

### 13.30 Anido v. Republic, G.R. No. 253527, October 21, 2024
* **Ponente:** Justice Henri Jean Paul B. Inting (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Rosary Kristine Anido, a Filipino physician, married Enrique Martin Gomez Pomar, a Peruvian citizen, in Parsippany, New Jersey, on May 17, 2012. After relocating their marital residence to Kentucky, marital relations deteriorated and Enrique filed for divorce before the Fayette County Circuit Court of Kentucky. On November 18, 2015, the Kentucky Court granted a decree of absolute divorce. In 2017, Anido filed a petition for enforcement of foreign divorce and correction of civil registry entries in RTC Quezon City. Anido submitted the authenticated divorce certificate from Kentucky, but to prove foreign law, she submitted copies of the Peru Civil Code and Kentucky marriage laws with self-authenticating notarized attestations. The trial court granted recognition. The Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the petition outright for failure to prove foreign law under Rule 132.
* **Issue:** Whether foreign statutes accompanied by notarized attestations satisfy the evidentiary requirements of Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25, and whether outright dismissal was proper.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court held that Anido failed to prove the Kentucky law on marriage and divorce. While notarized documents enjoy a presumption of regularity under Rule 132, Section 23 regarding their execution and date, a notary public has no legal custody over foreign statutes and cannot authenticate their official status or legal validity. Because the divorce was rendered by a Kentucky court, the foreign law governing the divorce was Kentucky law, which had to be proved in accordance with Rule 132. Crucially, however, the Supreme Court REVERSED and SET ASIDE the Court of Appeals' dismissal and REMANDED the case to the Court of Appeals for reception of evidence on the Kentucky laws on marriage and divorce, applying the policy of liberality to prevent substantive injustice to Filipinos in mixed marriages.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Clarified that notarized attestations cannot substitute for official publications or custodian attestations under Rule 132 to prove foreign law, while applying the policy of liberality to remand rather than dismiss recognition petitions.

### 13.31 De Munari v. Asprec, G.R. No. 262831, April 7, 2025
* **Ponente:** Justice Japar B. Dimaampao (Third Division)
* **Facts:** Gianni de Munari, an Italian national, married Thelma Gagui Asprec, a Filipina, in Italy. In 2022, De Munari filed a complaint for declaration of absolute nullity of marriage before the Family Court of Puerto Princesa City, alleging that Asprec was already married to another man at the time of their wedding. The trial court motu proprio dismissed the complaint for lack of jurisdiction under Article 17 of the Civil Code, reasoning that Italian courts had exclusive jurisdiction over the validity of a marriage celebrated in Italy.
* **Issue:** Whether Philippine Family Courts have jurisdiction to adjudicate the nullity of a marriage celebrated abroad between a Filipino and an alien under Paragraph 1 of Article 26.
* **Ruling:** The Supreme Court reversed the trial court and remanded the case for trial. The Supreme Court held that the trial court confused formal validity with intrinsic validity. Under Paragraph 1 of Article 26, when a marriage is celebrated abroad, its validity does not depend fully on foreign law. Irrespective of the place of celebration, Philippine laws bind the contracting Filipino citizen regarding legal capacity and marital impediments. A bigamous marriage celebrated abroad is void ab initio in the Philippines under Article 35(4) in relation to Article 26(1), and Philippine Family Courts have full subject-matter jurisdiction to declare its nullity.
* **Doctrinal Contribution:** Clarified the operation of Paragraph 1 of Article 26, affirming the subject-matter jurisdiction of Philippine Family Courts over nullity actions involving foreign-celebrated marriages and delineating the boundary between formal formalities and intrinsic substantive capacity.

## Key Cases

* **Van Dorn v. Romillo, Jr., G.R. No. 68470, October 8, 1985:** Foundational precedent holding that a foreign divorce obtained by an alien spouse releases both parties from marital obligations; the alien spouse cannot sue for conjugal assets in the Philippines, and the Filipino spouse is freed from marital claims.
* **Pilapil v. Ibay-Somera, G.R. No. 80116, June 30, 1989:** Applied the foreign divorce doctrine to criminal law, ruling that an alien ex-spouse who secured a foreign divorce ceases to be an offended spouse under Article 344 of the Revised Penal Code and lacks standing to file criminal adultery charges.
* **Garcia v. Recio, G.R. No. 138322, October 2, 2001:** Established the governing evidentiary rule that Philippine courts cannot take judicial notice of foreign laws or divorce decrees; both the foreign divorce decree and the national law of the alien capacitating remarriage must be alleged and proved as facts under Rule 132.
* **Roehr v. Rodriguez, G.R. No. 142820, June 20, 2003:** Held that while a foreign divorce decree dissolving the marital tie is recognizable, ancillary awards of child custody and support remain subject to independent judicial evaluation by Philippine courts under the best interests of the child standard.
* **Republic v. Iyoy, G.R. No. 152577, September 21, 2005:** Settled that Article 26(2) does not apply where both spouses were Filipino citizens at the time the foreign divorce was obtained; foreign divorces between Filipinos are completely void under the nationality principle of Article 15 of the Civil Code.
* **Republic v. Orbecido III, G.R. No. 154380, October 5, 2005:** Landmark En Banc decision establishing the twin elements of Article 26(2) and the naturalization reckoning point, while setting aside the capacity declaration for lack of competent evidence.
* **San Luis v. San Luis, G.R. No. 133743, February 6, 2007:** Confirmed that the *Van Dorn* principle applied to marriages celebrated under the Civil Code of 1950 prior to the Family Code; a surviving Filipino spouse married to a partner who had been divorced by an alien spouse has legal standing to petition for letters of administration.
* **Bayot v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 155635, November 7, 2008:** Held that an American citizen spouse who obtained a foreign divorce is bound by that decree under her national law, and is legally estopped from petitioning Philippine courts for declaration of nullity and support against the former Filipino spouse.
* **Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas, G.R. No. 186571, August 11, 2010:** Landmark procedural ruling holding that an alien spouse has legal standing under Rule 39, Section 48 to petition for recognition of a foreign divorce decree; established that Rule 39 recognition may be joined with a Rule 108 civil registry petition in a single proceeding.
* **Fujiki v. Marinay, G.R. No. 196049, June 26, 2013:** Landmark En Banc decision holding that A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC does not bar interested prior spouses from petitioning under Rule 108 to recognize a foreign judgment declaring a subsequent marriage void for bigamy.
* **Medina v. Koike, G.R. No. 215723, July 27, 2016:** Reaffirmed the strict requirement of proving foreign law under Rule 132, while establishing the Supreme Court's authority under Rule 56, Section 6 to refer the case to the Court of Appeals for reception of evidence rather than dismiss the appeal outright.
* **Sarto v. People, G.R. No. 206284, February 28, 2018:** Critical criminal precedent establishing that a foreign divorce decree does not operate automatically in the Philippines; a Filipino spouse who contracts a second marriage without first obtaining a judicial decree of recognition commits the crime of bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code.
* **Republic v. Manalo, G.R. No. 221029, April 24, 2018:** Watershed En Banc decision abandoning the restrictive literal doctrine; held that Article 26(2) applies regardless of whether the foreign divorce proceeding was initiated by the foreign spouse, jointly by both spouses, or solely by the Filipino spouse, grounded on constitutional equal protection.
* **Racho v. Tanaka, G.R. No. 199515, June 25, 2018:** Held that Japanese administrative divorces by mutual agreement (*kyogi rikon*) fall within Article 26(2), recognizing the agency of the Filipino spouse to agree to an out-of-court dissolution.
* **Galapon v. Republic, G.R. No. 243722, January 22, 2020:** Formulated the canonical Tripartite Rule of *Manalo*, holding that Article 26(2) covers foreign divorces: (1) obtained solely by the alien spouse; (2) obtained jointly by both spouses; and (3) obtained solely by the Filipino spouse.
* **Abel v. Rule, G.R. No. 234457, May 12, 2021:** Reaffirmed the harmonious application of the *Orbecido* post-marriage naturalization rule within the modern *Manalo* parity framework.
* **Johansen v. Office of the Civil Registrar General, G.R. No. 256951, November 29, 2021:** Reaffirmed that an action for recognition of foreign judgment under Rule 39 and a petition for cancellation or correction of civil registry entries under Rule 108 may be joined in a single judicial proceeding.
* **Republic v. Bayog-Saito, G.R. No. 247297, August 17, 2022:** Confirmed that mutual agreement divorces valid under Japanese law are fully recognizable in the Philippines under the *Galapon* tripartite framework.
* **Tsutsumi v. Republic, G.R. No. 258130, April 17, 2023:** Confirmed that an Apostille Certificate issued pursuant to the 1961 Hague Apostille Convention fully satisfies Rule 132, Section 24, replacing consular legalization for public documents from member states.
* **Republic v. Ng, G.R. No. 249238, February 27, 2024:** Landmark En Banc ruling confirming that Article 26(2) encompasses administrative divorces by agreement (*kyogi rikon*), while remanding for Rule 132 proof of Japanese law and clarifying OCA Circular No. 157-2022-A.
* **Asilo v. Gonzales-Betic, G.R. No. 232269, July 10, 2024:** Established that reversion to the maiden name under Article 370 of the Civil Code may be granted as an ancillary prayer in a Rule 108 foreign divorce recognition petition without requiring a separate Rule 103 proceeding.
* **Anido v. Republic, G.R. No. 253527, October 21, 2024:** Held that foreign statutes supported only by notarized attestations fail Rule 132 proof, but remanded under the policy of liberality for reception of evidence on Kentucky law.
* **De Munari v. Asprec, G.R. No. 262831, April 7, 2025:** Landmark ruling on Paragraph 1 of Article 26, establishing that Philippine Family Courts possess subject-matter jurisdiction to adjudicate the nullity of foreign-celebrated marriages involving Filipino citizens where intrinsic legal capacity is violated.

## Suggested Answers

### Question 1: Recognition of Foreign Divorce Initiated by Filipino Spouse

**Problem:**
Elena, a Filipino citizen, married Kenji, a Japanese national, in Tokyo, Japan, in 2018. After three years of marriage, Kenji abandoned the marital home. Unable to endure the abandonment, Elena filed for divorce before the Tokyo Family Court. In 2023, the Japanese court rendered a final judgment granting the divorce and dissolving the marital union. Under the Civil Code of Japan, Kenji was fully capacitated to contract a subsequent marriage upon the finality of the judgment. Elena returned to the Philippines and filed a petition before the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City for the judicial recognition of the Japanese divorce decree and cancellation of her marriage record under Article 26(2) of the Family Code. The Office of the Solicitor General opposed the petition, asserting that Article 26(2) provides relief only when the divorce is obtained abroad by the alien spouse, and that Elena, as a Filipino citizen, is prohibited by Article 15 of the Civil Code from seeking a divorce. Should the Regional Trial Court grant Elena's petition? Explain.

**Suggested Answer:**
Yes, the Regional Trial Court should grant Elena's petition for judicial recognition of the foreign divorce decree.

Under Paragraph 2 of Article 26 of the Family Code, where a marriage between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner is validly celebrated and a divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law. In the landmark En Banc ruling of *Republic v. Manalo* (2018), reaffirmed in *Galapon v. Republic* (2020), the Supreme Court abandoned the restrictive literal interpretation that required the alien spouse to be the sole initiator of the divorce.

The Supreme Court ruled that Article 26(2) applies regardless of whether the foreign divorce was initiated by the foreign spouse, jointly by both spouses, or solely by the Filipino spouse. The equal protection clause of the Constitution prohibits arbitrary discrimination between Filipinos who filed for divorce abroad and those whose alien partners filed the petition, because both are left in identical circumstances: their marital ties are severed under foreign law, and their alien partners are free to remarry. The decisive statutory criterion is not who initiated the divorce proceeding, but whether the foreign decree validly dissolved the marriage and capacitated the alien spouse to remarry under their national law.

Here, Elena's marriage was celebrated between a Filipino and a Japanese citizen, and the Japanese court decree dissolved the union and capacitated Kenji to remarry under Japanese law. Elena proved both the decree and the Japanese Civil Code under Rule 132 of the Rules of Court. Therefore, Elena is entitled to judicial recognition of the Japanese divorce and the cancellation of her marriage record, restoring her legal capacity to remarry under Philippine law.

### Question 2: Extraterritorial Marriage Celebrated Abroad and Lex Loci Celebrationis

**Problem:**
Marco, a twenty-year-old Filipino citizen residing in the United States, married Sophia, an eighteen-year-old Filipino citizen, in Las Vegas, Nevada. Under Nevada law, individuals aged eighteen and above possess full legal capacity to marry without parental consent. However, Marco did not obtain parental consent from his parents in the Philippines as required under Article 14 of the Family Code for contracting parties between the ages of eighteen and twenty-one. Two years later, Marco returned to the Philippines and met Clara, whom he wished to marry. Marco's parents advised him that his marriage in Las Vegas was void in the Philippines under Article 26(1) for lack of parental consent. Sophia, on the other hand, insisted that their marriage was perfectly valid under Nevada law and therefore valid in the Philippines under the doctrine of lex loci celebrationis. Is the marriage between Marco and Sophia valid, voidable, or void in the Philippines? Explain.

**Suggested Answer:**
The marriage between Marco and Sophia is voidable under Philippine law.

Under Paragraph 1 of Article 26 of the Family Code, all marriages solemnized outside the Philippines, in accordance with the laws in force in the country where they were solemnized, and valid there as such, shall also be valid in this country, except those prohibited under Articles 35(1), (4), (5), (6), 36, 37, and 38. This provision codifies the rule of lex loci celebrationis, subject to specific public policy exceptions.

Under Article 15 of the Civil Code, laws relating to family rights, status, condition, and legal capacity bind Filipino citizens even when living abroad. Marco and Sophia, being both Filipino citizens, remained governed by Philippine law regarding their substantive legal capacity to marry. Under Article 14 of the Family Code, an individual between eighteen and twenty-one years of age must obtain parental consent. However, the lack of parental consent does NOT render a marriage void ab initio under Article 35; rather, under Article 45, Paragraph 1 of the Family Code, lack of parental consent renders a marriage merely voidable.

Significantly, Article 45 is NOT among the statutory exceptions enumerated in Paragraph 1 of Article 26 that render a foreign marriage completely void in the Philippines. The exceptions listed in Article 26(1) are strictly confined to Articles 35(1), (4), (5), (6), 36, 37, and 38. As the Supreme Court held in *De Munari v. Asprec* (2025), foreign marriages involving Filipino citizens are invalid in the Philippines only when they violate essential public policy prohibitions or lack fundamental capacity under the specified codal exceptions.

Because Marco and Sophia were both above eighteen years of age (satisfying Article 35(1)), their marriage was not void ab initio. The lack of parental consent makes the marriage merely voidable under Article 45(1). Consequently, the marriage remains valid and binding in the Philippines unless and until it is annulled by a competent Family Court in a direct proceeding filed within the five-year prescriptive period under Article 47. Marco cannot marry Clara without first obtaining a judicial decree of annulment.

### Question 3: Subsequent Remarriage Prior to Judicial Recognition and Bigamy Liability

**Problem:**
Arman, a Filipino citizen, married Brigitte, a French national, in Paris, France, in 2015. In 2020, Brigitte secured an absolute divorce decree from the Judicial Court of Paris. The divorce decree became final and executory under French law, completely capacitating Brigitte to remarry. Brigitte sent Arman an authenticated copy of the French divorce decree. In 2022, Arman met Danica in Cebu City and proposed marriage. Arman showed Danica the French divorce decree and assured her that under Article 26(2) of the Family Code and the *Manalo* doctrine, he was legally capacitated to remarry. Believing Arman, Danica married him in a civil ceremony in Cebu City without first filing a petition in court for the recognition of the French divorce decree. In 2024, their relationship soured, and Danica discovered that Arman's first marriage was still recorded as subsisting in the Philippine Statistics Authority database. Danica filed a criminal complaint against Arman for bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code. Arman moved to dismiss the charge, arguing that his first marriage was dissolved by the French divorce prior to his second marriage, and that *Pulido v. People* allows him to assert the dissolution as a complete defense. Is Arman criminally liable for bigamy? Explain.

**Suggested Answer:**
Yes, Arman is criminally liable for the crime of bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code.

In the controlling case of *Sarto v. People* (2018), the Supreme Court categorically ruled that a foreign divorce decree does not operate automatically in the Philippines. Foreign judgments relating to the status of a marriage must first be judicially recognized by a competent Philippine court under Rule 39 or Rule 108 of the Rules of Court before their legal effects can be extended in this jurisdiction. Until a Philippine Regional Trial Court renders a judgment recognizing the foreign divorce under Rule 39 or Rule 108 of the Rules of Court, the first marriage is presumed to be subsisting under Philippine law.

Arman cannot invoke the landmark En Banc ruling in *Pulido v. People* (2021). The doctrine in *Pulido* applies strictly to marriages that are **void ab initio** under Articles 35, 36, 37, and 38 of the Family Code, where the marriage was legally non-existent from its inception. In contrast, Arman's marriage to Brigitte in Paris was an initially valid marriage that required a foreign judicial act to dissolve. Because the dissolution of an initially valid marriage depends on an external foreign decree that has no automatic force in the Philippines, prior judicial recognition is an indispensable condition precedent to contracting a subsequent marriage.

When Arman married Danica in 2022, no Philippine court had recognized the French divorce decree. Consequently, his first marriage was still subsisting in the eyes of Philippine criminal law. Arman's mistaken belief that the foreign divorce operated automatically was an error of law, which under Article 3 of the Civil Code does not excuse compliance nor negate criminal intent. Therefore, Arman is guilty of bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code.

# Quotes

> "Thus, taking into consideration the legislative intent and applying the rule of reason, we hold that Paragraph 2 of Article 26 should be interpreted to include cases involving parties who, at the time of the celebration of the marriage were Filipino citizens, but later on, one of them becomes naturalized as a foreign citizen and obtains a divorce decree. The Filipino spouse should likewise be allowed to remarry as if the other party were a foreigner at the time of the solemnization of the marriage. To rule otherwise would be to sanction absurdity and injustice. Where the interpretation of a statute according to its exact and literal import would lead to mischievous results or contravene the clear purpose of the legislature, it should be construed according to its spirit and reason, disregarding as far as necessary the letter of the law. A statute may therefore be extended to cases not within the literal meaning of its terms, so long as they come within its spirit or intent."[^39]

> "The reckoning point is not the citizenship of the parties at the time of the celebration of the marriage, but their citizenship at the time a valid divorce is obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating the latter to remarry."[^40]

> "To reiterate, the purpose of Paragraph 2 of Article 26 is to avoid the absurd situation where the Filipino spouse remains married to the alien spouse who, after a foreign divorce decree that is effective in the country where it was rendered, is no longer married to the Filipino spouse. The provision is a corrective measure to address an anomaly where the Filipino spouse is tied to the marriage while the foreign spouse is free to marry under the laws of his or her country. Whether the Filipino spouse initiated the foreign divorce proceeding or not, a favorable decree dissolving the marriage bond and capacitating his or her alien spouse to remarry will have the same result: the Filipino spouse will effectively be without a husband or wife. A Filipino who initiated a foreign divorce proceeding is in the same place and in "like circumstance as a Filipino who is at the receiving end of an alien initiated proceeding. Therefore, the subject provision should not make a distinction. In both instance, it is extended as a means to recognize the residual effect of the foreign divorce decree on Filipinos whose marital ties to their alien spouses are severed by operation of the latter's national law."[^41]

> "On the contrary, there is no real and substantial difference between a Filipino who initiated a foreign divorce proceedings and a Filipino who obtained a divorce decree upon the instance of his or her alien spouse. In the eyes of the Philippine and foreign laws, both are considered as Filipinos who have the same rights and obligations in a alien land. The circumstances surrounding them are alike. Were it not for Paragraph 2 of Article 26, both are still married to their foreigner spouses who are no longer their wives/husbands. Hence, to make a distinction between them based merely on the superficial difference of whether they initiated the divorce proceedings or not is utterly unfair. Indeed, the treatment gives undue favor to one and unjustly discriminate against the other."[^42]

> "We qualify our above conclusion - i.e., that the second paragraph of Article 26 of the Family Code bestows no rights in favor of aliens - with the complementary statement that this conclusion is not sufficient basis to dismiss Gerbert's petition before the RTC. In other words, the unavailability of the second paragraph of Article 26 of the Family Code to aliens does not necessarily strip Gerbert of legal interest to petition the RTC for the recognition of his foreign divorce decree. The foreign divorce decree itself, after its authenticity and conformity with the alien's national law have been duly proven according to our rules of evidence, serves as a presumptive evidence of right in favor of Gerbert, pursuant to Section 48, Rule 39 of the Rules of Court which provides for the effect of foreign judgments. This Section states:"[^43]

> "We hasten to point out, however, that this ruling should not be construed as requiring two separate proceedings for the registration of a foreign divorce decree in the civil registry - one for recognition of the foreign decree and another specifically for cancellation of the entry under Rule 108 of the Rules of Court. The recognition of the foreign divorce decree may be made in a Rule 108 proceeding itself, as the object of special proceedings (such as that in Rule 108 of the Rules of Court) is precisely to establish the status or right of a party or a particular fact. Moreover, Rule 108 of the Rules of Court can serve as the appropriate adversarial proceeding by which the applicability of the foreign judgment can be measured and tested in terms of jurisdictional infirmities, want of notice to the party, collusion, fraud, or clear mistake of law or fact."[^44]

> "Since the recognition of a foreign judgment only requires proof of fact of the judgment, it may be made in a special proceeding for cancellation or correction of entries in the civil registry under Rule 108 of the Rules of Court. Rule 1, Section 3 of the Rules of Court provides that “[a] special proceeding is a remedy by which a party seeks to establish a status, a right, or a particular fact.” Rule 108 creates a remedy to rectify facts of a person’s life which are recorded by the State pursuant to the Civil Register Law or Act No. 3753. These are facts of public consequence such as birth, death or marriage, which the State has an interest in recording. As noted by the Solicitor General, in Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas this Court declared that “[t]he recognition of the foreign divorce decree may be made in a Rule 108 proceeding itself, as the object of special proceedings (such as that in Rule 108 of the Rules of Court) is precisely to establish the status or right of a party or a particular fact.”"[^45]

> "The second paragraph of Article 26 is only a corrective measure to address the anomaly that results from a marriage between a Filipino, whose laws do not allow divorce, and a foreign citizen, whose laws allow divorce. The anomaly consists in the Filipino spouse being tied to the marriage while the foreign spouse is free to marry under the laws of his or her country. The correction is made by extending in the Philippines the effect of the foreign divorce decree, which is already effective in the country where it was rendered. The second paragraph of Article 26 of the Family Code is based on this Court’s decision in Van Dorn v. Romillo which declared that the Filipino spouse “should not be discriminated against in her own country if the ends of justice are to be served.”"[^46]

> "Pursuant to the majority ruling in Manalo, Article 26(2) applies to mixed marriages where the divorce decree is: (i) obtained by the foreign spouse; (ii) obtained jointly by the Filipino and foreign spouse; and (iii) obtained solely by the Filipino spouse."[^47]

> "Altogether, these cases uniformly embody the current jurisprudential rule that foreign divorce by mutual agreement is within the ambit of Article 26(2) of the Family Code, and as such, may be judicially recognized in the Philippines."[^48]

> "A plain reading of Article 26(2) of the Family Code reveals that it only requires that the divorce be "validly obtained abroad." To insist that the divorce be obtained through judicial proceedings in a foreign jurisdiction is to insert a condition not provided in the law. Indeed, the law does not distinguish between divorces obtained through judicial proceedings and administrative proceedings; or between those where one spouse files for divorce and the other contests it, and those where the divorce is a product of mutual agreement. The plain meaning rule prohibits this Court from imposing its own distinctions and qualifications on the clear and unambiguous language of Article 26(2). To do so would be tantamount to judicial legislation, an unwarranted overstepping of the Court's judicial functions. After all, it is also an elementary rule in statutory construction that where the law does not distinguish, the courts should not distinguish. Ubi lex non distinguit nec nos distinguere debemos."[^49]

> "Along this line, it is useful to state that when the marriage is celebrated elsewhere, its validity does not depend fully on foreign law. While accepted in the jurisdiction in which it is celebrated, it may be held invalid in the Philippines when it falls under the instances mentioned in par. 1, Article 26 of the Family Code such as incestuous or bigamous marriages. As well, irrespective of the place of solemnization of marriage, Philippine laws bind the contracting Filipino citizen with respect to "family rights and duties, status, condition, and legal capacity;" any controversy arising therefrom would then have to be determined in accordance with the same law.15 (Emphasis supplied)"[^50]

> "A divorce decree obtained abroad by an alien spouse is a foreign judgment relating to the status of a marriage. As in any other foreign judgment, a divorce decree does not have an automatic effect in the Philippines. Consequently, recognition by Philippine courts may be required before the effects of a divorce decree could be extended in this jurisdiction. Recognition of the divorce decree, however, need not be obtained in a separate petition filed solely for that purpose. Philippine courts may recognize the foreign divorce decree when such was invoked by a party as an integral aspect of his claim or defense."[^51]

> "A divorce obtained abroad by an alien may be recognized in our jurisdiction, provided such decree is valid according to the national law of the foreigner. However, the divorce decree and the governing personal law of the alien spouse who obtained the divorce must be proven. Our courts do not take judicial notice of foreign laws and judgments; hence, like any other facts, both the divorce decree and the national law of the alien must be alleged and proven according to our law on evidence."[^52]

> "Judicial recognition of a foreign divorce requires that the national law of the foreign spouse and the divorce decree be pleaded and proved as a fact before the Regional Trial Court. The Filipino spouse may be granted the capacity to remarry once our courts find that the foreign divorce was validly obtained by the foreign spouse according to his or her national law, and that the foreign spouse's national law considers the dissolution of the marital relationship to be absolute."[^53]

> "The Solicitor General's narrow interpretation of Article 26 disregards any agency on the part of the Filipino spouse. It presumes that the Filipino spouse is incapable of agreeing to the dissolution of the marital bond. It perpetuates the notion that all divorce proceedings are protracted litigations fraught with bitterness and drama. Some marriages can end amicably, without the parties harboring any ill will against each other. The parties could forgo costly court proceedings and opt for, if the national law of the foreign spouse allows it, a more convenient out-of-court divorce process. This ensures amity between the former spouses, a friendly atmosphere for the children and extended families, and less financial burden for the family."[^54]

> "As such, the Van Dorn case is sufficient basis in resolving a situation where a divorce is validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse. With the enactment of the Family Code and paragraph 2, Article 26 thereof, our lawmakers codified the law already established through judicial precedent."[^55]


# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Diagnostic Checklist for Article 26 Recognition Petitions**
> 1. **Reckoning of Citizenship:** Verify that at least one spouse was an alien at the time the foreign divorce was obtained (*Republic v. Orbecido III*). If both spouses remained Filipino citizens at the time of divorce, Article 26(2) does not apply (*Republic v. Iyoy*).
> 2. **Initiator Immateriality:** The petition is maintainable regardless of whether the foreign divorce was initiated by the foreign spouse, jointly by both spouses, or solely by the Filipino spouse (*Republic v. Manalo*; *Galapon v. Republic*).
> 3. **Nature of Divorce:** Confirm that the foreign divorce is an absolute dissolution capacitating remarriage, whether rendered by court judgment or administrative agreement (*Republic v. Ng*).
> 4. **Apostille or Consular Proof:** Ensure the foreign divorce decree and the foreign statutory law are authenticated by an Apostille Certificate from the foreign authority or legalized by the Philippine Consulate (*Tsutsumi v. Republic*).
> 5. **Procedural Consolidation:** File a petition before the Regional Trial Court joining the action for recognition under Rule 39 with a prayer for cancellation and correction of entries under Rule 108 (*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*; *Johansen v. Civil Registrar*).
> 6. **Implead Indispensable Parties:** Name the foreign former spouse, the Local Civil Registrar, and the Civil Registrar General as respondents, and serve mandatory notice on the Office of the Solicitor General.
> 7. **Surname Ancillary Prayer:** If the petitioner is a woman wishing to resume her maiden name, include an express prayer for reversion of surname in the Rule 108 petition (*Asilo v. Gonzales-Betic*).

### Pleading and Proving Both Decree and Foreign Law Under Rule 132

> [!NOTE]
> **Litigation Practice Tip: Avoid the Fatal Evidentiary Trap of Processual Presumption**
> The most frequent cause of dismissal or protracted delay in Article 26(2) recognition petitions is the failure to properly prove the foreign law governing the alien spouse. Philippine courts never take judicial notice of foreign laws. Do not rely on uncertified internet printouts, commercial legal compendiums, or notarized attorney affidavits (*Anido v. Republic*). You must present either an official publication of the foreign statute printed under governmental authority or an attested copy issued by the legal custodian, accompanied by an Apostille Certificate or consular authentication. Where the divorce was obtained in Japan by mutual agreement, offer both the Acceptance Certificate (*Juri Shomeisho*) or Family Register (*Koseki Tohon*) and authenticated excerpts of the Civil Code of Japan demonstrating that the administrative registration absolutely dissolves the marriage and capacitates remarriage.

### Avoiding Criminal Bigamy Liability Prior to Judicial Recognition

> [!TIP]
> **Bar and Client Counseling Tip: Foreign Divorce Never Operates Automatically in Criminal Law**
> Never advise a Filipino client who has obtained a foreign divorce abroad to remarry immediately without securing a Philippine court judgment of recognition. Under *Sarto v. People*, a foreign divorce decree does not operate automatically in this jurisdiction. Until a Philippine Family Court renders a final decree recognizing the foreign judgment under Rule 39 or Rule 108, the Filipino spouse remains legally married in the eyes of Philippine criminal law. Remarrying before judicial recognition exposes the client to non-bailable or serious criminal prosecution for bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code. Remind clients that the defense in *Pulido v. People* applies only to marriages that were void from the beginning, not to initially valid marriages dissolved by foreign divorce.

### Efficient Consolidation of Rule 39 Recognition and Rule 108 Remedies

> [!NOTE]
> **Procedural Strategy Tip: Single-Petition Practice Before the Regional Trial Court**
> Avoid filing two separate petitions. Under the established doctrine of *Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*, *Fujiki v. Marinay*, and *Johansen v. Civil Registrar*, an action for recognition of a foreign judgment under Rule 39, Section 48 may be joined directly with a petition for cancellation or correction of entries under Rule 108 in a single petition before the Regional Trial Court acting as a Family Court. Venue is properly laid in the RTC of the city or province where the marriage was recorded, or in the RTC of Manila if the marriage was celebrated abroad and recorded through the City Civil Registrar of Manila. Ensure that the petition prays for: (1) judicial recognition of the foreign divorce; (2) direction to the Local Civil Registrar and the PSA to annotate the decree on the marriage certificate; and (3) direction to record the wife's reversion to her maiden name under Article 370 of the Civil Code.
