> Art. 2. No marriage shall be valid, unless these essential requisites are present:
>
> (1) Legal capacity of the contracting parties who must be a male and a female; and
>
> (2) Consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer. (53a)

[^family-code]

# Explanation

Article 2 of the Family Code establishes the two essential requisites without which no marriage is valid: legal capacity of the contracting parties who must be a male and a female together with consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer. Under Article 4 of the Family Code, the absence of any essential or formal requisite renders the marriage void *ab initio* except as stated in Article 35(2) on good faith belief in the authority of the solemnizing officer, whereas a defect in any essential requisite renders the marriage voidable as provided in Article 45.[^2]

In [*Tenebro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47053), the Supreme Court classified the statutory requirements for a valid marriage into the essential requisites under Article 2 (legal capacity of the contracting parties and their consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer) alongside the formal requisites under Article 3 (authority of the solemnizing officer, a valid marriage license unless exempted by law plus a marriage ceremony in the presence of at least two witnesses).[^3] Because Articles 2 and 3 enact positive statutory commands, the Supreme Court held in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) that estoppel as a principle of equity cannot supplant the essential and formal requisites of the Family Code even where a party participates in misrepresenting facts in a marriage certificate.[^4]

## Legal Capacity of a Male and a Female

Legal capacity under Article 2(1) operates together with Article 5 of the Family Code, which provides that any male or female of the age of eighteen years or upwards not under any of the impediments mentioned in Articles 37 and 38 may contract marriage.[^5] Beyond age and freedom from incestuous or public policy impediments, Article 2(1) restricts marriage to a male and a female.[^6]

In [*Silverio v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44532), a post-operative male-to-female transsexual petitioned under Rule 108 to change his first name and sex entry in his birth certificate so that he could marry his male fiancé.[^7] Denying the petition, the Supreme Court ruled that under Act No. 3753, sex is determined visually at birth and remains immutable absent legislation while the common meaning of male and female does not encompass post-operative transsexuals.[^8] The Supreme Court said that the requested change would substantially reconfigure and greatly alter the laws on marriage and family relations by allowing a union between two biological males. It added that courts cannot alter this through equity or Article 9 of the Civil Code.[^9]

The heteronormative framework of Articles 1 and 2 was subsequently challenged in [*Falcis III v. Civil Registrar General*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65744), where the petitioner sought to nullify Articles 1, 2, 46(4) and 55(6) of the Family Code for excluding same-sex couples from marriage.[^10] The Supreme Court observed that while the plain text of Article XV, Section 2 of the Constitution does not restrict marriage on the basis of sex, gender, sexual orientation or gender identity or expression, Articles 1 and 2 of the Family Code hew to a heteronormative standard that underpins numerous sex-specific statutes in the Civil Code, the Family Code, the Revised Penal Code and Republic Act No. 8187 on paternity leave.[^11] Concluding that striking down Articles 1 and 2 alone would impliedly amend interconnected laws and usurp legislative power, the Supreme Court dismissed the petition for lack of an actual case or controversy and legal standing without invalidating the provisions. It observed that the time for a definitive judicial fiat on the matter may not yet have come and that Congress might act.[^12]

### Sex Entries in the Civil Register

The petitioner in [*Silverio v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44532) was born in Manila on April 4, 1962 and registered as male.[^13] He underwent sex reassignment surgery in Bangkok on January 27, 2001.[^14] He then lived as a female and was engaged to be married.[^15] On November 26, 2002, he asked the Regional Trial Court of Manila to change his first name to Mely and his sex entry to female.[^16]

The trial court granted the petition on June 4, 2003.[^17] It reasoned that justice and equity favored the petitioner and that no one would be harmed.[^18] The Republic went to the Court of Appeals on certiorari.[^19] The Court of Appeals ruled that no law allowed the change and ordered the petition dismissed.[^20]

The Supreme Court first dealt with the first name.[^21] It held that Republic Act No. 9048 now governs a change of first name and makes the remedy primarily administrative.[^22] Sex reassignment is not a ground under that law. The petition was also filed in the wrong forum and venue.[^23] The Supreme Court added that a change of name does not alter legal capacity or civil status.[^24]

On the sex entry, the Supreme Court held that a change of sex is a substantial change that falls under Rule 108.[^25] It found nothing to correct because the birth certificate contained no error.[^26] Sex reassignment is not among the acts or events that the Civil Code allows to be entered in the civil register.[^27] No special law governs sex reassignment and its effects.[^28] A birth certificate records the facts as they existed at birth under the Civil Register Law.[^29] Sex is therefore determined at birth by visual examination and stays immutable absent error and absent a law recognizing sex reassignment.[^30] The words male and female also carry their ordinary meaning, which does not include persons who underwent sex reassignment.[^31]

The link to Article 2(1) appears in the discussion of equity.[^32] The Supreme Court noted that the trial court itself saw the petition as a first step toward the petitioner's marriage to his male fiancé.[^33] It recalled that one essential requisite of marriage is the legal capacity of parties who must be a male and a female.[^34] Granting the changes, it said, would substantially reconfigure and greatly alter the laws on marriage and family relations.[^35] It also pointed to laws that apply particularly to women, such as provisions of the Labor Code, certain felonies under the Revised Penal Code and a presumption of survivorship under Rule 131.[^36]

Article 9 of the Civil Code forbids courts to decline judgment because the law is silent, obscure or insufficient.[^37] The Supreme Court held that this is not a license for judicial legislation.[^38] It left to the legislature the task of setting guidelines on the recognition of sex reassignment.[^39] The petition was denied.[^40]

### A Challenge to Articles 1 and 2 That Failed on Justiciability

Atty. Jesus Nicardo M. Falcis III filed a petition for certiorari and prohibition before the Supreme Court on May 18, 2015.[^41] He asked the Supreme Court to declare Articles 1 and 2 of the Family Code unconstitutional and to nullify Articles 46(4) and 55(6) as a consequence.[^42] He argued that strict scrutiny should apply and that same-sex couples are as capable as opposite-sex couples of founding a family.[^43] The Civil Registrar General answered that the petition was personal to Falcis and showed no injury and no actual case.[^44] It also argued that the legal definition of marriage is a policy issue for Congress.[^45]

The Supreme Court unanimously chose the path of caution.[^46] It observed that the plain text of the Constitution does not define or restrict marriage on the basis of sex, gender, sexual orientation or gender identity or expression.[^47] Even so, it held that the petition could not be entertained as a facial challenge.[^48] A facial challenge is a narrow exception that requires a showing that the law curtails free expression.[^49]

The Supreme Court also noted that the Civil Registrar General had no part in enacting the Family Code.[^50] The choice of that office as respondent showed the lack of an actual case.[^51] On standing, it held that a wish to find a partner and the normative impact of a law are not direct injuries.[^52] It added that the Family Code was enacted in 1987 and the petition was filed only in 2015.[^53] It found neither an actual case nor legal standing.[^54]

The Supreme Court then explained why the relief asked was beyond its power.[^55] Articles 1 and 2 supply a definition and basic requisites. The definition underpins many gendered provisions in the Family Code and other laws.[^56] Examples include the use of a husband's surname, the prevailing decision of the husband in some property and parental matters, the offenses of adultery and concubinage and the Paternity Leave Act.[^57] Striking down two articles would impliedly amend all of those laws and would arrogate legislative power.[^58] The Supreme Court added that Falcis never identified the other provisions affected.[^59]

The petition in intervention fared no better.[^60] The intervenors never sought a writ of mandamus compelling the Civil Registrar General to issue marriage licenses.[^61] The Supreme Court treated the intervention as a veiled attempt to cure the defects of the original petition.[^62]

In its final note, the Supreme Court expressed sympathy for same-sex couples.[^63] It said that the time for a definitive judicial fiat may not yet be here.[^64] It added that Congress might act with dispatch even before an actual case arrives.[^65] The petition and the petition in intervention were dismissed, which left Articles 1 and 2 standing.[^66] Falcis was found guilty of indirect contempt and fined P5,000.00.[^67]

## Consent Freely Given in the Presence of the Solemnizing Officer

Article 2(2) requires consent to be freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer.[^68] In [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the Supreme Court defined valid consent under Article 2 as having two indispensable qualities: it must be freely given (meaning real and not vitiated or rendered defective by fraud, force, intimidation or undue influence under Articles 45 and 46 of the Family Code) and it must be conscious or intelligent (meaning the parties are capable of understanding the nature as well as the beneficial and unfavorable consequences of marriage, unimpaired by insanity, intoxication, drugs or hypnotism).[^69]

### Marriage in Jest Versus Limited-Purpose Marriage

The Supreme Court in [*Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278) carefully distinguished a marriage in jest from a limited-purpose marriage.[^70] A marriage in jest is a pretended marriage, legal in form yet entered into as a joke with no intention of creating any legal ties whatsoever or entering into the actual marriage status, which makes it void *ab initio* for a complete absence of consent rather than for defective or vitiated consent.[^71]

By contrast, where parties deliberately intend to create the real legal bond of marriage in order to achieve a specific benefit such as acquiring foreign citizenship or money, genuine consent under Article 2(2) is present.[^72] In [*Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the respondent married an American citizen in exchange for `$2,000.00` solely to obtain American citizenship. The Supreme Court held the marriage valid and subsisting because both parties willingly and deliberately intended to establish the very legal tie required to support a citizenship application.[^73] Protected by marital privacy, motives such as convenience, companionship, money, status or title do not invalidate a marriage that satisfies all essential and formal requisites, nor does immigration fraud qualify as annulment fraud under Article 46 or entitle a co-conspirator to sue under Article 47(3).[^74]

Consistent with that principle, the Supreme Court in [*Vda. de Mijares v. Villaluz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34934) held that all essential and formal requisites under Articles 2 and 3 were satisfied when a former appellate justice knowingly and voluntarily signed a marriage contract before an authorized Municipal Circuit Trial Court judge, rejecting his defense that the marriage was merely a sham or caper of levity entered into to assist the complainant in an administrative case.[^75] Regardless of his private motive, his consent was freely given before a competent solemnizing officer. Contracting another marriage four months later warranted his suspension from the practice of law.[^76]

### Totally Simulated Marriages

Genuine consent is absent, however, when the parties never intend to marry at all and merely simulate marital documents without a ceremony or license.[^77] In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), the petitioner signed a blank marriage certificate solely to facilitate a Japanese visa application without any marriage ceremony, marriage license or *bona fide* intention to become a spouse.[^78] Emphasizing that courts must distinguish the authentic underlying consent of the parties from its external manifestation, the Supreme Court declared the marriage void *ab initio* for lack of genuine consent under Article 2(2) as well as absence of formal requisites under Article 3.[^79]

The Supreme Court gave credence to the petitioner's admission of duplicitous simulation as an admission against interest because it exposed her to possible liability for falsification. The Supreme Court also cited *People v. Santiago*, as discussed in [*Ado-an-Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), where a church wedding staged solely as a ruse to escape criminal liability for rape while the victim acted under duress was held void for lack of essential consent.[^80]

### Marriage Fraud for Immigration Purposes

Liberty Albios married Daniel Lee Fringer, an American citizen, on October 22, 2004 before a Metropolitan Trial Court judge in Mandaluyong City.[^81] On December 6, 2006, she petitioned the Regional Trial Court for a declaration of nullity.[^82] She described the marriage as one made in jest.[^83] Fringer did not answer the petition and did not attend the pre-trial.[^84]

The Regional Trial Court gave credence to Albios.[^85] It found that she had agreed to pay Fringer $2,000.00 so that he would marry her and enable her to acquire American citizenship.[^86] It also found that the parties separated after the ceremony and that she never paid because Fringer never processed her petition for citizenship.[^87] The trial court declared the marriage void ab initio.[^88] The Court of Appeals affirmed and found that consent was lacking in a case similar to a marriage in jest.[^89] The Republic argued through the Office of the Solicitor General that consent must be distinguished from motive.[^90] It maintained that both parties freely consented because their purpose would have been defeated otherwise.[^91]

The Supreme Court framed the sole question as whether a marriage contracted only to acquire American citizenship for $2,000.00 is void ab initio for lack of consent.[^92] It answered in the negative.[^93] Before ruling, it examined marriage fraud in immigration.[^94] It described a limited purpose marriage as one entered into only to achieve a particular benefit.[^95] It noted that United States immigration law asks whether the parties intended to evade immigration laws and that this inquiry does not decide legal validity.[^96] Its survey included *United States v. Rubenstein*, as cited in [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278). That case treated a marriage agreed only for immigration purposes as no marriage for want of consent. The survey also included *Mpiliris v. Hellenic Lines*, which upheld a marriage entered into for a limited purpose.[^97] It observed that United States courts have generally denied annulments of limited purpose marriages and have upheld them as valid.[^98]

Applying Article 2 to the facts, the Supreme Court found that consent was real and also conscious and intelligent.[^99] The freely given quality was shown by the conscious purpose of acquiring citizenship through marriage.[^100] The parties understood the legal tie because that precise tie was needed to reach their goal.[^101]

The Supreme Court then relied on Article 1.[^102] The avowed purpose of marriage is to establish conjugal and family life, but a possible absence of intent to build a life together does not nullify a marriage freely entered into in accordance with law.[^103] Article 1 also provides that the nature, consequences and incidents of marriage are governed by law and not by stipulation.[^104] A marriage may be declared void or voidable only on grounds provided by law.[^105] No law voids a marriage entered into for a purpose such as acquiring foreign citizenship.[^106] So long as all essential and formal requisites are present and no ground of nullity or annulment applies, the marriage is valid.[^107]

The Supreme Court grounded this view on marital privacy.[^108] The State does not dictate the kind of life a couple chooses to lead.[^109] Regulating that life would raise serious constitutional questions on the right to privacy.[^110] Marriages entered into for convenience, companionship, money, status or title are equally valid if they comply with the legal requisites.[^111]

The marriage was not voidable for fraud either.[^112] Article 46 enumerates the only circumstances that constitute fraud. Evading immigration laws is not among them.[^113] Under Article 47(3), fraud may be raised only by the injured or innocent party.[^114] There was no such party because both Albios and Fringer conspired in the sham.[^115]

The Supreme Court viewed her use of marriage for dishonest ends with disdain.[^116] It nonetheless refused to let her use the courts to escape the marriage after she had availed of its benefits.[^117] It added that marriage is an inviolable social institution protected by the Constitution and must be safeguarded from the whims of the parties.[^118] The petition was granted, the decision of the Court of Appeals was annulled and the civil case was dismissed for lack of merit.[^119]

### The Sham Marriage Defense in a Disciplinary Case

[*Vda. de Mijares v. Villaluz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34934) began as a complaint for disbarment filed on June 6, 1995 by a trial court judge against a retired justice of the Court of Appeals.[^120] She charged him with gross immorality and grave misconduct.[^121] The Supreme Court referred the case to an investigating justice and found itself in full accord with his findings.[^122]

The complainant and the respondent married on January 7, 1994 before a Municipal Circuit Trial Court judge.[^123] The respondent later claimed that the marriage was a sham that he entered into to help the complainant in an administrative case filed against her.[^124] Four months later, he married another woman after making a false statement in his marriage license application that his previous marriage had been annulled.[^125]

The Supreme Court quoted with approval the investigating justice's finding that the first marriage was valid.[^126] That finding rested on the satisfaction of all essential and formal requisites under Articles 2 and 3.[^127] The respondent knowingly and voluntarily signed the marriage contract before a judge competent to solemnize a civil marriage.[^128] Regardless of his private intention, his consent was freely given and both parties had legal capacity.[^129]

The Supreme Court added that even a caper of levity in bad taste would not speak well of the respondent's moral values.[^130] It stressed that he was a former judge and appellate justice who must have known the consequences of a marriage celebrated with all legal requisites.[^131] The findings it adopted also observed that a wrong cannot be righted by another wrong and that he could have testified in the complainant's favor instead.[^132] They also held that no one may perform a sham marriage with impunity because society protects the sanctity of marriage.[^133]

The Supreme Court found the respondent guilty of immoral conduct and deceit.[^134] It rejected disbarment as too harsh in view of his advanced age and his years of commendable service.[^135] It suspended him from the practice of law for two years.[^136]

### A Simulated Marriage That Was Registered

Rosario Ado-an-Morimoto agreed to simulate a marriage with Yoshio Morimoto to help her obtain a Japanese visa.[^137] On December 5, 2007, they met at the Manila City Hall and signed a blank marriage certificate.[^138] The solemnizing officer assured them that the certificate would never be registered.[^139] She never saw Yoshio again.[^140] Later, she learned that a marriage certificate had been registered in San Juan.[^141] It stated that a reverend had officiated the marriage on a marriage license.[^142]

She filed a petition for declaration of nullity before the Quezon City Regional Trial Court on October 5, 2009.[^143] The prosecutor reported no collusion.[^144] The trial court denied the petition on January 7, 2016 and the Court of Appeals denied her appeal on October 10, 2018.[^145]

The Supreme Court declared the marriage simulated and utterly lacking in essential and formal requisites.[^146] It restated that the absence of an essential or formal requisite makes a marriage void ab initio except under Article 35(2).[^147] A defect in an essential requisite makes the marriage voidable and an irregularity in a formal requisite leaves it valid but makes those responsible liable.[^148]

Consent is indispensable to marriage as a special contract.[^149] The Supreme Court distinguished the authentic underlying consent of the parties from its external manifestation during a ceremony.[^150] When there is no bona fide intention of becoming a spouse, a marriage is void for want of consent even if ceremonies were held.[^151] In *People v. Santiago*, as cited in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), a man who raped his niece married her in a ceremony solemnized by a church minister.[^152] The marriage was void for lack of essential consent because the ceremony was a ruse to escape criminal consequences and the victim acted under duress.[^153] The Supreme Court said that the case before it was worse because there was no ceremony at all.[^154]

The Supreme Court gave great credence to Rosario's declaration that the marriage was totally simulated.[^155] It treated the declaration as an admission against interest because it exposed her to possible liability for falsification.[^156] It cited *Go-Bangayan v. Bangayan, Jr.*, *Quinsay v. Avellaneda* and *Pomperada v. Jochico*, as cited in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), to show the risk that such admissions carry.[^157] It clarified that these references made no conclusion on her ultimate liability.[^158]

Other evidence bolstered her declaration.[^159] The report of no collusion answered any suggestion that Yoshio's silence showed duplicity on her part.[^160] The Philippine Statistics Authority also certified that it had mistakenly certified that a marriage was solemnized.[^161] The Supreme Court found the marriage totally fictitious and inexistent.[^162] It recalled the rule from *Go-Bangayan*, as cited in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), that absolutely simulated contracts are inexistent and void from the beginning under Article 1409 of the Civil Code.[^163]

The Supreme Court added a second ground.[^164] No marriage license had been issued to the parties, as a certification of the San Juan civil registrar showed.[^165] An officer of that registry testified to authenticate the certification.[^166] It was neither alleged nor shown that the marriage was exempt from a license under Articles 27 to 34.[^167]

The Supreme Court also dealt with certifications that no record exists.[^168] *Sevilla v. Cardenas*, as cited in [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), had required a categorical statement that a diligent search was made.[^169] The Supreme Court stated that it had abandoned that stringent application.[^170] Rule 132 does not require such a statement because the presumption of regularity already presumes a diligent search unless there is contrary proof.[^171]

The lower courts had hesitated to void the marriage in order to preserve its sanctity.[^172] The Supreme Court answered that insisting on the validity of a fake marriage would lend judicial approval to transactional marriages used as fronts for illicit benefits.[^173] It did not condone the ruse but held that courts must not become unsuspecting endorsers of duplicitous designs.[^174] The decision and resolution of the Court of Appeals were reversed and the marriage registered as taking place on December 5, 2007 was declared void ab initio.[^175]

## Relation to Psychological Incapacity, Bigamy and Civil Jurisdiction

Article 2 interacts with other provisions of the Family Code, criminal statutes on bigamy plus rules on trial court jurisdiction across three distinct lines of jurisprudence.

First, a declaration of nullity for psychological incapacity under Article 36 does not imply that the marriage lacked the essential requisites under Article 2.[^176] In [*Tenebro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47053), the Supreme Court pointed out that both parties to the second marriage were over eighteen years old, legally capacitated under Article 5 while freely consenting before a judge with a license and two witnesses, so all essential and formal requisites were met.[^177] Because a subsequent marriage contracted during the subsistence of a valid first marriage still produces legal consequences and already satisfies the elements of bigamy upon celebration, an Article 36 nullity judgment on the second marriage does not absolve the accused of bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code.[^178]

Second, where the second marriage is alleged to be void *ab initio* for an actual absence of statutory requisites under Articles 2 and 3, estoppel cannot substitute for the missing requisites to sustain a bigamy conviction.[^179] In [*Genio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), the second marriage was impugned for lack of an authorized solemnizing officer and lack of a marriage ceremony. The accused had invoked the Article 34 license exemption, but that exemption dispenses only with the license and never with the ceremony. The good faith exception of Article 35(2) was not shown.[^180] Applying *Pulido v. People*, the Supreme Court held that estoppel cannot defeat positive marriage law and acquitted the accused of bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code for failure to prove every element beyond reasonable doubt, while finding him guilty of violating Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code for knowingly contracting an illegal marriage.[^181]

Third, even when a marriage is solemnized in church, its civil validity and legal consequences remain governed by the Family Code's essential and formal requisites rather than by Canon Law.[^182] In [*Tilar v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63283), the Supreme Court held that an ecclesiastical proceeding does not bind civil courts. Regional Trial Courts possess exclusive original jurisdiction under Section 19(15) of Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 over petitions for declaration of nullity of church-solemnized marriages without violating the separation of Church and State.[^183]

### Nullity for Psychological Incapacity and the Crime of Bigamy

Veronico Tenebro married Leticia Ancajas on April 10, 1990 before a city trial court judge in Lapu-Lapu City.[^184] In 1991, he told her that he had been married to Hilda Villareyes on November 10, 1986.[^185] On January 25, 1993, he married a third woman.[^186] Ancajas then filed a complaint for bigamy.[^187]

Tenebro denied that a marriage ceremony ever took place with Villareyes.[^188] He claimed that he signed a marriage contract only so that she could get an allotment from his work as a seaman.[^189] The Regional Trial Court convicted him of bigamy on November 10, 1997 and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^190] His marriage to Ancajas was later declared void ab initio on the ground of psychological incapacity.[^191]

The Supreme Court listed the elements of bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code.[^192] They are a legal first marriage, no legal dissolution of it, a second or subsequent marriage and a second marriage that has all the essential requisites for validity.[^193]

On the first marriage, the Supreme Court gave more weight to the marriage contract than to certifications that no record existed.[^194] The marriage contract was a certified public document and the best evidence of its contents.[^195] The certifications attested only to the absence of a record and not to the absence of a marriage.[^196] No law makes the submission of a marriage contract to the civil registrar a condition for validity.[^197] A missing record therefore does not invalidate a marriage.[^198] The certifications were also dated after the second marriage.[^199]

Tenebro argued that the nullity declaration retroacted and erased the crime.[^200] The Supreme Court held that a declaration of nullity for psychological incapacity is of no moment for the penal laws.[^201] The second marriage was void anyway because it was contracted during a valid first marriage.[^202] Article 349 punishes the mere act of contracting a second marriage during the subsistence of a valid marriage.[^203] Bigamy was therefore consummated when the second marriage was celebrated.[^204]

The Supreme Court also held that the nullity declaration did not show that the second marriage lacked essential requisites.[^205] Both parties were over eighteen and voluntarily married with a license before a judge and two witnesses.[^206]

A declaration of nullity retroacts as to the bond between the spouses.[^207] The Supreme Court added that such a marriage is not without legal effects.[^208] Children conceived or born before the judgment are legitimate.[^209] Criminal liability for bigamy is among the legal consequences.[^210] A contrary holding would let offenders flaw each marriage to escape the penal law.[^211]

The Supreme Court noted that Tenebro married a third time while two marriages subsisted.[^212] It denied the petition and affirmed the conviction in toto with an indeterminate penalty of four years and two months of prision correccional as minimum to eight years and one day of prision mayor as maximum.[^213]

### A Void Second Marriage as a Defense to Bigamy

Rommel Genio married Magdalena Esler on May 20, 2006 before the mayor of Cabanatuan City.[^214] He was charged with bigamy for marrying Maricar Galapon on September 7, 2013 in Guimba, Nueva Ecija.[^215] The marriage certificate stated that the mayor solemnized the second marriage at his office before three witnesses.[^216] The defense admitted the existence and authenticity of the certificate.[^217]

Genio argued that the second marriage was void because the mayor never appeared and no ceremony took place.[^218] Maricar testified that the civil registrar officiated at a simple ceremony in her home.[^219] Her sister testified that the mayor was absent and that no vows or rings were exchanged.[^220] The Regional Trial Court convicted him on April 26, 2019 and the Court of Appeals affirmed on July 16, 2021.[^221] The Court of Appeals required clear and convincing evidence to rebut the presumed regularity of the certificate.[^222] It also held that Genio could not escape liability because he signed the document and caused its misrepresentation.[^223]

The Supreme Court framed the sole issue as whether the prosecution proved beyond reasonable doubt that the second marriage had all the essential and formal requisites.[^224] It reiterated that an accused in bigamy may raise a void second marriage without a prior judicial declaration of nullity.[^225] If the second marriage was void on grounds other than being bigamous, the element of a second marriage is lacking and acquittal follows.[^226] It tied this rule to Articles 2, 3 and 4 and noted that the absence of a requisite makes the marriage void ab initio except under Article 35(2).[^227] It recalled *Morigo v. People*, as cited in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), where the accused was acquitted because the parties merely signed a contract without a solemnizing officer.[^228]

The Supreme Court held that the Court of Appeals erred in requiring clear and convincing evidence from the accused.[^229] That standard fits civil cases and cannot extend to criminal cases where the State bears the burden on every element.[^230] The accused need only present substantial evidence that contradicts the presumed fact.[^231] The presumption is overthrown when there is even an iota of doubt that the presumed fact follows from the basic fact.[^232]

Maricar, her sister and a third witness uniformly testified that the mayor never appeared.[^233] The Supreme Court found them competent to testify on the ceremony.[^234] It rejected the view that Maricar was untrustworthy because she was a party, since a second spouse who was duped is also an offended party.[^235] Their testimonies produced reasonable doubt that the solemnities were observed.[^236] The certificate then ceased to be proof of its contents and the burden returned to the prosecution.[^237] To prove the fourth element, the prosecution was left with only the certificate and a complainant who was absent from the ceremony.[^238]

The Supreme Court refused to apply estoppel against Genio.[^239] Estoppel is a principle of equity that cannot be applied against positive law. Articles 2 and 3 set out the essential and formal requisites.[^240] It distinguished *Santiago v. People*, as cited in [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), where the accused lied to the solemnizing officer to invoke the license exemption of Article 34 and so the marriage ostensibly had all requisites.[^241] Genio's case differed because the Family Code does not allow a marriage ceremony to be dispensed with.[^242] As to the authority of the solemnizing officer, the marriage is void unless the good faith exception of Article 35(2) applies. None was shown.[^243]

The Supreme Court nevertheless found Genio guilty under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code through the variance doctrine.[^244] It held that knowingly contracting a marriage against the provisions of law is included in bigamy.[^245] The elements are a marriage, knowledge that legal requirements were not complied with or that a legal impediment exists and that the accused is not within Article 349.[^246] The information sufficed because willfully carries the meaning of knowingly.[^247] Genio contracted the marriage, as the certificate he signed and the testimony of Maricar showed.[^248] He knew of his first marriage as an impediment because he stated in the certificate that he was single.[^249] He also failed to comply with Article 40, which requires a final judgment declaring a prior marriage void.[^250]

The Supreme Court granted the petition in part and set aside the bigamy conviction on reasonable doubt.[^251] It found Genio guilty of knowingly contracting a marriage against the provisions of law and imposed six months of arresto mayor as minimum to three years, six months and twenty-one days of prision correccional as maximum.[^252]

### Church Solemnization and Civil Jurisdiction

Jerrysus Tilar married his wife on June 29, 1996 in a Catholic church in Cebu.[^253] On November 4, 2010, he petitioned the Regional Trial Court of Baybay City for a declaration of nullity on the ground of psychological incapacity under Article 36.[^254] The trial court dismissed the petition on June 3, 2014 for lack of jurisdiction.[^255] It reasoned that marriage is a sacrament governed by Canon Law and that the State cannot encroach on the Church.[^256] Tilar went directly to the Supreme Court and the Solicitor General agreed that courts have jurisdiction.[^257]

The Supreme Court began with Article XV, Section 2 of the Constitution on the inviolability of marriage.[^258] It stressed that marriage is not only a civil contract but also an institution whose maintenance the public is deeply interested in.[^259] It then quoted Article 1 of the Family Code and observed that the terms of marriage are governed by law and not by stipulation.[^260] The Family Code provides the essential and formal requisites for validity and requires no prescribed form or religious rite.[^261] The parties must appear personally before the solemnizing officer and declare before two witnesses of legal age that they take each other as husband and wife.[^262] Article 7 allows an authorized and registered priest or minister to solemnize marriages and Article 8 allows solemnization in a church.[^263]

Tilar's marriage was solemnized by a Catholic priest in a church in accordance with those provisions.[^264] A marriage may be a sacrament in the Catholic church but it has civil consequences governed by the Family Code.[^265] The petition sought to nullify only the civil marriage contract and an ecclesiastical declaration is another matter.[^266] Church annulment proceedings under Canon Law do not bind the State, so the separation of church and state finds no application.[^267]

The grounds for terminating marriage may be invoked in a petition for annulment or declaration of nullity decided only by the court with jurisdiction.[^268] Section 19(15) of Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 gives Regional Trial Courts exclusive original jurisdiction over actions involving the contract of marriage and marital relations.[^269] The trial court therefore erred in dismissing the petition for lack of jurisdiction.[^270] The petition was granted and the trial court was ordered to proceed with the case on the evidence presented.[^271]

## Related Provisions

- [Article 1](art-001.md): Defines marriage as a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman.
- [Article 3](art-003.md): Enumerates the formal requisites of a valid marriage.
- [Article 4](art-004.md): Prescribes void *ab initio* status for absence of essential or formal requisites (except under Article 35(2)) and voidability for defective essential requisites.
- [Article 5](art-005.md): Fixes the minimum marrying age of eighteen years for any male or female not subject to Articles 37 or 38 impediments.
- [Article 35](art-035.md): Lists marriages void from the beginning, including those contracted below age eighteen or without an authorized solemnizing officer absent good faith.
- [Article 36](art-036.md): Provides psychological incapacity as a ground for nullity distinct from lack of Article 2 requisites.
- [Article 45](art-045.md) and [Article 46](art-046.md): Govern voidable marriages where consent was vitiated by fraud, force, intimidation, or undue influence.

## Key Cases

- [*Tenebro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47053) (2004): Distinguishes Article 2 essential requisites and Article 3 formal requisites from Article 36 psychological incapacity and affirms bigamy liability despite an Article 36 nullity decree.[^280]
- [*Silverio v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44532) (2007): Holds that civil-registry sex is fixed at birth absent legislation and that the change sought would substantially reconfigure and greatly alter the laws on marriage and family relations, with guidelines on sex reassignment left to the legislature.[^281]
- [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278) (2013): Defines freely given and conscious consent under Article 2(2), distinguishing void marriages in jest from valid limited-purpose marriages entered into for citizenship or money.[^282]
- [*Falcis III v. Civil Registrar General*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65744) (2019): Explains that Articles 1 and 2 reflect a statutory heteronormative model embedded in interrelated laws and dismisses the facial challenge for lack of justiciability because the relief sought would impliedly amend interconnected laws and arrogate legislative power.[^283]
- [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555) (2021): Declares a totally simulated visa marriage void *ab initio* for lack of authentic underlying consent under Article 2(2) and absence of formal requisites under Article 3.[^284]
- [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309) (2024): Holds that estoppel cannot supplant Articles 2 and 3 positive requisites in a bigamy prosecution under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code, while convicting the accused under Article 350.[^285]
- [*Vda. de Mijares v. Villaluz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34934) (1997): Rejects a sham-marriage defense where a party knowingly signed a marriage contract before an authorized judge, holding that private motive does not negate Article 2 consent.[^286]
- [*Tilar v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63283) (2017): Confirms that the Family Code's essential and formal requisites govern the civil effects of church-solemnized marriages within the exclusive jurisdiction of Regional Trial Courts.[^287]

## Suggested Answers

### Question 1

A Filipino citizen and a foreign national sign a blank marriage certificate solely to support a visa application, with no marriage ceremony or marriage license and no intention to live as spouses. Compare the validity of that arrangement with a second scenario in which two parties appear before an authorized solemnizing officer with a valid marriage license and exchange vows solely so one spouse may acquire foreign citizenship in exchange for `$2,000.00`.

**Answer:**

The first arrangement is void *ab initio*, whereas the second marriage is valid and subsisting. Under Article 2(2) of the Family Code, valid consent must be freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer. Under Article 4, the absence of any essential or formal requisite renders the marriage void from the beginning.[^288]

In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), the Supreme Court distinguished authentic underlying consent from its external manifestation and held that a totally simulated marriage where the parties sign a blank marriage certificate solely to facilitate a visa without any ceremony, license or intent to become spouses is void *ab initio* for lack of genuine consent and formal requisites.[^289] By contrast, in [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the Supreme Court ruled that a limited-purpose marriage entered into for `$2,000.00` to obtain American citizenship is not a marriage in jest because both parties deliberately intended to create the real legal tie required to support the citizenship application. Private motives do not invalidate consent or render the marriage voidable under Article 46 or Article 47(3).[^290]

### Question 2

After undergoing sex reassignment surgery abroad, a biological male files a petition under Rule 108 to change his first name and sex entry in his birth certificate from male to female so he can marry his male fiancé, invoking equity under Article 9 of the Civil Code and constitutional protection for marriage. Should the court grant the petition?

**Answer:**

No, the petition should be denied. Under Article 2(1) of the Family Code, one of the essential requisites of marriage is the legal capacity of the contracting parties who must be a male and a female.[^291]

In [*Silverio v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44532), the Supreme Court held that under Act No. 3753, sex is determined visually at birth and cannot be altered in the civil registry absent enabling legislation from Congress. The Supreme Court held that the change sought would substantially reconfigure and greatly alter the laws on marriage and family relations, since it would allow the union of a man with another man who has undergone sex reassignment.[^292] The Supreme Court added that Article 9 of the Civil Code does not authorize judicial legislation on grounds of equity. In [*Falcis III v. Civil Registrar General*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65744), the Supreme Court later dismissed a facial challenge to Articles 1 and 2 for lack of justiciability. It reasoned that the relief sought would impliedly amend many interrelated laws and arrogate legislative power.[^293]

### Question 3

During the subsistence of a valid first marriage, X contracts a second marriage before a judge with a valid marriage license and witnesses before later obtaining a judicial declaration of nullity of that second marriage on the ground of psychological incapacity under Article 36. In a separate case, Y contracts a second marriage while a first marriage subsists by signing a marriage certificate without an authorized solemnizing officer or marriage ceremony. Can X or Y avoid criminal liability for bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code?

**Answer:**

X cannot avoid bigamy liability on the basis of Article 36, whereas Y's prosecution for bigamy under Article 349 fails if the absence of essential or formal requisites leaves an element of bigamy unproven beyond reasonable doubt, although Y may incur liability under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code.

In [*Tenebro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47053), the Supreme Court held that an Article 36 declaration of nullity does not negate the presence of Article 2 essential requisites or Article 3 formal requisites at the time of celebration and does not absolve the accused of bigamy under Article 349.[^294] In [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), however, the Supreme Court held that estoppel cannot supply missing statutory requisites under Articles 2 and 3, so when the second marriage lacks an authorized solemnizing officer outside Article 35(2) and lacks a marriage ceremony, conviction for bigamy under Article 349 is unwarranted for lack of proof beyond reasonable doubt, even as the accused remains guilty under Article 350 for knowingly contracting an illegal marriage.[^295]

# Quotes

> "The changes sought by petitioner will have serious and wide-ranging legal and public policy consequences. First, even the trial court itself found that the petition was but petitioner’s first step towards his eventual marriage to his male fiancé. However, marriage, one of the most sacred social institutions, is a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman. One of its essential requisites is the legal capacity of the contracting parties who must be a male and a female. To grant the changes sought by petitioner will substantially reconfigure and greatly alter the laws on marriage and family relations. It will allow the union of a man with another man who has undergone sex reassignment (a male-to-female post-operative transsexual). Second, there are various laws which apply particularly to women such as the provisions of the Labor Code on employment of women, certain felonies under the Revised Penal Code and the presumption of survivorship in case of calamities under Rule 131 of the Rules of Court, among others. These laws underscore the public policy in relation to women which could be substantially affected if petitioner’s petition were to be granted."[^296]

> "Articles 1 and 2 of the Family Code provide a definition and spell out basic requisites, respectively. Without passing upon the validity of the definition under Article 1, this Court nonetheless observes that this definition serves as the foundation of many other gendered provisions of the Family Code and other laws."[^297]

> "Under said Article 2, for consent to be valid, it must be (1) freely given and (2) made in the presence of a solemnizing officer. A “freely given” consent requires that the contracting parties willingly and deliberately enter into the marriage. Consent must be real in the sense that it is not vitiated nor rendered defective by any of the vices of consent under Articles 45 and 46 of the Family Code, such as fraud, force, intimidation, and undue influence. Consent must also be conscious or intelligent, in that the parties must be capable of intelligently understanding the nature of, and both the beneficial or unfavorable consequences of their act. Their understanding should not be affected by insanity, intoxication, drugs, or hypnotism."[^298]

> "In ruling that Albios’ marriage was void for lack of consent, the CA characterized such as akin to a marriage by way of jest. A marriage in jest is a pretended marriage, legal in form but entered into as a joke, with no real intention of entering into the actual marriage status, and with a clear understanding that the parties would not be bound. The ceremony is not followed by any conduct indicating a purpose to enter into such a relation. It is a pretended marriage not intended to be real and with no intention to create any legal ties whatsoever, hence, the absence of any genuine consent. Marriages in jest are void ab initio, not for vitiated, defective, or unintelligent consent, but for a complete absence of consent. There is no genuine consent because the parties have absolutely no intention of being bound in any way or for any purpose."[^299]

> "It is vital to distinguish the authentic, underlying consent of the parties from the external manifestation of such consent during a marriage ceremony. Jurisprudence therefore recognizes that, when there is no bona fide intention of becoming a spouse to another, a marriage is void for want of consent even when marriage ceremonies have been conducted and, there, the parties declared their intent to enter into married life."[^300]

> "Moreover, the declaration of the nullity of the second marriage on the ground of psychological incapacity is not an indicator that petitioner’s marriage to Ancajas lacks the essential requisites for validity. The requisites for the validity of a marriage are classified by the Family Code into essential (legal capacity of the contracting parties and their consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer) and formal (authority of the solemnizing officer, marriage license, and marriage ceremony wherein the parties personally declare their agreement to marry before the solemnizing officer in the presence of at least two witnesses). Under Article 5 of the Family Code, any male or female of the age of eighteen years or upwards not under any of the impediments mentioned in Articles 37 and 38 may contract marriage."[^301]

> "Estoppel is a principle of equity that may only be applied in the absence of, and never against, positive law. Relevantly, Articles 2 and 3 of the Family Code unequivocally provide the essential requisites for a valid marriage. Thus, the Court cannot apply estoppel and supplant the clear requirements of the Family Code, notwithstanding any alleged misrepresentation on the part of Rommel."[^302]

> "That what complainant and respondent contracted was a valid marriage is borne out by law and the evidence. To be sure, all the essential and formal requisites of a valid marriage under Articles 2 and 3 of the Family Code, i.e., legal capacity of the contracting parties, who must be a male and a female; consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer; authority of the solemnizing officer; a valid marriage license except in the cases provided for in Chapter 2 of Title I on marriage, Family Code; and a marriage ceremony with the appearance of the contracting parties before the solemnizing officer, and their personal declaration that they take each other as husband and wife, in the presence of not less than two witnesses of legal age, were satisfied and complied with."[^303]

> "Thus, the contract of marriage is entered into by complying with the requirements and formalities prescribed by law. The marriage of petitioner and respondent which was solemnized by a Catholic priest and was held in a church was in accordance with the above-quoted provisions. Although, marriage is considered a sacrament in the Catholic church, it has civil and legal consequences which are governed by the Family Code. As petitioner correctly pointed out, the instant petition only seeks to nullify the marriage contract between the parties as postulated in the Family Code of the Philippines; and the declaration of nullity of the parties' marriage in the religious and ecclesiastical aspect is another matter. Notably, the proceedings for church annulment which is in accordance with the norms of Canon Law is not binding upon the State as the couple is still considered married to each other in the eyes of the civil law. Thus, the principle of separation of the church and state finds no application in this case."[^304]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Quick Diagnostic Checklist for Article 2 Problems.** Before you call a marriage void, voidable or valid, ask four questions in this order:
> 1. **Is there legal capacity?** Check the age of eighteen, the impediments in Articles 37 and 38 and the requirement of a male and a female. In [*Silverio v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44532), the Supreme Court refused to change the sex entry of a post-operative transsexual so that he could marry a male fiancé. In [*Falcis III v. Civil Registrar General*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65744), it dismissed a challenge to Articles 1 and 2 for lack of an actual case and standing. Both provisions therefore still stand.
> 2. **Is there any consent at all?** Ask whether the parties meant to create the marital bond. If they never intended to be spouses, consent is absent and the marriage is void from the beginning. A marriage in jest and a totally simulated marriage both fall here.
> 3. **Is the consent only defective?** Fraud, force, intimidation and undue influence under Articles 45 and 46 make the marriage voidable. They do not make it void. Evading immigration laws is not fraud under Article 46.
> 4. **Were the formal requisites also present?** A simulated marriage usually fails Article 3 as well. In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), the Supreme Court found a second ground in the missing marriage license.

### Motive Is Not Consent

> [!TIP]
> **Separate the reason for marrying from the intent to marry.** Money, citizenship, convenience, status and companionship are motives. They do not erase consent if the parties truly meant to create the legal tie. Use this contrast on every bar question.
> - **Valid:** In [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), the respondent married an American citizen for $2,000.00 to obtain American citizenship. Both parties deliberately created the legal tie that the application needed. The marriage stayed valid.
> - **Valid:** In [*Vda. de Mijares v. Villaluz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34934), a former appellate justice signed a marriage contract before an authorized judge and later called it a sham. His private motive did not negate his consent.
> - **Void:** In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), the petitioner signed a blank certificate for a visa. There was no ceremony and no license, so the marriage was void.
> The test is whether the parties wanted the marriage itself. It is not whether they wanted the benefit that came with it.

### Who Can Attack the Marriage and How

> [!NOTE]
> **A fraud theory needs an innocent party.** Under Article 47(3), fraud may be raised only by the injured or innocent party. In [*Republic v. Albios*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56278), both spouses took part in the scheme, so no one could invoke fraud. Do not choose annulment for fraud when both spouses are in on the plan.
> The simulation theory works differently because it asks whether a marriage existed at all. Expect the court to look for proof beyond the petitioner's word. In [*Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67555), the Supreme Court treated the petitioner's confession as an admission against interest because it exposed her to liability for falsification. It also relied on a registrar's certification and on the report of no collusion.

### Nullity Does Not Erase Bigamy

> [!TIP]
> **Do not treat Article 36 as proof that Article 2 failed.** In [*Tenebro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47053), both parties to the second marriage were over eighteen and consented freely before a judge. They had a license and two witnesses. A later nullity decree for psychological incapacity did not remove the crime. Bigamy was consummated when the second marriage was celebrated.
> **Estoppel cannot fill a missing requisite.** In [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), the second marriage lacked an authorized solemnizing officer and a ceremony. The Supreme Court refused to apply estoppel against the accused because Articles 2 and 3 are positive law. It acquitted him of bigamy for reasonable doubt and found him guilty under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code. The Article 34 exemption removes the license only and never the ceremony.
> **The standard of proof depends on the case.** In a civil nullity case, the petitioner must overcome the presumption of regularity of the marriage certificate. In a bigamy prosecution, the accused need only present substantial evidence that contradicts the presumed fact. The State carries the burden on every element.

### Church Weddings and Court Jurisdiction

> [!NOTE]
> **A church ceremony does not move the case to the Church.** In [*Tilar v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63283), the husband was married in a Catholic church. The Supreme Court held that the civil marriage is governed by the Family Code. Church annulment does not bind the State. Regional Trial Courts have exclusive original jurisdiction over a petition to declare the marriage void. Do not answer a jurisdiction question with Canon Law.
