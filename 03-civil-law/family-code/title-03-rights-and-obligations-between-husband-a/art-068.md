> Art. 68. The husband and wife are obliged to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, and render mutual help and support. (109a)

[^family-code]

# Explanation

## Constitutional Basis and Scope

Article 68 of the Family Code codifies the mutual personal obligations between husband and wife to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity and render mutual help and support.[^family-code] It implements the constitutional mandate recognizing marriage as an inviolable social institution and the foundation of the family.[^2] Sitting En Banc in [*Guevarra v. Atty. Eala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44146), the Supreme Court noted that Executive Order No. 209 echoes Section 2, Article XV of the Constitution by imposing these mutual marital covenants upon spouses.[^3] In [*Fujiki v. Marinay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55916), the Supreme Court further explained that a spouse's substantive interest in protecting the marriage extends beyond constitutional property rights to the relational rights recognized under Title III of the Family Code.[^4]

None of the decisions reviewed for this article adjudicates a standalone civil action filed strictly between spouses to compel performance of Article 68 cohabitation or mutual respect outside nullity, *habeas corpus*, support-related, electoral domicile, social security, criminal or bar disciplinary proceedings.[^5] [^6] Nor does any of them directly apply Civil Code Article 109, the statutory predecessor indicated in the codal text.[^family-code] As established across [*Chi Ming Tsoi v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34416) and [*Ilusorio v. Ilusorio-Bildner*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52421), courts do not enforce cohabitation or consortium by direct judicial compulsion because spontaneous mutual affection provides the sanction for these personal covenants.[^7] [^8]

## Mutual Obligation to Live Together

### Spontaneous Affection Versus Judicial Compulsion

Under Article 68, the legal obligation of spouses to live together cannot be enforced by a writ of *habeas corpus* or court order directing marital consortium.[^9] In [*Ilusorio v. Ilusorio-Bildner*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52421), the wife filed a *habeas corpus* petition in the Court of Appeals seeking custody of her husband in consortium after the spouses had lived apart since 1972.[^10] After the appellate court dismissed the petition for lack of unlawful restraint and the Supreme Court initially dismissed her appeal and nullified a CA visitation order, the wife moved for reconsideration citing Article XII of the 1987 Constitution alongside Articles 68 and 69 of the Family Code.[^11] Denying reconsideration, which had also become moot upon the husband's June 28, 2001 death, the Supreme Court agreed that the Constitution and Articles 68 and 69 oblige spouses to live together and care for each other.[^12] However, echoing its earlier pronouncement in [*Chi Ming Tsoi v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34416) that quoted *Cuaderno v. Cuaderno* (120 Phil. 1298), the Supreme Court held that no legal mandate or court order may compel consortium because the sanction for Article 68 is "spontaneous, mutual affection between husband and wife".[^13] [^14]

### Presumption of Common Residence in Election Law

Although courts cannot compel spouses to live together, Article 68 and Article 69 create a working legal presumption of common marital residence when evaluating statutory residence qualifications.[^15] In [*Limbona v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45452) (`G.R. No. 181097`), a rival candidate sought to disqualify petitioner Norlainie Limbona from running for Mayor of Pantar, Lanao del Norte for lacking the one-year residency requirement.[^16] Affirming the Commission on Elections Resolutions disqualifying petitioner, the Supreme Court held that Articles 68 and 69 support the presumption that husband and wife live together in one legal residence.[^17] Because petitioner failed to prove she maintained a separate residence from her husband, who transferred domicile to Pantar only on November 11, 2006, her domicile changed by operation of law only on that same date.[^18] In the sequel [*Limbona v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50224) (`G.R. No. 186006`), after she filed as a substitute for her disqualified husband and was disqualified again, the En Banc Supreme Court held itself bound by its earlier ruling and reproduced Articles 68 and 69.[^19]

### Statutory Dependency Limit Under Social Security Law

The mutual support and cohabitation duties under Article 68 do not dispense with statutory proof of actual dependency under social security legislation.[^20] In [*Social Security Commission v. Favila*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37424), a legal wife who had lived apart *de facto* from her husband for roughly 17 years before his death claimed survivor death benefits.[^21] The Social Security Commission denied her claim, while the Court of Appeals reversed that denial.[^22] The wife relied on *Ceneta v. Social Security System*, a Court of Appeals ruling that invoked Article 68 to treat a legal wife as dependent for support.[^23] Reversing the appellate court, the Supreme Court found untenable her claim that a legal wife is presumed dependent. Under Section 8(e) and (k) of Republic Act No. 1161, she had to prove actual dependency on her husband at the time of his death. She failed to do so.[^24]

### Formal Cohabitation Versus Deliberate Estrangement and Coercive Control

Living under the same roof does not establish compliance with Article 68 when one spouse subjects the other to coercive control, violence or apathy.[^25] In [*Tani-De La Fuente v. De La Fuente, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62960), the trial court declared the marriage void under Article 36, but the Court of Appeals reversed after finding the psychologist's testimony hearsay and the husband's behavior not shown to come from a psychological illness.[^26] Reinstating the trial court's decree, the Supreme Court held that although the spouses physically lived together, the husband failed to comply with Article 68 through extreme jealousy, stalking, isolation from family and friends, gun threats, sexual maltreatment and apathy.[^27] Similarly, in [*Maristela-Cuan v. Cuan, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67962), the spouses married at the Quezon City Hall in 1997 without telling their parents and never lived under one roof. They never consummated the marriage. The husband's unfounded jealousy turned violent three months after the wedding.[^28] Reversing the Court of Appeals under the [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) standard, the Supreme Court found only the husband incapacitated. It held that he failed the most basic marital obligations to love, respect and live together with his wife.[^29] In [*Dela Cruz-Lanuza v. Lanuza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69467), the trial court denied the wife's petition and the Court of Appeals dismissed her petition as the improper remedy. The Supreme Court reversed and declared the marriage void. It noted that the husband left his family in 1994, gave no financial support, visited his children once for less than an hour and appears to have contracted marriage several times with different women. These facts showed that he did not understand his obligations as a husband and father.[^30] In [*Calma v. Santos-Calma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66925), the husband worked abroad on a three-year contract. The Supreme Court found that his wife could not settle in a common abode, squandered the support he sent and left him for another partner. It held that she was utterly incapable of living together, observing mutual love, respect and fidelity and rendering mutual help and support.[^31]

## Mutual Love, Respect and Sexual Autonomy

### Marital Rape Versus Unjustified Refusal of Sexual Cooperation

Marriage does not confer property rights over a spouse's body or permit forced sexual intercourse.[^32] Affirming convictions for two counts of marital rape by the trial court and the Court of Appeals in [*People v. Jumawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56922), the Supreme Court held that the Family Code obligates spouses to love one another, but this rule sanctions only spontaneous and mutual affection and sexual intimacy rather than intimacy exacted unilaterally by force or coercion.[^33] A spouse aggrieved by persistent sexual refusal may seek judicial declaration of nullity under Article 36 rather than resorting to force.[^34] Earlier, in [*Chi Ming Tsoi v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34416), the trial court and the Court of Appeals declared a marriage void where the husband persistently and senselessly avoided sexual intercourse for almost ten months despite physical capability.[^35] Affirming nullity, the Supreme Court observed that while Article 68 relies on spontaneous mutual affection rather than judicial compulsion, protracted and senseless refusal of sexual cooperation defeats procreation and essential marital communion.[^36]

### Honesty, Domination and Respect

Mutual love and respect under Article 68 presuppose truth, empathy and equal partnership.[^37] [^38] In [*Antonio v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40916), the trial court voided the marriage due to the wife's pervasive fabrication of stories, friends and background, which the Court of Appeals reversed.[^39] Reinstating nullity, the Supreme Court held that a pathological liar unable to adhere to reality cannot comply with Article 68 obligations founded on love, trust and respect.[^40] It carefully distinguished fraud under Articles 45(3) and 46 (which vitiates the deceived spouse's consent) from the lying spouse's own incapacity under Article 36.[^41] Under the standard set in [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), both the trial court and the Court of Appeals in [*Tee Ten v. Tee Ten*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_259322_2025.html) voided the marriage where the wife acted with an arrogant sense of entitlement, monitored her husband's every move, attacked him physically, alienated their children and falsely accused him of incest.[^42] Affirming the nullity decree and remanding support *pendente lite* and Article 147 property liquidation, the Supreme Court singled out Article 68 as "the most basic" of the essential marital obligations.[^43] Similarly, in [*Fopalan v. Fopalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68588), the husband's public insolence toward his wife, rejection of their autistic child and chronic philandering violated Articles 68 to 71.[^44]

## Mutual Fidelity

### Fidelity as the Norm in Nullity Cases

Under Article 68, fidelity is a mutual legal standard rather than an aspirational ideal.[^45] In [*Quiogue v. Quiogue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68704), the husband Antonio petitioned to void his own marriage. The trial court found both spouses incapacitated under Article 36, but the Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the petition.[^46] Reversing the appellate court, the Supreme Court declared the marriage void solely on account of the husband's chronic and antecedent infidelity.[^47] Interpreting Article 68, the Supreme Court stressed that the law recognizes fidelity as the norm and that a spouse should not be made to settle for anything less than absolute faithfulness.[^48] It emphasized that a single indiscretion does not automatically void a marriage, yet chronic philandering paired with condescending entitlement toward an unemployed housewife satisfied gravity, antecedence and legal incurability under [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), whereas the wife's retaliatory anger during the marriage did not amount to psychological incapacity.[^49] In [*Halog v. Halog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68037), where the husband committed repeated infidelities, pointed a gun at his wife, married another woman in Qatar and abandoned his family, the Supreme Court reinstated the trial court's nullity decree and quoted Article 68 verbatim.[^50]

### Marital Infidelity and Abandonment Under R.A. No. 9262

The Supreme Court directly linked Article 68 to psychological violence under Section 5(i) of Republic Act No. 9262 in two key criminal appeals.[^51] [^52] In [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69419) (`G.R. No. 263449`), the husband abruptly deserted the conjugal home with their househelper after being caught kissing her, leaving his wife to face debts and distress until she discovered his new child online years later.[^53] Modifying only the prison term while affirming conviction by the trial court and the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court applied Article 68 directly to hold that sudden abandonment constitutes psychological violence because the duties to cohabit, love, respect and support cannot be performed after abandonment.[^54] It clarified that his abandonment, rather than unproven ongoing distress over infidelity during the six-year gap, caused her mental anguish.[^55]

Sitting En Banc in [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69576) (`G.R. No. 252739`), the Supreme Court affirmed the conviction of a husband who maintained a mistress and sired a four-year-old child while married.[^56] Expressly holding that marital infidelity violates the spousal obligations under Article 68 in relation to Article 55 of the Family Code, the En Banc Supreme Court ruled that infidelity is not conduct innocent in itself, so intent to cause anguish under Section 5(i) is presumed when the wife suffers resulting emotional distress.[^57] At the same time, it recognized boundaries where extramarital relationships occur between estranged spouses or consciously consenting spouses and therefore do not inflict psychological suffering.[^58]

### Disciplinary Liability of Lawyers for Breach of Marital Fidelity

Violations of spousal fidelity also establish grossly immoral conduct in attorney disbarment proceedings.[^59] In [*Guevarra v. Atty. Eala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44146), the Integrated Bar of the Philippines Board of Governors passed Resolution No. XVII-2006-06 in a complaint against a married lawyer who carried on an affair and fathered a child with complainant's wife.[^60] Annulling that resolution and disbarring the lawyer, the En Banc Supreme Court invoked Section 2, Article XV of the Constitution and the Family Code duty of spouses to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity and render mutual help and support.[^61] The subsequent judicial declaration of nullity of the woman's marriage did not erase the offense because the acts transpired while her marriage subsisted.[^62] Likewise, in [*Cojuangco, Jr. v. Palma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43593), the En Banc Supreme Court denied a motion to vacate disbarment where a married lawyer contracted a bigamous second marriage, ruling that financially providing for one's children does not excuse violating a husband's duties to live with his wife, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity and render help and support.[^63] Both disciplinary rulings state these Family Code duties and cite Article 68 only in a footnote.[^64] [^65]

## Mutual Help and Support

### Equal Spousal Responsibility Beyond Traditional Gender Roles

Article 68 and Article 70 distribute mutual help and family support equally between husband and wife regardless of changing economic roles.[^66] In [*Azcueta v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49232), the husband pretended to work, depended solely on his mother's allowance, made no independent household decisions and lived with his mother.[^67] Reversing the Court of Appeals and reinstating the trial court's nullity decree, the Supreme Court held that he failed to discharge Article 68 duties.[^68] It observed that although women increasingly contribute financially amid shifting gender roles, spouses must still cooperate and share equal responsibility for the support and well-being of the family.[^69]

Following [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), Supreme Court decisions enforce this mutual standard symmetrically against both husbands and wives.[^70] [^71] In [*Egmalis-Ke-eg v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68461), the husband stayed unemployed, drank habitually and spent family support while the wife worked abroad.[^72] Reversing the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court declared the marriage void for the husband's incapacity under Articles 68 and 69, while reversing the trial court's unpleaded finding against the wife because she actively worked to support the family.[^73] In [*Dedicatoria v. Dedicatoria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68584), extreme dependence on parents, refusal to work or establish an independent home and cohabiting with another partner breached the pledge of mutual help and fidelity.[^74] In [*Fopalan v. Fopalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68588), a gainfully employed husband who left all household expenses solely to his wife failed his mutual duty under Articles 68 to 71.[^75] Conversely, in [*Republic v. Yeban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68228), the Supreme Court affirmed nullity where a wife went to work in a Dubai bank with her husband's consent. She sent little money for their children despite her earnings and told him she had no plans of returning.[^76]

### Civil Support Remedies Versus R.A. No. 9262 Criminal Liability

Mere failure or inability to provide spousal support gives rise to civil remedies under the Civil Code and Family Code rather than automatic criminal liability under Republic Act No. 9262.[^77] Sitting En Banc in [*Acharon v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68085), the Supreme Court acquitted a husband whose remittances stopped after an accident and fire while working abroad, reversing both lower courts.[^78] Construing the mutual duty of support under the Family Code without citing a specific article number, the Supreme Court emphasized that both spouses owe financial support in keeping with family capacity, so a husband's non-intentional inability cannot be criminalized under Section 5(i) or Section 5(e) without proof of willful denial intended to inflict anguish or control movement.[^79] Instead, an aggrieved spouse may file a civil action for support.[^80]

Applying [*Acharon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68085) in [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68838) (`G.R. No. 255877`), a seafarer accused under Section 5(i) expressly invoked Article 68 to argue that both spouses must support each other.[^81] Reversing the trial court and the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court acquitted him. The spouses had no children or common home. The wife ran a store and worked as a massage therapist. She filed the complaint without first seeking support. He had stopped remitting money to pay for his parents' medical expenses.[^82] It reiterated that spousal support under the Family Code is mutual and "not a one-way street for the husband to support his wife".[^83]

## Interplay with Article 36 and Post-Tan-Andal Standards

### Defining Essential Obligations and Current Evidentiary Rules

From the inception of Article 36 jurisprudence, Article 68 has defined the core duties a psychologically incapacitated spouse cannot comprehend or perform.[^84] In [*Santos v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/jan1995/gr_112019_1995.html), the En Banc Supreme Court construed Article 36 alongside Article 68, distinguishing Articles 46 and 55 while dismissing a husband's petition on failure of proof after his wife stayed abroad.[^85] In [*Republic v. Court of Appeals and Molina*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34463), Guideline 6 identified Articles 68 to 71 (between spouses) and Articles 220, 221 and 225 (between parents and children) as the essential marital obligations that must be alleged and proven under Article 36.[^86]

The En Banc landmark [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) fundamentally revised the evidentiary rules governing how breach of Articles 68 to 71 is proved under Article 36.[^87] Reversing the Court of Appeals and reinstating the trial court's decree, the Supreme Court abandoned [*Molina*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34463)'s second guideline requiring medical identification of a clinical root cause and expert testimony.[^88] It recast psychological incapacity as a legal concept proved through clear and convincing evidence of durable personality structures and modified incurability into legal incurability relative to a specific partner.[^89] Earlier rulings decided before [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), including [*Santos v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/jan1995/gr_112019_1995.html), [*Republic v. Court of Appeals and Molina*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34463), [*Antonio v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40916), [*Azcueta v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49232) and [*Calma v. Santos-Calma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66925), applied those former medical or preponderance criteria.[^90] [^91] [^92] [^93] [^94] Under governing law, [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) retains [*Molina*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34463) Guideline 6 by reproducing Articles 68 to 71 verbatim and further holds under Article 147 that a spouse who failed to care for and maintain the family and household acquires no co-ownership share in property built through the other spouse's sole efforts.[^95]

### Pleading Sufficiency and Failure-of-Proof Boundaries

While allegations matching Article 68 duties suffice to sustain a nullity complaint against a Rule 16 motion to dismiss for pre-March 15, 2003 petitions as held in [*Aurelio v. Aurelio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36434) (noting that A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC bars motions to dismiss for subsequent complaints), mere proof of marital conflict or even mental illness does not establish Article 36 nullity.[^96] [^97] In [*Datu v. Datu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67869), the Supreme Court explained after [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) that schizophrenia does not automatically render a spouse unable to comply with Articles 68 to 71, although clear and convincing proof of enduring delusions causing abandonment, non-support and polygamous beliefs established incapacity.[^98] Conversely, in [*Clavecilla v. Clavecilla*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68752), the Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of a nullity suit where the husband's complaint that his wife neglected family income was refuted by her overseas employment records while her other Articles 68 to 71 duties went unchallenged. His own psychological report also lacked a consistent pattern of failing to be present, loving, faithful, respectful and supportive.[^99]

## Case Discussions

### [*Santos v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/jan1995/gr_112019_1995.html): Psychological Incapacity and Article 68 Covenants

First Lieutenant Leouel Santos married Julia Bedia in 1986, after which they lived with her parents until she left to work as a nurse in the United States in 1988. Leouel alleged that she never returned or communicated with him for more than five years.[^100] Julia opposed the complaint and then manifested that she would neither appear nor submit evidence. Both the trial court and the Court of Appeals dismissed his Article 36 complaint for lack of merit.[^101] The En Banc Supreme Court affirmed dismissal, defining psychological incapacity in conjunction with Article 68 covenants while ruling that his evidence of Julia's prolonged absence and silence did not show psychological incapacity (with medical criteria later revised in [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507)).[^102] [^103] The case shows that Article 68 supplies the core marital duties referenced in Article 36 without turning abandonment alone into automatic nullity.[^104]

### [*Republic v. Court of Appeals and Molina*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34463): Guideline 6 and Essential Obligations

Roridel Molina petitioned for nullity alleging her husband Reynaldo was immature, relied on his parents and abandoned the family.[^105] The trial court and the Court of Appeals declared the marriage void, prompting the Solicitor General to appeal.[^106] Reversing both lower courts, the En Banc Supreme Court held that irreconcilable differences did not prove incapacity and issued eight guidelines where Guideline 6 identified Articles 68 to 71 and 220, 221 and 225 as essential marital obligations (with Guideline 2 and medical incurability later abandoned or modified in [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507)).[^107] [^108] The case shows that Title III duties under Articles 68 to 71 form the statutory benchmark of essential spousal obligations.[^109]

### [*Cojuangco, Jr. v. Palma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43593): Disbarment for a Second Marriage

On June 22, 1982, Atty. Leo J. Palma married the 22-year-old daughter of Eduardo Cojuangco, Jr. despite his subsisting marriage. Cojuangco filed a disbarment complaint on November 8, 1982.[^110] After the Solicitor General investigated, the Supreme Court disbarred Palma on September 15, 2004 and later denied his motion to vacate.[^111] Rejecting Palma's defense that he provided comfortably for his children, the Supreme Court stressed that as a husband he remained obliged to live with his wife, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity and render help and support until death.[^112] The case shows that discharging parental financial support never excuses breaching spousal fidelity and cohabitation duties.[^113]

### [*Guevarra v. Atty. Eala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44146): Disbarment for an Adulterous Affair

Joselano Guevarra filed a disbarment complaint in 2002 against married lawyer Atty. Jose Emmanuel Eala for carrying on an adulterous affair and fathering a child with Guevarra's wife, Irene Moje.[^114] After the Integrated Bar of the Philippines Board of Governors passed Resolution No. XVII-2006-06, the Supreme Court reviewed the record and annulled it.[^115] Quoting the constitutional protection of marriage and the Family Code duties to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity and render help and support, the En Banc Supreme Court disbarred Eala even though the marriage of Guevarra and Moje was later declared void.[^116] The case shows that subsequent annulment does not validate extramarital conduct committed while a marriage subsists.[^117]

### [*Limbona v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45452) (`G.R. No. 181097`): Marital Residence Presumption

Mayoralty candidate Malik Alingan sought to disqualify Norlainie Limbona in Pantar, Lanao del Norte for failing the one-year residency requirement.[^118] The Commission on Elections disqualified Limbona after finding she failed to corroborate residence in Pantar and only acquired her husband's Pantar domicile on November 11, 2006.[^119] Dismissing her petition for certiorari, the En Banc Supreme Court affirmed the disqualification and the reliance on Articles 68 and 69 to presume spouses share one legal residence absent proof of a separate abode.[^120] The case shows how Article 68 cohabitation shapes domicile analysis in election disputes.[^121]

### [*Limbona v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50224) (`G.R. No. 186006`): Substitute Candidacy and Prior Ruling

After the Commission on Elections disqualified her husband, Norlainie Limbona filed a certificate of candidacy as his substitute on July 21, 2007. The Commission on Elections Second Division disqualified her on November 23, 2007.[^122] The En Banc Commission on Elections affirmed on January 14, 2009 and cited the Supreme Court's earlier ruling.[^123] The En Banc Supreme Court dismissed her petition for certiorari, held itself bound by its earlier ruling and again reproduced Articles 68 and 69 to confirm her marital domicile followed her husband's domicile absent proof of a separate residence.[^124] The case shows settled reliance on Articles 68 and 69 in tandem when proving marital residence.[^125]

### [*Social Security Commission v. Favila*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37424): Dependency for Survivor Benefits

Teresa Favila claimed survivor pension benefits from the Social Security System after her husband Florante died following roughly 17 years of *de facto* separation.[^126] The Social Security Commission denied her claim, but the Court of Appeals reversed.[^127] Reversing the appellate court, the Supreme Court found untenable the wife's claim of presumed dependency, which she drew from the *Ceneta* ruling invoking Article 68. She had to prove actual dependency under Republic Act No. 1161.[^128] The case shows that a legal wife separated *de facto* must prove dependency under social security law and cannot rest on a presumption from the marriage.[^129]

### [*Aurelio v. Aurelio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36434): Pleading Sufficiency

Vida Aurelio filed an Article 36 nullity complaint in 2002 alleging failure of support, hostility, public humiliation and aversion to cohabit.[^130] The Regional Trial Court denied husband Danilo's motion to dismiss and the Court of Appeals dismissed his certiorari petition.[^131] Affirming the appellate court, the Supreme Court held that the complaint's allegations fell under Article 68 and stated a cause of action requiring trial, while noting post-March 15, 2003 petitions bar motions to dismiss under A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC.[^132] The case shows that factual averments breaching Article 68 duties satisfy pleading sufficiency for nullity trials.[^133]

### [*Fujiki v. Marinay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55916): Standing of a Prior Spouse

Japanese national Minoru Fujiki petitioned the Regional Trial Court to recognize a Japanese decree voiding his Filipino wife's subsequent bigamous marriage and cancel the Philippine civil registry entry under Rule 108.[^134] The trial court dismissed the petition for improper venue and lack of standing.[^135] Reversing and ordering reinstatement, the Supreme Court held that a prior spouse's real interest extends beyond property and support rights to relational rights recognized under Title III of the Family Code.[^136] The case shows that Title III marital rights confer standing upon a prior spouse to protect the integrity of an existing marriage.[^137]

### [*People v. Jumawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56922): Marital Rape

Edgar Jumawan was convicted of two counts of marital rape against his wife by the trial court and the Court of Appeals.[^138] On automatic review, he argued that marriage precluded rape prosecution by a wife.[^139] Affirming his convictions, the Supreme Court held that the Family Code obligation of spouses to love one another sanctions only spontaneous and mutual affection and intimacy rather than forced coitus, leaving nullity under Article 36 as the lawful remedy for refusal. It sentenced him to reclusion perpetua for each count.[^140] The case shows that Article 68 duties protect spousal autonomy and never shield marital rape.[^141]

### [*Calma v. Santos-Calma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66925): Failure to Keep a Common Home

While Jeffrey Calma worked abroad under a three-year contract, his wife Mari Kris could not settle in one home and kept asking for money. She left him for another partner and abandoned their son to him.[^142] Both the trial court and the Court of Appeals dismissed his Article 36 petition for lack of gravity, antecedence and incurability.[^143] In a 2020 decision before [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the Supreme Court reversed both courts and declared the marriage void. It held that she was incapable of the covenants that Article 68 states and that she disregarded her duties under Articles 70 and 220.[^144] The case illustrates refusal to maintain a common home and fidelity, though [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) later revised [*Molina*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34463)'s expert-weight rules.[^145]

### [*Datu v. Datu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67869): Schizophrenia and Essential Obligations

Discharged from military service due to schizophrenia, Alfredo Datu sought Article 36 nullity because paranoid delusions drove him to leave his wife, stop working and claim divine permission to take multiple wives.[^146] Both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals declared the marriage void.[^147] Denying his wife's petition under [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the Supreme Court clarified that psychosis such as schizophrenia does not automatically create incapacity under Articles 68 to 71, yet sustained nullity because enduring psychosis disabled cohabitation, love, respect, fidelity and support.[^148] The case shows that medical illness alone does not breach Article 68 unless enduring dysfunction prevents fulfilling marital duties.[^149]

### [*Halog v. Halog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68037): Abuse, Infidelity and Abandonment

Ma. Virginia Halog sued for nullity after her husband Wilbur repeatedly engaged in affairs, threatened her with a gun, ceased financial support and married another woman in Doha, Qatar.[^150] The trial court granted nullity, but the Court of Appeals reversed because the psychiatrist did not personally examine Wilbur.[^151] Reinstating the trial court's decree under [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the Supreme Court admitted collateral psychiatric evidence and held Wilbur's abuse and abandonment prevented compliance with Article 68.[^152] The case shows that combined abuse, infidelity and abandonment breach Article 68's core duties.[^153]

### [*Republic v. Yeban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68228): Overseas Spouse and Support

Bryan Yeban petitioned for nullity after his wife Maria Fe went to work in a Dubai bank with his consent and support. She sent little money for their children and told him she had no plans of returning.[^154] The trial court dismissed the petition, while the Court of Appeals reversed and voided the marriage.[^155] Denying the Republic's appeal, the Supreme Court held that Fe failed Articles 68 to 71 essential duties and noted under [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) that personal examination of the absent spouse is unnecessary.[^156] The case shows that mutual support and communication under Articles 68 to 71 bind an overseas earning spouse equally.[^157]

### [*Egmalis-Ke-eg v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68461): Unemployment and Domicile

After a tribal wedding, Ireneo never fixed a family domicile, stayed chronically unemployed and drank habitually while Aida worked overseas to support their son.[^158] The Regional Trial Court voided the marriage on both spouses' incapacity. However, the Court of Appeals reversed.[^159] The Supreme Court voided the marriage for Ireneo's incapacity under Articles 68 and 69, while rejecting the trial court's unpleaded finding against Aida because she worked responsibly to sustain the family.[^160] The case shows courts distinguish the spouse who abandons domicile and support from the spouse who shoulders them.[^161]

### [*Dedicatoria v. Dedicatoria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68584): Overdependence on a Parent

Ferdinand Dedicatoria stayed dependent on his mother, remained jobless, refused to live independently with his wife and child and later cohabited with another woman.[^162] The trial court granted nullity citing Article 68, but the Court of Appeals reversed for lack of independent proof before marriage.[^163] Reinstating nullity under [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the Supreme Court credited collateral testimony from Ferdinand's sister and approved the finding that his conduct breached mutual love, respect, support and fidelity.[^164] The case shows how filial overdependence and abandonment defeat Article 68 partnership.[^165]

### [*Fopalan v. Fopalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68588): Insolence and Withheld Support

Zeth Fopalan petitioned for nullity because her husband Neil publicly insulted her, stayed indifferent to their autistic son, pursued affairs and refused to share family expenses despite employment.[^166] The trial court granted nullity then reversed on reconsideration. The Court of Appeals affirmed dismissal.[^167] Reinstating nullity under [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the Supreme Court held Neil failed fundamental duties under Articles 68 to 71 and 220 to 221.[^168] The case shows that deliberate financial withholding despite employment and domestic hostility reflect incapacity under Article 68.[^169]

### [*Clavecilla v. Clavecilla*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68752): A Disproved Grievance

Fernando Clavecilla sought nullity blaming his wife Marivic for lacking interest in earning income and alternatively alleging his own narcissistic disorder.[^170] The trial court granted nullity, while the Court of Appeals reversed.[^171] Affirming the dismissal under [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the Supreme Court held that Marivic's overseas employment records refuted the solitary financial complaint and that Fernando's psychological report showed no consistent failure across Articles 68 to 71 duties.[^172] The case shows that an isolated, disproved grievance does not satisfy Article 36.[^173]

### [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68838) (`G.R. No. 255877`): Mutual Support and Acquittal

Seafarer XXX stopped remitting money after his parents fell ill and he paid their medical expenses. His childless wife lived with her parents and supported herself. After about 13 years of silence she filed a Section 5(i) R.A. No. 9262 complaint without first seeking support.[^174] Both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals convicted him.[^175] Acquitting him under [*Acharon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68085), the Supreme Court noted his invocation of Article 68 and held spousal support under the Family Code is mutual and not a one-way burden on the husband.[^176] The case shows how reciprocal spousal duties defeat criminal prosecution when willful denial and need are absent.[^177]

### [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69419) (`G.R. No. 263449`): Sudden Abandonment

Caught kissing their househelper, husband XXX abruptly abandoned the conjugal home, leaving his wife in distress with unpaid business debts.[^178] Both the trial court and the Court of Appeals convicted him under Section 5(i) of R.A. No. 9262.[^179] Modifying only the sentence, the Supreme Court applied Article 68 to hold sudden abandonment constitutes psychological violence because cohabitation, love, respect and support cannot be rendered after desertion.[^180] The case shows Article 68 informs psychological abuse analysis when sudden abandonment inflicts emotional anguish.[^181]

### [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69576) (`G.R. No. 252739`): Infidelity and Presumed Intent

Husband XXX maintained a mistress and fathered a four-year-old child while staying with his wife on weekends.[^182] The trial court and the Court of Appeals convicted him of psychological violence under Section 5(i) of R.A. No. 9262.[^183] Affirming conviction, the En Banc Supreme Court cited Article 68 in relation to Article 55 to rule that marital infidelity is not innocent in itself, raising a presumption of intent when emotional distress ensues outside estranged or consenting setups.[^184] The case shows Article 68 anchors fidelity across civil and protective criminal laws.[^185]

## Suggested Answers

### 1. Compelling Cohabitation or Sexual Cooperation Under Article 68

**Question.** After living separately for years, a wife petitions the court for a writ of *habeas corpus* and a mandatory order compelling her husband to return to their conjugal dwelling and resume cohabitation under Article 68 of the Family Code. Meanwhile, a husband in another marriage insists that Article 68 entitles him to demand sexual intercourse from his reluctant wife whenever he chooses. How should a court resolve both claims?

**Answer.** Both claims fail. Under [*Ilusorio v. Ilusorio-Bildner*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52421) and [*Chi Ming Tsoi v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34416), while Article 68 obliges spouses to live together and observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, courts cannot compel marital consortium through a *habeas corpus* writ or judicial order because the sanction is spontaneous mutual affection.[^222] [^223] Likewise, under [*People v. Jumawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56922), the Family Code duty to love one another sanctions only spontaneous and mutual affection and sexual intimacy rather than unilateral coercion, so forcing a spouse into non-consensual intercourse constitutes marital rape under Republic Act No. 8353.[^224] Where a spouse senselessly and persistently refuses sexual cooperation, the proper legal recourse is a petition for declaration of nullity under Article 36.[^225] [^226]

### 2. Mutual Support Under Article 68, VAWC Prosecutions and Social Security Claims

**Question.** A seafarer stops sending monthly remittances after his employment contract ends and his parents fall ill. His employed, childless wife never demands support for over a decade while residing with her parents, then files a criminal complaint under Section 5(i) of Republic Act No. 9262 invoking the husband's duty of support. Separately, a surviving wife separated *de facto* for 17 years claims Social Security System survivor death benefits arguing that Article 68 makes a legal spouse presumed dependent for support. Resolve both claims.

**Answer.** Neither the criminal conviction nor the automatic death-benefit claim succeeds. Under [*Acharon v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68085) and [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68838) (`G.R. No. 255877`), spousal support under the Family Code is a mutual obligation shared according to capacity rather than a one-way street imposed solely on the husband.[^227] [^228] Mere failure or inability to support creates civil remedies under the Civil Code and Family Code rather than criminal liability under Section 5(i) or Section 5(e) absent proof of willful denial intended to inflict mental anguish or restrict agency.[^229] [^230] As to the survivor claim, [*Social Security Commission v. Favila*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37424) rejects the presumed dependency that the wife drew from Article 68 through *Ceneta*, requiring a spouse separated *de facto* to prove actual dependency on the deceased member for support at the time of death under Republic Act No. 1161.[^231]

### 3. Infidelity, Abandonment and Nullity Under [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507)

**Question.** Over a multi-year marriage, a husband repeatedly commits extramarital affairs, views giving a household allowance as compensating an unemployed spouse and blames his wife's angry reactions for driving him out. Meanwhile, a separate criminal prosecution under Section 5(i) of Republic Act No. 9262 charges another husband who secretly sired a child with a mistress, prompting him to argue that he never intended to cause emotional distress. How do Article 68 and Article 36 apply to each dispute?

**Answer.** Both rulings apply Article 68 as an enforceable benchmark of fidelity. Under [*Quiogue v. Quiogue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68704), Article 68 recognizes fidelity as the norm and no spouse need settle for less than absolute faithfulness.[^232] When chronic infidelity stems from an antecedent, legally incurable personality structure shown by clear and convincing evidence under [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the marriage is void for the unfaithful spouse's psychological incapacity, whereas the other spouse's retaliatory outbursts during marriage do not constitute incapacity.[^233] [^234] In the criminal case, [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69576) (`G.R. No. 252739`) holds that marital infidelity violates Article 68 in relation to Article 55 and is not innocent in itself, so intent to cause anguish under Section 5(i) of Republic Act No. 9262 is presumed once distress results, save in estranged or consciously consenting arrangements.[^235]

# Quotes

## On Consortium and Spontaneous Mutual Affection

> "The law provides that the husband and the wife are obliged to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity. The sanction therefor is the "spontaneous, mutual affection between husband and wife and not any legal mandate or court order" to enforce consortium."[^236]

> "It is true that the Family Code, obligates the spouses to love one another but this rule sanctions affection and sexual intimacy, as expressions of love, that are both spontaneous and mutual and not the kind which is unilaterally exacted by force or coercion."[^237]

## On Domicile and Statutory Dependency Limits

> "Considering that petitioner failed to show that she maintained a separate residence from her husband, and as there is no evidence to prove otherwise, reliance on these provisions of the Family Code is proper and is in consonance with human experience."[^238]

> "This notwithstanding, we still find untenable Teresa's assertion that being the legal wife, she is presumed dependent upon Florante for support. In Re: Application for Survivor's Benefits of Manlavi, this Court defined "dependent" as "one who derives his or her main support from another [or] relying on, or subject to, someone else for support; not able to exist or sustain oneself, or to perform anything without the will, power or aid of someone else." Although therein, the wife's marriage to the deceased husband was not dissolved prior to the latter's death, the Court denied the wife's claim for survivorship benefits from the Government Service Insurance System (GSIS) because the wife abandoned her family to live with other men for more than 17 years until her husband died. Her whereabouts was unknown to her family and she never attempted to communicate with them or even check up on the well-being of her daughter with the deceased. From these, the Court concluded that the wife during said period was not dependent on her husband for any support, financial or otherwise, hence, she is not a dependent within the contemplation of RA 8291 as to be entitled to survivorship benefits. It is worthy to note that under Section 2(f) RA 8291, a legitimate spouse dependent for support is likewise included in the enumeration of dependents and under Section 2(g), the legal dependent spouse in the enumeration of primary beneficiaries."[^239]

## On Fidelity, Abandonment and Relational Rights

> "Under Article 68 of the Family Code, the "husband and wife are obliged to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, and render mutual help and support." Clearly, the law itself recognizes fidelity as the norm and a spouse should not be made to settle for anything less than absolute faithfulness from the other. This stems from the nature of marriage being a "special contract" of an exclusive partnership between a man and a woman. While it is true that infidelity is a ground for legal separation, the same may also be an indication of a psychological incapacity if, for the same reason, one is completely unable to discharge the essential obligations of marriage."[^240]

> "There cannot be any serious debate that the act of "causing mental or emotional anguish, public ridicule or humiliation to the woman or her child," through "repeated verbal and emotional abuse" and marital infidelity, among others, is not innocent in itself. Marital infidelity is even violative of the obligations between a husband and wife under Article 68, in relation to Article 55, of the Family Code. Indeed, as early as 1948, the Court has recognized that damages may be recovered for mental and psychological suffering. such act that causes mental or emotional anguish is a form of violence upon persons."[^241]

> "A perusal of Section 3(c) of Republic Act No. 9262 would reveal that it does not contain an exclusive list of what constitutes psychological violence as shown by the phrase "such as but not limited to." Instead, it defines psychological violence as any act or omission causing mental or emotional suffering to the victim. To recapitulate, Section 5(i) punishes the act of causing mental or emotional anguish to the woman, including emotional abuse. Undoubtedly, a husband's abandonment of his wife falls under psychological violence and emotional abuse penalized under Republic Act No. 9262, as such an action would naturally cause mental and emotional suffering to the wife, a person whom the husband is obliged to cohabit with, love, respect, and give support to. Indeed, under Article 68 of the Family Code "the husband and wife are obliged to live together, observe mutual love, respect, and fidelity, and render mutual help and support." These obligations could not be fulfilled should the husband abandon the wife. In practice, these obligations need not even be dictated by law, as these naturally arise as part of human nature between two individuals sharing their love with each other. Sudden abandonment without any explanation would certainly cause emotional anguish."[^242]

> "Property rights are already substantive rights protected by the Constitution, but a spouse’s right in a marriage extends further to relational rights recognized under Title III (“Rights and Obligations between Husband and Wife”) of the Family Code. A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC cannot “diminish, increase, or modify” the substantive right of the spouse to maintain the integrity of his marriage. In any case, Section 2(a) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC preserves this substantive right by limiting the personality to sue to the husband or the wife of the union recognized by law."[^243]

## On Mutual Support and Equal Partnership

> "At this point, the Court is not unmindful of the sometimes peculiar predicament it finds itself in those instances when it is tasked to interpret static statutes formulated in a particular point in time and apply them to situations and people in a society in flux. With respect to the concept of psychological incapacity, courts must take into account not only developments in science and medicine but also changing social and cultural mores, including the blurring of traditional gender roles. In this day and age, women have taken on increasingly important roles in the financial and material support of their families. This, however, does not change the ideal that the family should be an "autonomous" social institution, wherein the spouses cooperate and are equally responsible for the support and well-being of the family. In the case at bar, the spouses from the outset failed to form themselves into a family, a cohesive unit based on mutual love, respect and support, due to the failure of one to perform the essential duties of marriage."[^244]

> "To be sure, under the Family Code, the obligation to support is imposed mutually upon the spouses. In other words, both the husband and the wife have the obligation to give support to each other. However, even as the law imposes the obligation to support mutually upon the spouses, the failure of the wife to financially support the husband only results in civil liability, whereas if it is the husband who fails to provide financial support to the wife, this will result not only in civil liability, but also criminal liability under Section 5(i) of R.A. 9262. Surely, this cannot be the case, as the law recognizes no substantial distinction between the husband and the wife as regards their responsibility to provide financial support to each other and the family."[^245]

> "Lastly, it bears stressing that the obligation to provide support is imposed by the law mutually upon both spouses. The obligation is not a one-way street for the husband to support his wife. The wife has the identical obligation to provide support to her husband. The law certainly did not intend to impose a heavier burden on the husband to provide support for his wife, or institutionalize criminal prosecution as a measure to enforce support from him."[^246]

## On Coercive Control and Legal Incapacity

> "Article 68 of the Family Code obligates the husband and wife "to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, and render mutual help and support." In this case, petitioner and respondent may have lived together, but the facts narrated by petitioner show that respondent failed to, or could not, comply with the obligations expected of him as a husband. He was even apathetic that petitioner filed a petition for declaration of nullity of their marriage."[^247]

> "Reading together the deliberations of the Code Committee and our rulings in Santos and Molina, we hold that the psychological incapacity contemplated in Article 36 of the Family Code is incurable, not in the medical, but in the legal sense; hence, the third Molina guideline is amended accordingly. This means that the incapacity is so enduring and persistent with respect to a specific partner, and contemplates a situation where the couple's respective personality structures are so incompatible and antagonistic that the only result of the union would be the inevitable and irreparable breakdown of the marriage. "[A]n undeniable pattern of such persisting failure [to be a present, loving, faithful, respectful, and supportive spouse] must be established so as to demonstrate that there is indeed a psychological anomaly or incongruity in the spouse relative to the other.""[^248]

> "As expressed in Article 68 of the Family Code, the marital covenants include the mutual obligations of husband and wife to live together, observe love, respect, and fidelity and to help and support each other."[^249]

> "It is quite apparent that Nellie's psychological incapacity, as shown by the behavioral manifestations of her personality disorder that is rooted in her childhood and carried over into her adulthood and married life, is grave and legally incurable. The actions of Nellie demonstrate an undeniable pattern of failure on her part to comply with her essential marital obligations to Willy. Thus, the CA correctly upheld the RTC Decision which declared the parties' marriage void on the ground of Nellie's psychological incapacity."[^250]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Focus: No Court Compulsion of Cohabitation or Intimacy.** Neither a *habeas corpus* petition nor a marital duty of love authorizes a court order forcing spouses to live together or submit to non-consensual sexual intercourse ([*Ilusorio v. Ilusorio-Bildner*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52421) and [*People v. Jumawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56922)). Distinguish judicial non-interference in consortium from Article 36 nullity where prolonged senseless refusal of sexual cooperation or coercive control proves psychological incapacity ([*Chi Ming Tsoi v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34416) and [*Tani-De La Fuente v. De La Fuente, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62960)).

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Focus: Separate Civil Support From VAWC and SSS Dependency.** Do not treat failure or inability to send spousal support as an automatic crime under Republic Act No. 9262 ([*Acharon v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68085) and [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68838)). Check whether the husband willfully denied support to inflict anguish or restrict movement. For Social Security System claims by a separated spouse, verify actual proof of dependency at death rather than presuming it from the marriage ([*Social Security Commission v. Favila*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37424)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Applying Post-Tan-Andal Nullity Standards Carefully.** Under [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), plaintiffs no longer need medical identification of a clinical disorder or expert testimony to prove incapacity to fulfill Articles 68 to 71. Use clear and convincing evidence of an antecedent, legally incurable personality structure while recalling that isolated complaints or reactions to a spouse's infidelity do not suffice ([*Clavecilla v. Clavecilla*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68752) and [*Quiogue v. Quiogue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68704)). Also remember that abandonment or infidelity violates Article 68 and can trigger Section 5(i) liability under Republic Act No. 9262 ([*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69419) and [*XXX v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69576)).
