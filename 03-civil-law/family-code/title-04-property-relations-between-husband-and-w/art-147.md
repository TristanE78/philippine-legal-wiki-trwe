> Art. 147. When a man and a woman who are capacitated to marry each other, live exclusively with each other as husband and wife without the benefit of marriage or under a void marriage, their wages and salaries shall be owned by them in equal shares and the property acquired by both of them through their work or industry shall be governed by the rules on co-ownership.
>
> In the absence of proof to the contrary, properties acquired while they lived together shall be presumed to have been obtained by their joint efforts, work or industry, and shall be owned by them in equal shares. For purposes of this Article, a party who did not participate in the acquisition by the other party of any property shall be deemed to have contributed jointly in the acquisition thereof if the former’s efforts consisted in the care and maintenance of the family and of the household.
>
> Neither party can encumber or dispose by acts inter vivos of his or her share in the property acquired during cohabitation and owned in common, without the consent of the other, until after the termination of their cohabitation.
>
> When only one of the parties to a void marriage is in good faith, the share of the party in bad faith in the co-ownership shall be forfeited in favor of their common children. In case of default of or waiver by any or all of the common children or their descendants, each vacant share shall belong to the respective surviving descendants. In the absence of descendants, such share shall belong to the innocent party. In all cases, the forfeiture shall take place upon termination of the cohabitation. (144a)[^family-code]

# Explanation

Article 147 of the Family Code establishes the special co-ownership property regime that governs the property relations of a man and a woman who are legally capacitated to marry each other and who live exclusively with each other as husband and wife either without the benefit of marriage or under a marriage that is void from the beginning.[^family-code] [^2] In a void marriage, regardless of the cause of nullity, the property relations of the parties during the period of cohabitation are governed not by the regimes of absolute community of property or conjugal partnership of gains, which presuppose a valid or at least voidable marriage, but by the provisions of Article 147 or [Article 148](art-148.md) of the Family Code, as the case may be.[^2] [^3] [^4]

## 1. Legislative Genesis, Statutory Structure and Retroactive Application

### A. Evolution from Article 144 of the 1950 Civil Code

Article 147 of the Family Code is a legislative remake of [Article 144](../../civil-code/book-1-title-06-property-relations-between/art-0144.md) of the 1950 Civil Code as interpreted and applied in prior jurisprudence.[^2] [^5] Under Article 144 of the 1950 Civil Code, when a man and a woman lived together as husband and wife, but they were not married or their marriage was void from the beginning, the property acquired by either or both of them through their work or industry or their wages and salaries was governed by the rules on co-ownership.[^6] [^7] Although Article 144 of the Civil Code did not expressly state that the parties had to be capacitated to marry each other, the Supreme Court construed it to apply only to a relationship between a man and a woman who were not incapacitated to marry each other, or whose marriage was void from the beginning. It did not apply to a cohabitation amounting to adultery or concubinage, because it would be absurd to create a co-ownership where a prior conjugal partnership or absolute community already exists between the man and his lawful wife.[^7] [^8] *Malang v. Moson* adds that the rulings in *Juaniza v. Jose*, as cited in *Malang*, are embodied in the second paragraph of Article 148.[^6]

When the Family Code took effect on August 3, 1988, the Family Code split the regulation of non-marital and void-marriage property relations into two distinct provisions: Article 147 for unions where the man and woman are capacitated to marry each other and live exclusively together and [Article 148](art-148.md) to fill the statutory hiatus left by Article 144 of the Civil Code regarding adulterous, bigamous or incapacitated unions.[^6] [^9] [^10]

In recodifying Article 144 of the Civil Code into Article 147 of the Family Code, the Family Code introduced three refinements:

1. **Exclusive Cohabitation:** Whereas Article 144 of the Civil Code required only that the parties live together as husband and wife, the first paragraph of Article 147 of the Family Code explicitly added the requirement that they live *exclusively* with each other as husband and wife.[^6]
2. **Express Prohibition on Unilateral *Inter Vivos* Disposition During Cohabitation:** The third paragraph of Article 147 expressly prohibits either party from encumbering or disposing by acts *inter vivos* of his or her share in the property acquired during cohabitation and owned in common, without the consent of the other, until after the termination of their cohabitation.[^2] [^11]
3. **Forfeiture for Bad Faith in Void Marriages:** The fourth paragraph of Article 147 provides that when only one of the parties to a void marriage is in good faith, the share of the party in bad faith in the co-ownership shall be forfeited upon termination of the cohabitation in favor of their common children, surviving descendants or the innocent party.[^2]

At the constitutional and social-legislation level, the Supreme Court En Banc recognized in [*Dolera v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69261) (2023) that common-law relationships are acknowledged and protected under Philippine law, noting that the Family Code governs the property relations of couples living together as husband and wife without the benefit of marriage or under a void marriage through the co-ownership regimes of Articles 147 and 148.[^12] On that basis, [*Dolera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69261) struck down as unconstitutional a statutory proviso in the Social Security Act that disqualified a surviving spouse from receiving survivorship pension solely because she was a common-law partner at the time of the pensioner's disability prior to their subsequent valid marriage.[^12]

### B. Retroactive Application Under Article 256 and Vested Rights

Pursuant to [Article 256](../title-12-final-provisions/art-256.md) of the Family Code, the Code has retroactive effect insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights in accordance with the Civil Code or other laws.[^5] [^8] Because Article 147 is a remake of Article 144 of the 1950 Civil Code, the Supreme Court held in [*Paterno v. Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084) (2020) that Article 147 applies to a marriage celebrated before the Family Code took effect, so long as no vested or acquired rights are impaired.[^5] Similarly, in [*Uy v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/may1994/gr_102726_1994.html) (1994), where a 36-year common-law cohabitation ended upon the male partner's death on September 27, 1990 (after the Family Code took effect on August 3, 1988), the Supreme Court ruled that the judicial partition of properties acquired during the common-law union was governed by Articles 147 and 148 of the Family Code.[^13]

Conversely, in [*Belcodero v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/oct1993/gr_89667_1993.html) (1993), where a married man deserted his lawful wife in 1946, purchased a parcel of land on installment in 1949 that was titled in his paramour's name in 1959 and died in 1967, the Supreme Court held that Articles 147 and 148 of the Family Code could not be applied retroactively under Article 256 to interdict or prejudice the prior vested successional and conjugal rights that had already been acquired by his legal wife and legitimate children upon his death in 1967.[^8]

## 2. Requisites and Scope of Application

For the special co-ownership regime under Article 147 to arise, three cumulative requisites must concur:

1. **The man and the woman must be capacitated to marry each other**,
2. **They must live exclusively with each other as husband and wife** and
3. **Their union must be without the benefit of marriage, or their marriage must be void**.[^14] [^15] [^16] [^17] [^18]

### A. First Requisite: Legal Capacity to Marry Each Other

In [*Valdes v. Regional Trial Court, Branch 102, Quezon City*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980) (1996) and [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) (2021), the Supreme Court defined the term *capacitated* in the first paragraph of Article 147 as referring to the legal capacity of a party to contract marriage under [Article 5](../title-01-marriage/art-005.md) of the Family Code, namely any male or female of the age of eighteen years or upwards who is not under any of the legal impediments mentioned in [Article 37](../title-01-marriage/art-037.md) (incestuous marriages) and [Article 38](../title-01-marriage/art-038.md) (marriages void by reason of public policy) of the Family Code and who is not bound by a prior subsisting valid marriage.[^2] [^19]

Consequently, Article 147 applies to unions where the parties are at least eighteen years of age and free from any diriment impediment of incest, public-policy relationship or prior undissolved marriage, even though their marriage is void *ab initio* on grounds such as:

* **Psychological incapacity under [Article 36](../title-01-marriage/art-036.md)** of the Family Code, which is the most frequent judicial context for the application of Article 147 (*Valdes v. Regional Trial Court*, *Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr*, *Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*, *Gonzales v. Gonzales*, *Sales v. Sales*, *Diño v. Diño*, *Salas, Jr. v. Aguila*, *Barrido v. Nonato*, *Ocampo v. Ocampo*, *Paterno v. Paterno*, *Perez, Jr. v. Perez-Senerpida*, *Tan-Andal v. Andal*, *Tanyag v. Tanyag*, *Alberto v. Alberto*, *Tee Ten v. Tee Ten* and *Soto v. Reyes-Soto*)[^2] [^14] [^4] [^20] [^21] [^15] [^22] [^16] [^17] [^5] [^11] [^19] [^23] [^24] [^25] [^18]
* **Absence of a valid marriage license** under Article 80(3) of the 1950 Civil Code or [Article 35(3)](../title-01-marriage/art-035.md) of the Family Code, provided neither party had a subsisting prior marriage at the time of celebration (*Cariño v. Cariño* and *Diaz-Salgado v. Anson*)[^26] [^27] or
* **Purely non-marital cohabitation** where no marriage ceremony was ever completed, such as in [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519) (2006), where the couple lived together without marriage from 1986 to 1995 and [*Uy v. Spouses Lacsamana*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61014) (2015), where the parties' wedding ceremony was not consummated because of bombings on the day of the ceremony and the couple could not secure a marriage contract, leaving them to cohabit as unmarried partners.[^28] [^29]

By contrast, if either party is below eighteen years of age or is bound by a prior subsisting marriage during the cohabitation, the parties are **not** capacitated to marry each other and their property relations fall under [Article 148](art-148.md) rather than Article 147.[^9] [^26] [^30] [^31] [^32] [^33] [^34] [^35] [^10]

### B. Second Requisite: Exclusive Cohabitation as Husband and Wife

The second requisite demands that the man and the woman live *exclusively* with each other as husband and wife.[^6] [^15] In [*Malang v. Moson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59056) (2000), the Supreme Court En Banc emphasized that while Article 144 of the Civil Code merely required that the parties live together as husband and wife, Article 147 of the Family Code explicitly requires exclusivity, so plural or concurrent cohabitations cannot qualify under Article 147.[^6]

In [*Benasa v. Mahor*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) (2022), the Supreme Court explained the legal meaning of cohabitation, defining *cohabit* as dwelling together, living or residing together as husband and wife for some period of time, as distinguished from occasional, transient interviews for unlawful intercourse.[^10] Addressing the realities of overseas employment, [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) held that a Filipino seafarer's periodic physical absence while working aboard ocean-going vessels does not negate cohabitation where there is clear *animus revertendi* (intention to return), continuous communication by letters and phone calls, regular allotment of monthly salaries to the partner in the Philippines and shared residence in the common dwelling during every shore leave between employment contracts.[^10]

### C. Pre-Marital Cohabitation Followed by Marriage

Property acquired during a period of exclusive, capacitated pre-marital cohabitation that precedes a later marriage ceremony falls under Article 147, as *Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr* shows. *Nayve-Pua v. Union Bank of the Philippines* shows how such a claim can still fail on the evidence:

* In [*Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47393) (2003), Elna Mercado and Bruno Fehr began living together as husband and wife in March 1983, purchased Suite 204 of LCG Condominium on installment under a Contract to Sell in July 1983, had their first child in December 1983 and subsequently married in March 1985.[^14] After their 1985 marriage was declared void *ab initio* under Article 36 in 1998, the trial court declared Suite 204 to be Bruno's exclusive pre-marital property because it was purchased prior to their 1985 wedding.[^14] The Supreme Court reversed, holding that because Elna and Bruno were already living exclusively together as husband and wife without legal impediment in March 1983 when Suite 204 was purchased in July 1983, all three requisites of Article 147 were satisfied during their pre-marital cohabitation, rendering the condominium unit common property owned by them in equal shares.[^14]
* In [*Nayve-Pua v. Union Bank of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69444) (2024), Lani Nayve-Pua and Stephen Pua cohabited without legal impediment starting in December 1975 before marrying in July 1983.[^36] Lani invoked Article 147 over a Quezon City house and lot purchased in 1978 and 1979 during that pre-marital cohabitation. The Supreme Court held that Stephen bought the property with his personal funds and that it remained his separate property. It added that even if Article 147 were applied, its *prima facie* presumption was overcome by uncontroverted documentary evidence and by Lani's failure to prove any contribution.[^36]

## 3. Structure of the Special Co-Ownership: Wages, Salaries, Properties and Fruits

Under the first and second paragraphs of Article 147, the special co-ownership encompasses two primary asset categories while excluding a third category that would otherwise belong to a marital conjugal partnership:

### A. Wages, Salaries and Employment or Retirement Benefits

Under the first paragraph of Article 147, the **wages and salaries** earned by either or both parties during their exclusive cohabitation are owned by them in **equal shares** (`50%–50%`), even if only one party worked to earn the wages and the other party did not earn any income.[^family-code] [^26] [^19] Jurisprudence has applied this rule to both death benefits and retirement or separation benefits accrued from employment during the union:

* **Government Employee Death Benefits (*Cariño v. Cariño*):** In [*Cariño v. Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043) (2001), SPO4 Santiago Cariño contracted a 1969 marriage with Susan Nicdao without a marriage license. Both parties were capacitated to marry each other and were in good faith.[^26] Upon Santiago's death in 1992, Php 146,000.00 in death benefits and remunerations were released by various police and government agencies (MBAI, NAPOLCOM, Commutation, Pag-IBIG and PCCUI).[^26] The Supreme Court held that under Article 147, even though the disputed death benefits were earned by the deceased alone as a government police officer, Article 147 created an equal co-ownership over his wages and remunerations, entitling Susan Nicdao to one-half (`1/2`) thereof as her share in the Article 147 property regime, while the remaining one-half (`1/2`) pertaining to Santiago passed by intestate succession to his legal heirs (his children with Susan Nicdao).[^26]
* **Corporate Retirement and Separation Benefits (*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*):** In [*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809) (2005), after the marriage of Noel and Isabel Singh Buenaventura was declared void under Article 36, the Supreme Court sustained the award to the wife of one-half (`50%`) of the husband's Php 3,675,335.79 net retirement and separation benefits received from Far East Bank and Trust Co., as well as one-half (`50%`) of his shares of stock in Manila Memorial Park and the Provident Group of Companies, holding that because the retirement benefits accrued from services rendered during the union and the stocks were acquired during cohabitation without involving fruits of separate property, they were governed by equal co-ownership under Article 147.[^4]

### B. Property Acquired Through Work or Industry

Property acquired by either or both of the parties during their cohabitation through their work or industry is governed by the rules on equal co-ownership.[^family-code] [^2] [^19]

### C. Exclusion of Fruits of Separate Property

A critical doctrinal distinction between the special co-ownership under Article 147 and the Conjugal Partnership of Gains (CPG) under [Article 106](art-106.md) of the Family Code is the treatment of fruits and income derived from each partner's exclusive or separate property. In [*Valdes v. Regional Trial Court*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980) (1996), [*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809) (2005) and [*Tanyag v. Tanyag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68047) (2021, quoting *Valdes*), the Supreme Court stated that **unlike the conjugal partnership of gains, the fruits of the couple's separate property are not included in the co-ownership** under Article 147.[^2] [^4] [^23] In [*Tanyag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68047), the Supreme Court quoted this passage from *Valdes* in a case where a marriage had been declared void under Article 36.[^23]

## 4. The *Prima Facie* Presumption of Joint Acquisition and Care and Maintenance of the Family and Household

### A. The Statutory Presumption and Domestic Care Equivalence

Under the second paragraph of Article 147, in the absence of proof to the contrary, properties acquired while the capacitated parties lived exclusively together are **presumed to have been obtained by their joint efforts, work or industry** and shall be owned by them in **equal shares**.[^family-code] [^2] Furthermore, the second sentence of the second paragraph establishes a vital statutory rule of gender and domestic equity: a party who did not participate financially or directly in the acquisition by the other party of any property **shall be deemed to have contributed jointly in the acquisition thereof if the former's efforts consisted in the care and maintenance of the family and of the household**.[^family-code] [^2] [^9] [^19]

In [*Gonzales v. Gonzales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41817) (2005), after a marriage was declared void under Article 36 due to the husband's psychological incapacity, the husband sought to overturn the equal division of extensive real properties and vehicles acquired from their Fiesta Pizza business by claiming that the business and properties were exclusively his.[^20] The Supreme Court affirmed the equal division under Article 147, pointing out not only that Article 147 presumes joint acquisition and credits household and family care as a joint contribution, but also that the evidence, including the husband's own handwritten letter, proved that the wife worked seven days a week without salary managing the daily operations, personnel and outlets of Fiesta Pizza.[^20]

### B. Insufficiency of Bare Assertions or Unilateral Title Registration to Rebut the Presumption

Because the law presumes joint effort and equal ownership over properties acquired during cohabitation, a party who claims exclusive ownership bears the burden of presenting clear and convincing *proof to the contrary*:

* **Uncorroborated Testimony of Seed Money or Personal Loans:** In [*Ocampo v. Ocampo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60967) (2015), where both spouses in an Article 36 void marriage stipulated that the disputed properties were acquired during their marriage, the wife's uncorroborated testimonial claim that the family business capital came from her mother's seed money and that her husband was unfaithful failed to overcome the presumption of equal shares under Article 147.[^17] Likewise, in [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519) (2006), the male partner's bare, uncorroborated testimony that he borrowed money on his own to construct the house extension housing a sari-sari store in Benguet did not rebut the Article 147 presumption, especially where his common-law partner worked in Korea and remitted earnings to their joint bank account.[^28]
* **Unnotarized and Unauthenticated Private Deeds:** In [*Barrido v. Nonato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57928) (2014), a wife's reliance on an unnotarized private Deed of Sale purportedly conveying the Bacolod City family property to their children failed to defeat the husband's right to an equal one-half share under Article 147 because the unnotarized private document was inadmissible unless properly authenticated and its due execution was not proved.[^16]
* **Registration of Title or Tax Declaration in One Partner's Name Alone:** The mere fact that a Torrens certificate of title or tax declaration is issued in the name of only one partner, or describes that partner as married to a third person, does not by itself destroy the Article 147 co-ownership if the property was acquired during the cohabitation without proof to the contrary.[^28] [^22] [^17] In [*Salas, Jr. v. Aguila*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56234) (2013), after the marriage of Juan Salas, Jr. and Eden Aguila was declared void under Article 36, Eden discovered three lots acquired in 1999 and 2003 registered in the name of Juan Salas married to Rubina Salas (Juan's subsequent partner).[^22] The Supreme Court held that the phrase *married to Rubina C. Salas* in the Transfer Certificates of Title was merely descriptive of Juan's civil status and proved that Juan, not Rubina, was the registered owner and because Juan failed to rebut the *prima facie* presumption under Article 147, the discovered properties had to be partitioned between Juan and Eden on the basis of co-ownership.[^22]

### C. When the *Prima Facie* Presumption Is Rebutted (*Proof to the Contrary*)

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the presumption of joint acquisition and equal co-ownership under the second paragraph of Article 147 is **merely *prima facie* (disputable)** and yields whenever clear proof to the contrary establishes both (1) exclusive acquisition by one party and (2) the absence of any contribution (financial, industrial or domestic) by the other party:

1. **Exclusive Acquisition by Gratuitous Title and Loan Combined with Failure to Care for the Family and Household (*Tan-Andal v. Andal*):** In the landmark *En Banc* decision in [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) (2021), Rosanna Tan-Andal and Mario Andal's 1995 marriage was declared void under Article 36 due to Mario's psychological incapacity, shown by his drug use, joblessness, the loss of Rosanna's construction firm and his failure to care for the family.[^19] In 1998, Rosanna's aunt donated a 315-square-meter Parañaque lot in equal portions of 157.50 square meters to Rosanna and to her father. Rosanna's portion was titled in the name of Rosanna L. Tan-Andal, of legal age, Filipino, married to Mario Andal. Rosanna and her father then jointly borrowed Php 2,400,000.00 from the Elena P. Tan Foundation by promissory note to build a duplex house on the lot. Mario signed the note only to give marital consent.[^19] The trial court had declared Rosanna the sole owner of the duplex and the lot and the Court of Appeals reversed it. The Supreme Court reinstated the trial court decision and held that if a piece of property was obtained through only one party's effort, work or industry (or gratuitous title and personal loan), **and there is proof that the other party did not contribute even through the care and maintenance of the family and of the household**, the property acquired during cohabitation is **exclusively owned** by the party who acquired it.[^19] Because the funds for the duplex came solely from Rosanna and her father and Mario did not care for and maintain the family and the household, Mario had no share in Rosanna's half of the lot or in the duplex and the phrase *married to Mario Andal* on the Torrens title was merely descriptive of Rosanna's civil status.[^19]
2. **Pre-Marital Documentary Proof of Exclusive Purchase as a Single Person (*Nayve-Pua v. Union Bank of the Philippines*):** In [*Nayve-Pua v. Union Bank of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69444) (2024), Lani and Stephen Pua cohabited starting in 1975 and married in 1983.[^36] When Stephen mortgaged a Quezon City property to Union Bank in 1998, Lani sued to annul the mortgage, claiming co-ownership under Article 147 because the property was acquired in 1978 and 1979 while they were living together.[^36] The Supreme Court held that the property was Stephen's separate property. It added that even assuming Article 147 applied, its *prima facie* presumption was overcome by uncontroverted, self-speaking documentary evidence: (a) the Torrens title was registered solely in Stephen's name and (b) the 1978 Conditional Contract of Sale and 1979 Deed of Absolute Sale were executed solely between the seller and Stephen Pua, described as of legal age, single and a resident of Cauayan, Isabela, while Lani presented zero evidence of any contribution to the property's acquisition.[^36]
3. **Deed of Sale Signed by the Cohabitant Merely as an Instrumental Witness (*Uy v. Spouses Lacsamana*):** In [*Uy v. Spouses Lacsamana*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61014) (2015), Luis Uy and Petra Rosca cohabited without a valid marriage from about the time of World War II until 1973.[^29] When Petra sold a 484-square-meter Batangas City lot covered by TCT No. T-24660 (registered to Petra Rosca, married to Luis G. Uy) to Spouses Lacsamana in 1979 without Luis's consent, Luis sued to annul the sale under the co-ownership rules.[^29] The Supreme Court upheld the validity of Petra's unilateral sale, holding that the *prima facie* presumption of joint acquisition under Article 147 was overcome by proof to the contrary: (a) a Land Registration Commission Consulta resolution recognizing Petra as the sole registered owner of the property, (b) the January 1964 Deed of Sale of the 484-square-meter land, in which Luis signed only under the phrase *signed in the presence of*, which the Supreme Court read as an admission that the land was Petra's paraphernal property, (c) Petra's 1976 Affidavit of Ownership and (d) the established rule that the words *married to Luis G. Uy* on TCT No. T-24660 were merely descriptive of civil status.[^29]

## 5. Temporal Boundaries of Cohabitation, *De Facto* Separation and Installment Acquisitions (*The Paterno Doctrine*)

In [*Paterno v. Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084) (2020), the Supreme Court comprehensively resolved how *de facto* separation prior to a judicial decree of nullity affects the Article 147 co-ownership and how properties purchased on installment or financing during cohabitation but amortized after separation must be liquidated.[^5] In [*Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084), Simon and Dina Paterno married in December 1987, lived together for about a decade, separated *de facto* in June 1998 when Simon left the family home and obtained a final judicial declaration of nullity of marriage under Article 36 in March 2005.[^5] During liquidation under Article 147, Dina claimed a 50% co-ownership share in salaries and assets acquired by Simon after their June 1998 separation in fact, as well as in an Ayala Alabang house and a Rockwell condominium purchased on installment or financing during cohabitation but not fully paid at the separation.[^5]

The Supreme Court laid down three governing rules:

### A. Termination of the Article 147 Co-Ownership Upon *De Facto* Separation

Because a judicial declaration of absolute nullity under Article 36 retroacts to the very date of the celebration of the marriage, the parties are legally deemed never to have been validly married and are relegated to the status of common-law partners under Article 147.[^5] Under the plain text of Article 147 (which covers wages, salaries and properties acquired *while they lived together*), the special co-ownership **terminates upon the parties' *de facto* separation** (when they cease living together exclusively as husband and wife), **not** upon the finality of the judicial decree of nullity.[^5] Consequently, any wages, salaries or properties acquired by either party after their June 1998 separation in fact are excluded from the Article 147 co-ownership and belong exclusively to the acquiring party, unless proven to have been purchased using pre-existing common funds.[^5]

In so ruling, [*Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084) adopted a Court of Appeals exposition that distinguished [*Cariño v. Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043) (2001): in [*Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043), no judicial declaration of nullity was ever obtained prior to the police officer's death, whereas in [*Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084), a final judicial decree of nullity under Article 36 had been issued, retroacting to the wedding date and confining the co-ownership strictly to the period of actual cohabitation.[^5] [^26]

### B. Properties Purchased on Installment or Financing During Cohabitation

Rejecting the husband's contention that properties bought through bank financing or installment during cohabitation are not "acquired" until full payment of the loan, [*Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084) applied the canon of statutory construction that when the law does not distinguish, neither should the court.[^5] Because Article 147 makes no distinction regarding the mode of payment, **for as long as a property was purchased during the period of cohabitation (whether in cash, on installment, through bank financing or by any other mode of payment), the disputable presumption arises that it was obtained by the parties' joint efforts and is co-owned by them** (a principle likewise applied to a pre-marital installment Contract to Sell in [*Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47393)).[^14] [^5]

### C. Pro-Rata Partition of Post-Separation Amortizations

However, [*Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084) introduced a crucial equitable qualification when an installment property purchased during cohabitation continues to be amortized after the parties' *de facto* separation and the non-paying partner's contribution consisted solely in the care and maintenance of the family and household:

* From the moment of *de facto* separation, **there is no longer any common family or household to care for and maintain**.[^5]
* Therefore, where one party's contribution to an installment property consisted solely in caring for and maintaining the family and household, **his or her equal (`50%`) co-ownership share pertains only to the portion of the property paid for before the *de facto* separation**.[^5]
* Amortizations paid after the *de facto* separation belong exclusively to the paying party unless it is shown that those post-separation payments were taken from the parties' common funds.[^5]

## 6. Prohibition on *Inter Vivos* Disposition or Encumbrance During Cohabitation and Interplay with Article 87 (*The Perez, Jr. Doctrine*)

### A. Paragraph 3 of Article 147 Overrides Article 493 of the Civil Code

Under the third paragraph of Article 147, **neither party can encumber or dispose by acts *inter vivos* of his or her share in the property acquired during cohabitation and owned in common, without the consent of the other, until after the termination of their cohabitation**.[^family-code] [^2] [^11]

In the landmark decision of [*Perez, Jr. v. Perez-Senerpida*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66975) (2021), the Supreme Court settled the doctrinal conflict between [Article 493](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0493.md) of the Civil Code (governing ordinary co-ownership, which allows each co-owner to alienate, assign or mortgage his or her undivided ideal share without the consent of the other co-owners) and the third paragraph of Article 147 of the Family Code (governing special co-ownership between cohabitants).[^11]

In [*Perez, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66975), Eliodoro and Adelita Perez married in 1975 and acquired a 350-square-meter parcel of land in Olongapo City (TCT No. T-7396) during their union.[^11] In October 1995, while still married, Adelita executed a gratuitous Renunciation and Waiver of Rights (RWR) over the property in favor of Eliodoro.[^11] In July 2004, before the judgment declaring their marriage void *ab initio* under Article 36 became final in July 2005, Eliodoro executed a Deed of Donation donating the entire Olongapo property to his grandson Nicxon Perez, Jr. without Adelita's consent.[^11] The grandson argued that even if Adelita's waiver was void, Eliodoro's donation should be upheld at least as to Eliodoro's one-half (`1/2`) undivided ideal share under Article 493 of the Civil Code.[^11]

The Supreme Court rejected the argument and declared the 2004 Deed of Donation **completely void** in its entirety:

1. **Special Co-Ownership vs. Ordinary Co-Ownership:** While the first paragraph of Article 147 states that property acquired through work or industry shall be governed by the rules on co-ownership, the third paragraph of Article 147 carves out a specific, mandatory restriction prohibiting either party from encumbering or disposing by acts *inter vivos* of even his or her own share in the common property without the other's consent until after the termination of cohabitation.[^11]
2. **Subordination of Civil Code Article 493:** Because the third paragraph of Article 147 was specifically enacted to protect each partner's interest in the special co-ownership during cohabitation, **Article 493 of the Civil Code must yield to the third paragraph of Article 147 of the Family Code**.[^11] Consequently, any *inter vivos* sale, donation or encumbrance of common property, or even of the disposing partner's own undivided share therein, executed during cohabitation without the consent of the other partner is **null and void in its entirety**.[^11]

Similarly, in [*Sales v. Sales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49411) (2009), where a husband opposed a proposed project of partition in an Article 36 nullity case by alleging that his former wife had unilaterally sold four out of twelve co-owned Xavierville townhouse units and collected ten years of rentals without his consent in violation of Article 147, the Supreme Court held that the trial court committed reversible error in approving the partition without conducting a full evidentiary hearing to resolve the unauthorized sales and rental accounting.[^21]

### B. Interplay with Article 87: Void Donations and Gratuitous Waivers Between Cohabitants

[*Perez, Jr. v. Perez-Senerpida*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66975) (2021) also clarified the interplay between Article 147, [Article 89](art-089.md) and [Article 87](art-087.md) of the Family Code regarding waivers and donations between partners in a void marriage:

* **Inapplicability of Article 89:** The lower courts in [*Perez, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66975) had nullified Adelita's 1995 Renunciation and Waiver of Rights under Article 89 of the Family Code, which prohibits waivers of rights, interests, shares and effects of the absolute community of property during the marriage.[^11] The Supreme Court held that Article 89 was inapplicable because the marriage was declared void *ab initio* under Article 36, meaning no absolute community of property or conjugal partnership of gains ever existed between Eliodoro and Adelita.[^11]
* **Applicability of Article 87 to Gratuitous Waivers and Donations (*Matabuena* Codification):** Nevertheless, the Supreme Court held that Adelita's 1995 gratuitous Renunciation and Waiver of Rights in favor of Eliodoro was **void under [Article 87](art-087.md) of the Family Code**, which declares void every donation or grant of gratuitous advantage, direct or indirect, between the spouses during the marriage and expressly extends that prohibition to **persons living together as husband and wife without a valid marriage** (codifying the doctrine in *Matabuena v. Cervantes*, as cited in *Perez, Jr. v. Perez-Senerpida* and *Agapay v. Palang*).[^9] [^11]
* In [*Agapay v. Palang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006) (1997), the Supreme Court likewise applied Article 87 of the Family Code to nullify a disguised donation where an elderly married man paid the full purchase price of a house and lot in Binalonan, Pangasinan and had the title registered solely in the name of his young common-law partner during their cohabitation.[^9]

## 7. Forfeiture of Share in Case of Bad Faith and Moral Damages in Void Marriages

### A. Statutory Order of Forfeiture Under Paragraph 4

Under the fourth paragraph of Article 147, when **only one of the parties to a void marriage is in good faith**, the share of the party in bad faith in the special co-ownership is forfeited upon the termination of the cohabitation in the following strict statutory order of preference:

1. **First, in favor of their common children**,
2. **Second, in case of default of or waiver by any or all of the common children or their descendants**, each vacant share belongs to the **respective surviving descendants** and
3. **Third, in the absence of descendants**, such forfeited share belongs to the **innocent party**.[^family-code] [^2]

In all cases, the forfeiture takes place upon the termination of the cohabitation.[^family-code] [^2]

### B. Presumption of Good Faith and Evidentiary Standard for Forfeiture

Good faith is presumed in the absence of specific allegation and clear proof of bad faith:

* In [*Cariño v. Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043) (2001), because there was no allegation or proof of bad faith in the 1969 license-less marriage between Santiago Cariño and Susan Nicdao, the Supreme Court held that both parties were presumed to be in good faith, entitling each to an unreduced one-half (`1/2`) share in the Article 147 co-ownership.[^26]
* In [*Ocampo v. Ocampo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60967) (2015), the wife sought to forfeit the husband's one-half share under Article 147 in an Article 36 void marriage by testifying that he was unfaithful and failed to support the family.[^17] The Supreme Court rejected the forfeiture claim, holding that her uncorroborated testimonial allegations fell short of the clear proof of bad faith required to strip a co-owner of his statutory share.[^17]
* In [*Tee Ten v. Tee Ten*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_259322_2025.html) (2025), where the wife in an Article 36 nullity case prayed in the alternative that the husband be declared a spouse in bad faith so that his share in their common properties would be forfeited in her favor under the fourth paragraph of Article 147, both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals found no factual basis for bad-faith forfeiture and the Supreme Court declined to disturb those uniform factual findings on appeal.[^25]

### C. Incompatibility of Psychological Incapacity with Moral and Exemplary Damages (*Buenaventura* Rule)

Closely related to the question of bad faith is whether a finding of psychological incapacity under [Article 36](../title-01-marriage/art-036.md) entitles the other spouse to moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under [Article 21](../../civil-code/preliminary-title/art-0021.md) and the damages provisions of the Civil Code. In [*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809) (2005), the Supreme Court held that **a finding of psychological incapacity negates the possibility of awarding moral damages on the same set of facts**.[^4] It is contradictory to treat acts as the product of an innate inability and also as willful. An award of moral damages would instead need specific evidence that a party with knowledge of his or her disability deliberately and with malice concealed it.[^4]

## 8. Comparative Distinction: Article 147 (Capacitated Unions) vs. Article 148 (Incapacitated Unions)

A cornerstone of Family Code property litigation is the sharp statutory and doctrinal boundary between **Article 147** (governing capacitated unions) and **[Article 148](art-148.md)** (governing unions where the parties are *not* capacitated to marry each other because of a legal impediment, such as a subsisting prior marriage, adulterous concubinage, bigamy, incest under Article 37, public-policy relationships under Article 38 or non-age below eighteen years).[^2] [^9] [^7] [^26] [^30]

### A. Side-by-Side Application in *Cariño v. Cariño* (2001)

The contrast between Articles 147 and 148 is vividly illustrated in [*Cariño v. Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043) (2001), where the Supreme Court applied both provisions in a single decision to two successive void marriages contracted by the same police officer, SPO4 Santiago Cariño:[^26]

1. **First Marriage (Void for Lack of Marriage License, Governed by Article 147):** Santiago married Susan Nicdao in 1969 without a marriage license when both were capacitated to marry each other.[^26] Because they were capacitated to marry each other and lived exclusively together under a void marriage, **Article 147** governed their union.[^26] Under Article 147, wages and salaries earned by either party are owned in equal shares even if only one party earned them, so Susan Nicdao automatically owned one-half (`1/2`) of Santiago's Php 146,000.00 death benefits, while Santiago's other one-half (`1/2`) passed to his children with Susan Nicdao.[^26]
2. **Second Marriage (Void for Bigamy Under Article 40, Governed by Article 148):** Without first securing a judicial declaration of nullity of his 1969 marriage to Susan Nicdao as required by [Article 40](../title-01-marriage/art-040.md) of the Family Code, Santiago contracted a second marriage with Susan Yee in 1992 after cohabiting with her since 1982.[^26] Because Santiago had not first obtained a judicial declaration of nullity of his first marriage as Article 40 requires, the second marriage was bigamous and void and Santiago and Susan Yee were not capacitated to marry each other, placing their union under **Article 148**.[^26] Under Article 148, wages and salaries earned by a party belong exclusively to that earning party and co-ownership arises only over properties acquired through proven actual joint contribution of money, property or industry. Because Susan Yee contributed nothing to Santiago's police remunerations, she received zero share in his death benefits.[^26]

### B. Doctrinal Rules Governing Incapacitated Unions Under Article 148 (By Contrast to Article 147)

In the decisions below, the Supreme Court has delineated how [Article 148](art-148.md) departs from Article 147 whenever a legal impediment taints the union:

* **Actual Contribution of Money, Property or Industry Strictly Required, Domestic Care Excluded:** Unlike Article 147 (which presumes joint acquisition and treats efforts in the care and maintenance of the family and household as a joint contribution by a partner who has no salary, income, work or industry), **Article 148 strictly requires proof of actual joint contribution of money, property or industry** (*Agapay v. Palang*, *Tumlos v. Spouses Fernandez*, *Saguid v. Court of Appeals*, *Francisco v. Master Iron Works & Construction Corp.*, *Atienza v. De Castro*, *Borromeo v. Descallar*, *Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*, *Vda. de Canada v. Baclot* and *Benasa v. Mahor*).[^9] [^7] [^30] [^31] [^32] [^33] [^34] [^35] [^10] Under Article 148, care and maintenance of the home, spiritual or moral inspiration and mere administration of the other partner's property do **not** count as contributions (*Tumlos v. Spouses Fernandez*).[^7] If actual contribution of money, property or industry is not proved, there is **no co-ownership and no presumption of equal shares** (*Agapay v. Palang* and *Atienza v. De Castro*).[^9] [^32]
* **Proportional Shares vs. Equal Shares:** Under Article 147, co-owned properties and wages are owned in **equal (`50%–50%`) shares**.[^family-code] [^2] Under Article 148, properties acquired through actual joint contribution are owned in **proportion to the parties' respective contributions** and their contributions and shares are presumed equal **only** after actual joint contribution has first been proven, but the exact amount of each party's contribution cannot be determined (*Saguid v. Court of Appeals*, where a married 17-year-old partner proved only Php 11,413.00 in construction receipts for the house, while personal properties bought from an undisputed joint bank account were divided equally at Php 55,687.50 each for lack of proof of exact shares).[^30]
* **Invalid Foreign Divorce Between Filipinos Triggers Article 148 (*Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*):** In [*Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57326) (2014), a Filipino lawyer who was validly married in 1947 obtained a quick divorce in the Dominican Republic in 1976 and married a second Filipina the same day.[^34] The Supreme Court held that because a foreign divorce between Filipino citizens is void under the nationality principle ([Article 15](../../civil-code/preliminary-title/art-0015.md) of the Civil Code), the first marriage and its conjugal partnership subsisted until the lawyer's death, rendering the second marriage bigamous and governed by Article 148 rather than Article 147.[^34] Because the second wife failed to prove actual contribution toward the Makati law office condominium unit and law books and the phrase *married to Soledad L. Luna* on the condominium title was merely descriptive of civil status, she acquired no co-ownership share.[^34]
* **Self-Serving Waivers Against Creditors (*Francisco v. Master Iron Works*):** In [*Francisco v. Master Iron Works & Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43125) (2005), where a woman contracted a bigamous marriage with a married man and sought to annul a creditor's execution levy on two Parañaque lots by relying on her husband's annotated Affidavit of Waiver, the Supreme Court applied Article 148 retroactively under Article 256 and held that her contradictory claims and her husband's self-serving waiver did not prove her actual contribution against the judgment creditor.[^31]
* **Foreign Cohabitant and Married Filipino Partner (*Borromeo v. Descallar*):** In [*Borromeo v. Descallar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48948) (2009), an Austrian national cohabited with a Filipina who was still legally married to another man, purchased Mandaue real properties using his exclusive funds, registered them in the Filipina's name due to the constitutional ban on alien land ownership and later assigned his rights to a Filipino buyer.[^33] The Supreme Court held that Article 147 did not apply because the Filipina had a subsisting prior marriage. Under Article 148, she had no income or actual contribution and the alien's subsequent transfer of the land to a qualified Filipino citizen cured the constitutional flaw and vested valid title in the Filipino buyer.[^33]
* **Implied Trust Rules Immaterial, Partition Not a Collateral Attack (*Mallilin, Jr. v. Castillo*):** In [*Mallilin, Jr. v. Castillo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37522) (2000), where two partners who were both validly married to their respective spouses cohabited from 1979 to 1992 and operated a joint business, the Supreme Court held that while Article 144 of the Civil Code and Article 147 of the Family Code apply only to capacitated partners, Article 148 creates a limited co-ownership over properties acquired after August 3, 1988 through actual joint contribution. Because co-ownership is already a recognized form of trust, [Articles 1452](../../civil-code/book-4-title-05-trusts/art-1452.md) and [1453](../../civil-code/book-4-title-05-trusts/art-1453.md) of the Civil Code on implied trusts are immaterial and an action for partition to enforce a co-owner's share in properties registered solely in the other partner's name is not a prohibited collateral attack on a Torrens title.[^37]

## 9. Liquidation, Partition, Jurisdiction and Procedural Governance

### A. Inapplicability of Articles 50, 51, 52, 102 and 129, Civil Code Co-Ownership Rules Prevail

In the foundational case of [*Valdes v. Regional Trial Court, Branch 102, Quezon City*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980) (1996), the husband in a marriage declared void under Article 36 argued that the family dwelling should be adjudicated to him under [Articles 50](../title-01-marriage/art-050.md) and [129(9)](art-129.md) of the Family Code because their majority-aged children chose to remain with him.[^2] The Supreme Court rejected his argument and established the governing rule for all void marriages under Article 147:

1. **Civil Code Co-Ownership Rules Govern Liquidation:** In liquidating, partitioning and distributing properties co-owned under Article 147, the **provisions on co-ownership under the Civil Code** ([Articles 484](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0484.md) to [501](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0501.md)) prevail, **not** Articles 50, 51 and 52 in relation to [Articles 102](art-102.md) (ACP liquidation) and [129](art-129.md) (CPG liquidation) of the Family Code.[^2]
2. **Scope of Articles 50, 51 and 52 Limited to Voidable (Article 45) and Subsequent Void (Article 40) Marriages:** Although the first paragraph of Article 50 refers to the second, third and fourth paragraphs of [Article 43](../title-01-marriage/art-043.md) and to [Article 44](../title-01-marriage/art-044.md) as applying to marriages declared void *ab initio* or annulled by final judgment under [Articles 40](../title-01-marriage/art-040.md) and [45](../title-01-marriage/art-045.md), those liquidation and presumptive-legitime rules apply **only** to voidable marriages under Article 45 and, by express statutory exception, to subsequent void marriages contracted under Article 40 without a prior judicial declaration of nullity of a previous void marriage.[^2] [^14] [^4] [^15]
3. **Continued Force of Family Home Provisions:** [*Valdes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980) clarified, however, that the rules on the **family home** under Title V, Chapter 2 ([Articles 152](../title-05-the-family/art-152.md) to [162](../title-05-the-family/art-162.md)) of the Family Code remain in force during liquidation until the family home is partitioned in accordance with the rules on co-ownership.[^2]

Subsequent Supreme Court decisions have enforced this doctrine across diverse procedural settings:

* **No Three-Way Partition or Delivery of Presumptive Legitime to Children (*Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr*):** In [*Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47393) (2003), the trial court in an Article 36 nullity case ordered a three-way partition of the couple's properties (`1/3` to the wife, `1/3` to the husband and `1/3` to their two children as presumptive legitime under Articles 50 and 51).[^14] The Supreme Court set aside the three-way division, holding that Articles 50 and 51 do not apply to an Article 36 void marriage governed by Article 147 and remanded the case to the Regional Trial Court of Makati for liquidation of the properties under the Civil Code co-ownership rules.[^14]
* **Inapplicability of Article 129(9) Conjugal Dwelling Preference (*Barrido v. Nonato*):** In [*Barrido v. Nonato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57928) (2014), the Municipal Trial Court in Cities (MTCC) adjudicated the Bacolod City house and lot in a partition suit to the former wife under Article 129(9) of the Family Code because the couple's children chose to remain with her.[^16] The Regional Trial Court reversed the MTCC and the Court of Appeals affirmed the Regional Trial Court. The Supreme Court denied the former wife's petition. It held that because the marriage had been declared void under Article 36, Article 129 had no application and the property had to be partitioned equally under Article 147.[^16]
* **Disciplinary Sanction for Forum Shopping Between Article 36 and Article 45 (*In Re: Castro-Roa*):** In [*In Re: A.M. No. 04-7-373-RTC, Prosecutor Mary Ann T. Castro-Roa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62024) (2016), the Supreme Court suspended a public prosecutor for six months for forum shopping after she filed an Article 45 annulment petition while her Article 36 nullity petition was pending, emphasizing the distinct property consequences of the two actions: an Article 45 voidable marriage is governed by ACP or CPG requiring prior liquidation under Articles 50 and 51, whereas an Article 36 void marriage is governed by co-ownership under Articles 147 and 148.[^38]

### B. Prior Liquidation Is Not a Prerequisite to the Issuance of a Decree of Absolute Nullity (*The Diño Rule*)

In [*Diño v. Diño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33909) (2011), the Regional Trial Court declared the parties' marriage void *ab initio* under Article 36, but ordered that the Decree of Absolute Nullity of Marriage be issued only after the prior liquidation, partition and distribution of the parties' properties under Article 147, invoking Section 19(1) of the *Rule on Declaration of Absolute Nullity of Void Marriages and Annulment of Voidable Marriages* (A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC).[^15]

The Supreme Court reversed that condition and established a vital procedural rule:

* **Section 19(1) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC and Articles 50 and 51 of the Family Code apply only to marriages declared void under Article 40 or annulled under Article 45** and **do not apply to marriages declared void *ab initio* under Article 36** (which are governed by Article 147 or 148).[^15]
* Consequently, in an Article 36 case, **the Decree of Absolute Nullity of Marriage must be issued upon the finality of the decision without waiting for the liquidation, partition and distribution of the co-owned properties of the parties**.[^15]
* The Supreme Court reaffirmed this rule in [*Tee Ten v. Tee Ten*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_259322_2025.html) (2025) and [*Soto v. Reyes-Soto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70372) (2026), reiterating that it is not necessary to liquidate the common properties of the spouses as a prerequisite to the declaration of nullity under Article 36.[^25] [^18]

### C. Extrajudicial Partition Under Civil Code Article 496 and Co-Ownership Remedies

Because the liquidation of an Article 147 co-ownership is governed by the Civil Code provisions on co-ownership rather than Articles 50 and 51 of the Family Code, **partition may be made either by extrajudicial agreement between the parties or by judicial proceedings** under [Article 496](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0496.md) of the Civil Code.[^15] [^27] [^25] [^18]

* **Validity of Extrajudicial Partition Without Judicial Approval (*Diaz-Salgado v. Anson*):** In [*Diaz-Salgado v. Anson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62224) (2016), Luis Anson and Severina de Asis contracted a civil marriage in 1966 without a marriage license and separated in 1980–1981, executing notarized extrajudicial Partition Agreements dividing their real properties.[^27] Two decades later, after Severina sold three San Juan properties allocated to her under the partition to her daughter, Luis sued to annul the sales, arguing that the properties were conjugal and that their 1980–1981 extrajudicial partition agreements were void for lack of judicial approval under the Civil Code rules on separation of conjugal property.[^27] The Supreme Court rejected Luis's claim, holding that because their 1966 license-less marriage was void *ab initio* and both parties were capacitated to marry each other, their property regime was governed by Civil Code Article 144 in relation to Family Code Article 147 and under Article 496 of the Civil Code, **co-owners under Article 147 may validly partition their common properties by extrajudicial agreement without need of court approval**.[^27]
* **Inapplicability of Ejectment Against a Co-Owner (*Abing v. Waeyan*):** In [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519) (2006), after the partners prepared a Memorandum of Agreement that they left unsigned but that their witnesses signed, the female partner paid Php 232,397.66 as partial payment of the male partner's share in their properties and failed to pay the balance and the male partner filed an ejectment suit against her.[^28] The Supreme Court dismissed the ejectment suit, holding that as a co-owner under Article 147, she was entitled to possession and could not be ejected under [Article 487](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0487.md) of the Civil Code and that the male partner's proper remedy for the unpaid balance under the partition agreement was a civil action for collection of a sum of money or rescission of contract, not ejectment.[^28]

### D. Jurisdiction, Incidental Authority of the Nullity Court, *Litis Pendentia* and Indispensable Parties

Jurisprudence has delineated the jurisdictional rules governing judicial liquidation and partition of Article 147 properties:

1. **Incidental Jurisdiction of the Family Court in a Nullity Proceeding (*Domingo*, *Tanyag*, *Alberto* and *Tee Ten*):**
   * In [*Domingo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_104818_1993.html) (1993), the Supreme Court held that a party may petition for the judicial declaration of nullity of a void marriage not only for purposes of remarriage under Article 40, but also for purposes of liquidation, partition, distribution and separation of property and the court hearing the nullity petition has incidental jurisdiction to resolve property questions without requiring a separate civil action.[^3]
   * In [*Tanyag v. Tanyag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68047) (2021), while a wife's Article 36 Nullity Case was pending before the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, she filed a separate Property Case before the Regional Trial Court of La Trinidad, Benguet to declare two parcels of land as her exclusive paraphernal property.[^23] The Supreme Court ordered the dismissal of the Benguet Property Case on the ground of ***litis pendentia* and forum shopping by splitting a single cause of action**, holding that the Family Court hearing the Article 36 Nullity Case is clothed with authority to resolve incidental and consequential property matters, including determining whether specific properties are exclusive or belong to the Article 147 co-ownership under Section 21 of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC.[^23]
   * Similarly, in [*Alberto v. Alberto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68353) (2022), the Supreme Court reinstated the trial court's decision declaring the marriage void under Article 36 and ordering the liquidation of two residential properties covered by Transfer Certificates of Title in accordance with Article 147.[^24] And in [*Tee Ten v. Tee Ten*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_259322_2025.html) (2025), while reiterating that liquidation under Article 147 need not be done in the Article 36 proceeding, the Supreme Court held that because both spouses actively submitted their property liquidation and support claims in their pleadings before the Family Court, it was proper to remand the case to the Family Court for further proceedings on the liquidation, partition and distribution of their co-owned properties under Article 147.[^25]
2. **Requirement of an Evidentiary Hearing on Disputed Partition Issues and Mode of Appeal (*Sales v. Sales*):** In [*Sales v. Sales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49411) (2009), the Supreme Court held that where a party opposes a proposed project of partition under Article 147 by raising genuine factual issues (such as unremitted rentals, unauthorized sales of common properties and misappropriation of proceeds), the trial court cannot summarily approve the project of partition without conducting a trial for the reception of evidence and an order finally disposing of partition issues is a final order reviewable by ordinary appeal.[^21]
3. **Jurisdiction Over a Standalone Action for Partition Determined by Assessed Value (*Barrido v. Nonato*):** When the judicial declaration of nullity has already become final and a former spouse subsequently files a standalone civil complaint for judicial partition of real property co-owned under Article 147, the suit is a **real action** whose subject-matter jurisdiction between the first-level courts (MTC/MTCC) and the Regional Trial Court is determined by the **assessed value of the real property** under Section 33(3) of Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 (as amended by Republic Act No. 7691), as held in [*Barrido v. Nonato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57928) (2014).[^16]
4. **Indispensable Parties in Competing Estate and Co-Ownership Claims (*Uy v. Court of Appeals*):** In [*Uy v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/may1994/gr_102726_1994.html) (1994), where a decedent's alleged legal wife and legitimate son were actively asserting conjugal ownership over the decedent's properties in a pending intestate estate proceeding before one branch of the Regional Trial Court and the decedent's 36-year common-law partner filed a separate partition suit under Articles 147 and 148 before another branch excluding the legal wife and legitimate son and quickly entered into a compromise judgment with her own children, the Supreme Court held that the legal wife and legitimate son were **indispensable parties** whose exclusion rendered the compromise judgment null and void and open to intervention even after judgment.[^13]

## Related Provisions

* **[Article 5](../title-01-marriage/art-005.md), [Article 37](../title-01-marriage/art-037.md) and [Article 38](../title-01-marriage/art-038.md) of the Family Code:** Define legal capacity to contract marriage (any male or female of the age of eighteen years or upwards not suffering from any diriment impediment of incest or public policy), which supplies the statutory definition of *capacitated* under the first paragraph of Article 147.[^2] [^19]
* **[Article 35](../title-01-marriage/art-035.md) and [Article 36](../title-01-marriage/art-036.md) of the Family Code:** Enumerate marriages that are void from the beginning, including marriages contracted without a valid marriage license under Article 35(3) and marriages void due to psychological incapacity under Article 36. When neither party suffers from a legal impediment to marry the other, their property relations during cohabitation are governed by Article 147.[^2] [^26] [^19]
* **[Article 40](../title-01-marriage/art-040.md), [Article 43](../title-01-marriage/art-043.md), [Article 45](../title-01-marriage/art-045.md), [Article 50](../title-01-marriage/art-050.md), [Article 51](../title-01-marriage/art-051.md) and [Article 52](../title-01-marriage/art-052.md) of the Family Code:** Govern the liquidation of property, forfeiture of net profits and delivery of presumptive legitimes in voidable marriages annulled under Article 45 and subsequent void marriages under Article 40. Under *Valdes*, *Mercado-Fehr* and *Diño*, these provisions do **not** apply to void marriages governed by Article 147.[^2] [^14] [^15]
* **[Article 87](art-087.md) of the Family Code:** Declares void every donation or grant of gratuitous advantage, direct or indirect, between spouses during the marriage and extends that ban to persons living together as husband and wife without a valid marriage, rendering gratuitous waivers and disguised donations between Article 147 or Article 148 cohabitants null and void.[^9] [^11]
* **[Article 102](art-102.md) and [Article 129](art-129.md) of the Family Code:** Prescribe the liquidation procedures for Absolute Community of Property and Conjugal Partnership of Gains (including the adjudication of the conjugal dwelling under Article 129(9)), which are inapplicable to the liquidation of an Article 147 co-ownership.[^2] [^16]
* **[Article 148](art-148.md) of the Family Code:** Companion provision governing the limited co-ownership of unions without marriage or under a void marriage where the parties are **not** capacitated to marry each other due to a legal impediment, requiring strict proof of actual joint contribution of money, property or industry and excluding domestic care and maintenance.[^9] [^7] [^26] [^30]
* **[Article 152](../title-05-the-family/art-152.md) to [Article 162](../title-05-the-family/art-162.md) of the Family Code:** Govern the constitution, exemption and dissolution of the family home, which remain in force during the liquidation of an Article 147 co-ownership until the property is partitioned under the Civil Code co-ownership rules.[^2]
* **[Article 256](../title-12-final-provisions/art-256.md) of the Family Code:** Provides for the retroactive effect of the Family Code insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights, allowing Article 147 to apply retroactively to pre-1988 unions where no prior vested rights of third parties or legitimate heirs are impaired.[^8] [^5]
* **[Article 144](../../civil-code/book-1-title-06-property-relations-between/art-0144.md), [Article 484](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0484.md), [Article 487](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0487.md), [Article 493](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0493.md) and [Article 496](../../civil-code/book-2-title-03-co-ownership/art-0496.md) of the Civil Code:** Article 144 of the 1950 Civil Code is the statutory predecessor of Article 147. Articles 484 to 501 of the Civil Code govern the liquidation and extrajudicial or judicial partition of properties co-owned under Article 147, except that Article 493 yields to the third paragraph of Article 147 prohibiting unilateral *inter vivos* dispositions or encumbrances during cohabitation.[^2] [^28] [^27] [^11]

## Key Cases

* **[*Valdes v. Regional Trial Court, Branch 102, Quezon City*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980)** (G.R. No. 122749, July 31, 1996): Landmark decision establishing that in a marriage declared void *ab initio* under Article 36, the property relations of capacitated spouses are governed by the special co-ownership regime of Article 147 (under which fruits of separate property are excluded and household care counts as a joint contribution) and that liquidation is governed by the Civil Code rules on co-ownership rather than Articles 50, 51, 52, 102 and 129 of the Family Code.[^2]
* **[*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507)** (G.R. No. 196359, May 11, 2021, *En Banc*): Held that while Article 147 presumes joint acquisition of properties obtained during cohabitation in a void Article 36 marriage, the presumption is rebutted where a parcel of land was gratuitously donated to the wife and the duplex house thereon was funded solely by the wife and her father through a loan from a foundation and the evidence proved that the jobless, drug-dependent husband did not contribute even through the care and maintenance of the family and household.[^19]
* **[*Perez, Jr. v. Perez-Senerpida*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66975)** (G.R. No. 233365, March 24, 2021): Held that in a marriage declared void under Article 36, (1) a wife's gratuitous Renunciation and Waiver of Rights in favor of her husband during cohabitation is void under Article 87 of the Family Code and (2) Article 493 of the Civil Code yields to the third paragraph of Article 147, rendering a husband's unilateral *inter vivos* donation of common property to his grandson during cohabitation without the wife's consent completely void even as to the husband's undivided half-share.[^11]
* **[*Paterno v. Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084)** (G.R. No. 213687, January 8, 2020): Established that (1) Article 147 applies retroactively under Article 256 to a 1987 void marriage, (2) the Article 147 co-ownership terminates upon the parties' *de facto* separation rather than upon the finality of the judicial decree of nullity, (3) properties purchased on installment or financing during cohabitation are included in the co-ownership and (4) where a spouse's contribution consisted solely in caring for and maintaining the family and household, her equal 50% share in installment properties pertains only to the portion paid prior to *de facto* separation unless post-separation amortizations came from common funds.[^5]
* **[*Cariño v. Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043)** (G.R. No. 132529, February 2, 2001): Applied Articles 147 and 148 side by side to two void marriages of a deceased police officer, holding that his first marriage (void for lack of a marriage license, where both parties were capacitated) was governed by Article 147, entitling the first wife to one-half (`1/2`) of his Php 146,000.00 death benefits and wages, whereas his second marriage (void for bigamy under Article 40 for lack of a prior judicial decree of nullity) was governed by Article 148, giving the second wife zero share in his death benefits.[^26]
* **[*Diño v. Diño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33909)** (G.R. No. 178044, January 19, 2011): Held that Article 50 of the Family Code and Section 19(1) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC requiring prior liquidation, partition and distribution of properties before issuance of a Decree of Absolute Nullity apply only to marriages under Articles 40 and 45 and do not apply to Article 36 void marriages governed by Article 147.[^15]
* **[*Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47393)** (G.R. No. 152716, October 23, 2003): Held that a condominium unit purchased on installment during pre-marital cohabitation by capacitated partners whose subsequent marriage was declared void under Article 36 is common property owned in equal shares under Article 147 and reversed a trial court's three-way partition that erroneously awarded one-third (`1/3`) to the children as presumptive legitime under Articles 50 and 51.[^14]
* **[*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809)** (G.R. Nos. 127358 & 127449, March 31, 2005): Deleted moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees as incompatible with a finding of psychological incapacity under Article 36, while sustaining the award to the wife of 50% of the husband's retirement benefits and corporate shares of stock accrued or acquired during cohabitation under the equal co-ownership rule of Article 147.[^4]
* **[*Nayve-Pua v. Union Bank of the Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69444)** (G.R. No. 253450, January 22, 2024): Held that property bought by the male partner with his own funds before the marriage remained his separate property and added that even if Article 147 applied, its *prima facie* presumption was overcome where the Torrens title, Conditional Contract of Sale and Deed of Absolute Sale were all executed in the sole name of the male partner as a single man and the female partner failed to prove any contribution to its acquisition.[^36]
* **[*Diaz-Salgado v. Anson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62224)** (G.R. No. 204494, July 27, 2016): Held that where a marriage is void *ab initio* for lack of a marriage license between capacitated parties, their property regime is governed by Civil Code Article 144 in relation to Family Code Article 147 and under Article 496 of the Civil Code, they may validly partition their co-owned properties by extrajudicial agreement without need of court approval.[^27]
* **[*Tanyag v. Tanyag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68047)** (G.R. No. 231319, November 10, 2021): Dismissed a separate civil action for declaration of paraphernal property on the ground of *litis pendentia* and forum shopping because the Family Court hearing the pending Article 36 Nullity Case had incidental jurisdiction to determine which properties belonged to or were excluded from the Article 147 special co-ownership.[^23]
* **[*Barrido v. Nonato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57928)** (G.R. No. 176492, October 20, 2014): Held that a standalone complaint for partition of real property under Article 147 falls within the jurisdiction of the MTCC or RTC based on assessed value under B.P. Blg. 129 and that Article 129(9) adjudicating the conjugal dwelling to the spouse with whom the children choose to remain does not apply to a void marriage governed by Article 147.[^16]
* **[*Uy v. Spouses Lacsamana*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61014)** (G.R. No. 206220, August 19, 2015): Held that the *prima facie* presumption of joint acquisition under Article 147 in a non-marital union was overcome by an LRC Consulta resolution, an Affidavit of Ownership and a Deed of Sale in which the male cohabitant signed merely as an instrumental witness while the phrase *married to Luis G. Uy* on the Torrens title was merely descriptive of civil status.[^29]
* **[*Tee Ten v. Tee Ten*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_259322_2025.html)** (G.R. No. 259322, August 6, 2025) and **[*Soto v. Reyes-Soto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70372)** (G.R. No. 249759, April 22, 2026): Reaffirmed that liquidation of common properties under Article 147 is governed by the rules on co-ownership and is not a prerequisite to the declaration of nullity under Article 36, while holding in *Tee Ten* that where both spouses actively pleaded property and support issues in the nullity case, those incidental matters should be resolved on remand by the Family Court.[^25] [^18]

## Suggested Answers

**Q1: Hypothetical: Assume that A and B, both single and of legal age with no impediment to marry each other, married in 1987, purchased a house on bank financing during their cohabitation, separated in fact in 1998 when A left the family home and obtained a final judicial declaration of nullity of marriage under Article 36 in 2005. Assume further that B's contribution to the acquisition of the house consisted solely in the care and maintenance of the family and household while they lived together and that A continued paying the monthly loan amortizations from his separate post-1998 salary after their separation in fact. Is B entitled to an equal 50% share in the entire value of the house up to the finality of the nullity decree in 2005?**

No. In [*Paterno v. Paterno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66084), the Supreme Court held that because a judicial declaration of nullity under Article 36 retroacts to the celebration of the marriage, the special co-ownership under Article 147 terminates upon the parties' *de facto* separation rather than upon the finality of the nullity decree.[^5] While a property purchased on installment or bank financing during cohabitation is presumed obtained by joint efforts under Article 147, where a party's contribution consisted solely in the care and maintenance of the family and household, her equal one-half share pertains only to the portion paid before the *de facto* separation, because after separation in fact there is no longer a family or household to care for and maintain, unless the post-separation amortizations were paid from the parties' common funds.[^5]

**Q2: Hypothetical: Assume that H and W, who had no legal impediment to marry each other, acquired a parcel of land during their marriage, which was later declared void *ab initio* under Article 36. Assume further that while they were still cohabiting prior to the termination of their union, H executed a Deed of Donation donating the parcel of land to his grandson without W's consent. May the grandson validly claim ownership over at least H's one-half undivided ideal share in the property by invoking Article 493 of the Civil Code on ordinary co-ownership?**

No. In [*Perez, Jr. v. Perez-Senerpida*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66975), the Supreme Court held that in a special co-ownership under Article 147, Article 493 of the Civil Code must yield to the express prohibition in the third paragraph of Article 147, which bars either party from encumbering or disposing by acts *inter vivos* of his or her share in property acquired during cohabitation and owned in common, without the consent of the other, until after the termination of their cohabitation.[^11] Consequently, H's unilateral *inter vivos* donation of the common property during cohabitation without W's consent is completely void and the rules on ordinary co-ownership cannot vest validity even on H's undivided share.[^11]

**Q3: Hypothetical: Assume that M and W, both capacitated to marry each other, lived together under a marriage later declared void under Article 36. During their cohabitation, W's aunt gratuitously donated a residential lot to W and W together with her father obtained a loan to construct a duplex house on the lot, with M signing the promissory note solely for marital consent and the Torrens title naming W as married to M. Assume it is proven that M was jobless, abused drugs, contributed no money or industry to the construction and failed to care for and maintain the family and household. Is M entitled to a one-half co-ownership share in the lot and duplex under Article 147?**

No. In [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), the Supreme Court held that the presumption of joint acquisition under the second paragraph of Article 147 applies only in the absence of proof to the contrary: if a piece of property was obtained solely through one party's effort, work or industry (or gratuitous donation and construction loan with her father) and there is proof that the other party did not contribute even through the care and maintenance of the family and household, the property is exclusively owned by the party who acquired it.[^19] Furthermore, the phrase describing W as married to M on the Transfer Certificate of Title is merely descriptive of W's civil status and does not make M a co-owner.[^19]

# Quotes

> "The trial court correctly applied the law. In a void marriage, regardless of the cause thereof, the property relations of the parties during the period of cohabitation is governed by the provisions of Article 147 or Article 148, such as the case may be, of the Family Code. Article 147 is a remake of Article 144 of the Civil Code as interpreted and so applied in previous cases; it provides:"[^2]

> "This peculiar kind of co-ownership applies when a man and a woman, suffering no legal impediment to marry each other, so exclusively live together as husband and wife under a void marriage or without the benefit of marriage. The term "capacitated" in the provision (in the first paragraph of the law) refers to the legal capacity of a party to contract marriage, i.e., any "male or female of the age of eighteen years or upwards not under any of the impediments mentioned in Articles 37 and 38" of the Code."[^2]

> "Under this property regime, property acquired by both spouses through their work and industry shall be governed by the rules on equal co-ownership. Any property acquired during the union is prima facie presumed to have been obtained through their joint efforts. A party who did not participate in the acquisition of the property shall still be considered as having contributed thereto jointly if said party's "efforts consisted in the care and maintenance of the family household." Unlike the conjugal partnership of gains, the fruits of the couple's separate property are not included in the co-ownership."[^2]

> "Under Article 148, only the properties acquired by both of the parties through their actual joint contribution of money, property or industry shall be owned by them in common in proportion to their respective contributions. It must be stressed that actual contribution is required by this provision, in contrast to Article 147 which states that efforts in the care and maintenance of the family and household, are regarded as contributions to the acquisition of common property by one who has no salary or income or work or industry. If the actual contribution of the party is not proved, there will be no co-ownership and no presumption of equal shares."[^9]

> "In contrast to Article 148, under the foregoing article, wages and salaries earned by either party during the cohabitation shall be owned by the parties in equal shares and will be divided equally between them, even if only one party earned the wages and the other did not contribute thereto. Conformably, even if the disputed "death benefits" were earned by the deceased alone as a government employee, Article 147 creates a co-ownership in respect thereto, entitling the petitioner to share one-half thereof. As there is no allegation of bad faith in the present case, both parties of the first marriage are presumed to be in good faith. Thus, one-half of the subject "death benefits" under scrutiny shall go to the petitioner as her share in the property regime, and the other half pertaining to the deceased shall pass by, intestate succession, to his legal heirs, namely, his children with Susan Nicdao."[^26]

> "As regards the settlement of the common properties of petitioner and respondent, we hold that the Civil Code provisions on co-ownership should apply. There is nothing in the records that support the pronouncement of the trial court that the parties have agreed to divide the properties into three--1/3 share each to the petitioner, the respondent and their children. Petitioner, in fact, alleges in her petition before this Court that the parties have agreed on a four-way division of the properties--1/4 share each to the petitioner and the respondent, and 1/4 share each to their two children. Moreover, respondent's argument that the three-way partition is in accordance with Articles 50 and 51 of the Family Code does not hold water as said provisions relate only to voidable marriages and exceptionally to void marriages under Article 40 of the Family Code, i.e., the declaration of nullity of a subsequent marriage contracted by a spouse of a prior void marriage before the latter is judicially declared void."[^14]

> "It is clear from Article 50 of the Family Code that Section 19(1) of the Rule applies only to marriages which are declared void ab initio or annulled by final judgment under Articles 40 and 45 of the Family Code. In short, Article 50 of the Family Code does not apply to marriages which are declared void ab initio under Article 36 of the Family Code, which should be declared void without waiting for the liquidation of the properties of the parties."[^15]

> "The Court does not agree. In the construction of the term "acquired," this Court must be guided by the basic rule in statutory construction that when the law does not distinguish, neither should the court. A reading of Article 147 of the Family Code would show that the provision did not make any distinction or make any qualification in terms of the manner the property must be acquired before the presumption of co-ownership shall apply. As such, the term "acquired" must be taken in its ordinary acceptation. For as long as the property had been purchased, whether on installment, financing or other mode of payment, during the period of cohabitation, the disputable presumption that they have been obtained by the parties' joint efforts, work or industry, and shall be owned by them in equal shares, shall arise. Applied in this case, since the Ayala Alabang and Rockwell properties were purchased while the petitioner and the respondent were living together, it is presumed that both parties contributed in their acquisition through their joint efforts (which includes one's efforts in the care and maintenance of the family and of the household), work or industry. Thus, the properties must be divided between them equally."[^5]

> "Given the above express prohibition of a party to the cohabitation to encumber or alienate by acts inter vivos even his or her share in the property acquired during the cohabitation and owned in common, without the consent of the other party until after the termination thereof under Article 147, then the donation of any property acquired during the cohabitation by one party without the consent of the other can only be but void. The rules on ordinary co-ownership cannot apply to vest validity on the undivided share of the disposing party. The donation is simply void."[^11]

> "However, if a piece of property was obtained through only one party's effort, work, or industry, and there is proof that the other did not contribute through the care and maintenance of the family and of the household, the property acquired during the cohabitation shall be solely owned by the party who actually worked to acquire the property."[^19]

> "Moreover, even if we apply Article 147 of the Family Code, as Lani insisted, the presumption of co-ownership is only prima facie, and shall only apply if there is no proof to the contrary. As here, the CA aptly observed that Lani cannot anchor her claim of co-ownership solely on Article 147's prima facie presumption because: (1) the title to the subject property, which was registered under Torrens system, is under Stephen's name alone; and (2) the Conditional Contract of Sale and Deed of Absolute Sale were executed between the seller and "STEPHEN PUA, of legal age, single, resident of Cauayan, Isabela." These are eloquent, self-speaking documentary evidence that the mortgaged property is Stephen's exclusive, separate property. As emphasized by the RTC and the CA, these documents remained uncontroverted. Lani did not prove that she contributed in any form to the mortgaged property's acquisition."[^36]

> "It is also settled that it is not necessary to liquidate the common properties of the spouses in the same proceeding for declaration of nullity of marriage under Article 36."[^25]

# Tips

## 1. Determining Whether Article 147 or Article 148 Applies

> [!TIP]
> **Verify Legal Capacity at the Time of Cohabitation (*Valdes*, *Cariño* and *Lavadia*):**
> Before invoking the equal-sharing presumption and domestic-care rule of Article 147, verify that both partners were at least 18 years old, free from incestuous or public-policy impediments under Articles 37 and 38 and not bound by any subsisting prior valid marriage during the cohabitation. If either partner had an undissolved prior marriage—including where a prior void marriage was not yet judicially declared void under Article 40 (*Cariño v. Cariño*), or where a foreign divorce between Filipino citizens was void under the nationality rule (*Lavadia v. Heirs of Luna*)—Article 148 governs instead, wiping out the presumption of co-ownership and requiring strict documentary proof of actual contribution of money, property or industry.

## 2. Litigating and Rebutting the *Prima Facie* Presumption of Equal Shares

> [!NOTE]
> **How to Establish Exclusive Ownership Under Article 147 (*Tan-Andal*, *Nayve-Pua* and *Uy v. Spouses Lacsamana*):**
> Merely showing that a Torrens title or tax declaration is registered in one partner's name or contains the descriptive phrase married to the other partner will not defeat the Article 147 co-ownership (*Salas, Jr. v. Aguila*, *Ocampo v. Ocampo* and *Abing v. Waeyan*). To successfully exclude an asset acquired during cohabitation from equal division under Article 147, a litigant must prove **both**:
> 1. **Exclusive source of acquisition:** That the property was acquired by gratuitous title (such as donation or inheritance) or purchased exclusively with one party's separate funds or personal loan, supported by contracts of sale, deeds naming the buyer as single, loan records or deeds where the other partner signed merely as an instrumental witness (*Tan-Andal v. Andal*, *Nayve-Pua v. Union Bank of the Philippines* and *Uy v. Spouses Lacsamana*); and
> 2. **Absence of domestic contribution:** That the non-acquiring partner did not contribute even to the care and maintenance of the family and of the household (*Tan-Andal v. Andal*).

> [!TIP]
> **Fix the Exact Date of *De Facto* Separation for Installment Properties (*Paterno v. Paterno*):**
> In Article 36 nullity cases where the spouses separated in fact years before the judicial decree of nullity, establish the exact date when exclusive cohabitation ended. Under *Paterno v. Paterno*, wages and properties acquired after *de facto* separation are excluded from the Article 147 co-ownership, and for properties bought on installment or bank financing during cohabitation, a homemaker spouse whose contribution consisted solely in caring for the family and household is entitled to a 50% share **only in the portion paid prior to the *de facto* separation**, unless post-separation amortizations were paid from common funds.

## 3. Nullifying Unilateral Dispositions, Waivers and Forum Shopping

> [!NOTE]
> **Challenge Unilateral *Inter Vivos* Transfers and Gratuitous Waivers (*Perez, Jr. v. Perez-Senerpida*):**
> When a partner in a void marriage or non-marital union unilaterally sells, donates or mortgages common property during cohabitation without the other partner's consent, do not concede validity as to the transferor's 1/2 undivided share under Article 493 of the Civil Code. Under *Perez, Jr. v. Perez-Senerpida*, the third paragraph of Article 147 overrides Article 493 of the Civil Code and renders the unilateral *inter vivos* disposition completely void. Likewise, any gratuitous waiver or renunciation of rights executed by one cohabitant in favor of the other during cohabitation should be attacked under Article 87 of the Family Code (not Article 89).

> [!TIP]
> **Procedural Strategy in Nullity and Partition Proceedings (*Diño*, *Tanyag* and *Diaz-Salgado*):**
> * **Immediate Issuance of Nullity Decree:** If a trial court conditions the issuance of a Decree of Absolute Nullity under Article 36 upon prior property liquidation under Section 19(1) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC or Articles 50 and 51, move for partial reconsideration citing *Diño v. Diño* and *Soto v. Reyes-Soto*.
> * **Avoid Splitting Causes of Action:** Do not file a separate civil action in another court to declare properties paraphernal or exclusive while an Article 36 nullity case is pending before a Family Court; under *Tanyag v. Tanyag*, the nullity court has incidental jurisdiction to determine the scope of the Article 147 co-ownership, and a separate suit will be dismissed for *litis pendentia* and forum shopping.
> * **Extrajudicial Partition Option:** Once cohabitation has terminated, former partners under Article 147 may execute a notarized extrajudicial partition agreement under Article 496 of the Civil Code without need of judicial approval (*Diaz-Salgado v. Anson*).
