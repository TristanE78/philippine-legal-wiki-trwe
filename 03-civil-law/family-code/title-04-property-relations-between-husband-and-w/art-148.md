> Art. 148. In cases of cohabitation not falling under the preceding Article, only the properties acquired by both of the parties through their actual joint contribution of money, property, or industry shall be owned by them in common in proportion to their respective contributions. In the absence of proof to the contrary, their contributions and corresponding shares are presumed to be equal. The same rule and presumption shall apply to joint deposits of money and evidences of credit.
>
> If one of the parties is validly married to another, his or her share in the co-ownership shall accrue to the absolute community or conjugal partnership existing in such valid marriage. If the party who acted in bad faith is not validly married to another, his or her shall be forfeited in the manner provided in the last paragraph of the preceding Article.
>
> The foregoing rules on forfeiture shall likewise apply even if both parties are in bad faith. (144a)[^family-code]

# Explanation

## Nature and scope of the residual property regime

Article 148 governs the property consequences of cohabitation outside Article 147. Its central concern is the acquisition of particular assets through actual joint contribution. It neither establishes a valid marriage nor creates an absolute community or conjugal partnership between the cohabitants. The statute identifies which acquisitions may be owned in common, measures each contributor's interest and then directs the disposition of that interest when an existing valid marriage or bad faith is involved.[^2]

The distinction is consequential. In [*Agapay v. Palang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006), the Supreme Court required proof that the claimant contributed to the purchase even though the riceland had been conveyed to Miguel and Erlinda and they had subsequently undergone a marriage ceremony. Miguel's earlier marriage remained subsisting despite de facto separation. Neither the ceremony nor the conveyance supplied the missing contribution.[^3] In [*Tumlos v. Spouses Fernandez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330), the Supreme Court likewise held that conjugally living together without legal capacity to marry does not establish co-ownership unless each party actually contributed to acquisition. The relationship explains why Article 148 applies. Evidence of contribution explains why a particular property interest exists.[^4]

The special regime must also be distinguished from a categorical denial of property rights to persons in an illicit relationship. Article 148 expressly recognizes limited co-ownership where its requisites are proved. The existence of an impediment does not itself defeat every claim. It removes the parties from Article 147's more favorable presumptions and requires proof suited to Article 148. Conversely, recognition of an intimate relationship cannot be used to bypass that requirement.[^5]

A useful order of analysis follows the statute. First identify the relationship and the parties' legal capacity. Next identify the property and prove the actual contributions to its acquisition. Determine the proportions or apply the limited presumption of equal shares where the contribution threshold is met. Only then consider whether a proven share accrues to an existing marital estate or is forfeited under the bad-faith provisions. Reversing this sequence risks distributing an interest that was never established.[^6] [^7]

## Capacity, exclusivity and the boundary with Article 147

Article 147 concerns a man and a woman legally capacitated to marry each other who live exclusively as husband and wife without marriage or under a void marriage. Legal capacity and exclusive cohabitation are separate requirements. Article 148 is the residual provision for cohabitation outside that class. In [*Valdes v. Regional Trial Court*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980), the Supreme Court explained that the applicable regime in a void marriage is Article 147 or Article 148 according to the circumstances. The mere judicial declaration of nullity does not settle the choice.[^8]

Psychological incapacity under Article 36 must not be confused with an impediment to contract marriage for this property-law inquiry. In [*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), Mario and Rosanna's marriage was void under Article 36, but they had been of sufficient age, were not shown to fall within the incest or public-policy prohibitions and had lived exclusively with each other. The Supreme Court applied Article 147. Its conclusions on equal wages and deemed household contribution therefore cannot be transferred to an Article 148 relationship.[^9]

The converse appears in the police-benefits dispute decided in [*Cariño v. Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043). The Supreme Court treated the earlier license-defective union of otherwise capacitated parties under Article 147 and the later bigamous union under Article 148. Both marriages were void, but their property classifications differed. The judgment requiring payment of PHP 73,000 to the later claimant was reversed and her complaint dismissed. This is a concrete warning against treating all persons who went through void ceremonies as holders of identical property rights.[^10]

Rows one to three rest on Article 147 and on the decisions cited above. Rows four to six apply the text of Articles 35, 37, 38 and 53. They are statutory readings and not holdings of the Supreme Court. The classification concerns the relevant cohabitation and acquisition, rather than the label a party places on the relationship.

| Relationship or defect | Classification and qualification |
|---|---|
| Otherwise capacitated parties living exclusively together without marriage | Article 147 applies. It expressly recognizes the household contribution described in its second paragraph. |
| An Article 36 void marriage between otherwise capacitated exclusive cohabitants | Article 147 applies, as illustrated by [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507). Psychological incapacity alone does not establish the legal impediment relevant to Article 148. |
| A subsisting valid marriage to another person | Article 148 governs the cohabitation outside that marriage. De facto separation does not remove the impediment. Article 35(4) must be read with the Article 41 exception. |
| A marriage contracted by a party below eighteen under Article 35(1) | The statutory legal-capacity requirement of Article 147 is absent. Article 148 supplies the residual acquisition rule for cohabitation outside Article 147. |
| Incestuous marriages under Article 37 or prohibited relationships under Article 38 | These statutory impediments exclude the legal capacity contemplated by Article 147. The residual rule requires actual joint contribution. |
| A subsequent marriage void under Article 53, read with Articles 35(6) and 52 | The subsequent ceremony does not establish a valid marital property regime. The prior marriage, compliance requirements and the precise basis of nullity must be examined before assigning property consequences. |

Articles 35(1), 37 and 38 establish limits on capacity. Article 35(4) addresses bigamous or polygamous marriages outside Article 41. Article 53 makes the subsequent marriage void if the former spouses remarry without compliance with the immediately preceding registration requirements. These provisions explain why nullity cannot be reduced to a single property category. The decisions cited here do not decide the allocation for every possible Article 53 combination. A conclusion about such a combination must preserve the distinct consequences attached to Articles 40 and 45 discussed in [*Diño v. Diño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33909), rather than treating every prior-marriage problem as interchangeable.[^11]

The Article 148 cases include bigamous marriages, adulterous relationships, concubinage, relationships in which both parties are married to others and multiple alliances of a married person. [*Benasa v. Mahor*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) describes these classes while retaining the actual-contribution requirement. Article 148 does not require that an invalid ceremony first be proved. In [*Go-Bangayan v. Bangayan, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55960), the parties' simulated marriage did not exist as a marriage, but their cohabitation while Benjamin remained married to Azucena was nevertheless governed by Article 148.[^12] [^13]

In [*Acre v. Yuttikki*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45344), the same classification was applied where the respondent's marriage to Sofronio occurred while he remained married to Beatriz. The Supreme Court stated the actual-contribution rule and denied the petition challenging the appellate judgment. That decision supports the classification of the bigamous union. It should not be read as a presumption in favor of the later partner.[^14]

## Cohabitation, employment abroad and the acquisition inquiry

Cohabitation is a factual relationship, not merely a shared description in documents. Article 148 concerns acquisitions by the parties through actual joint contribution in the relevant union. A claimant should therefore establish the domestic relationship, identify the acquisition period and connect the alleged contributions with the disputed assets. Proof of affection alone and proof of the acquisition of a particular property answer different questions.[^15] [^16]

[*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) is particularly instructive where employment takes a partner overseas. Benasa worked as a seafarer while Mahor remained married to another man. The trial and appellate courts treated their relationship as insufficient cohabitation. The Supreme Court reversed. It explained that no fixed period determines cohabitation and that the circumstances must be considered. His work required absence, but his intention to return to their shared dwelling, reciprocal letters, dated photographs and remittances supported the continuing relationship. He stayed in the Quezon City property when he returned from work abroad.[^17]

The decision does not make every overseas remittance proof of cohabitation or of equal ownership. The Supreme Court considered the evidence together and separately examined contribution to the real properties. It ordered accounting and further evaluation of ownership and shares. It refused to sustain the personal-property claim merely because Benasa identified objects in photographs. A relationship can be sufficiently established while proof concerning some assets remains deficient.[^18]

The acquisition inquiry also cannot be defeated by an unproved change in chronology. In [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006), Erlinda claimed that the riceland had been bought before cohabitation. The Supreme Court found that the precise commencement of cohabitation had not been established. It added that even if the purchase preceded cohabitation, a claim of co-ownership would still require proof of actual contribution. The hypothetical chronology therefore did not supply a substitute for proof of acquisition funding.[^19]

The period and the manner of acquisition must not be borrowed from Article 147 decisions without their qualifications. In [*Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47393), the Supreme Court found that the installment purchase of Suite 204 occurred during the parties' cohabitation and applied Article 147 because they were capacitated and lived exclusively together. That ruling illustrates Article 147's operation. It does not establish that a purchase made during any intimate relationship automatically becomes jointly owned under Article 148.[^20]

## Actual joint contribution and the burden of proof

The controlling threshold is actual joint contribution of money, property or industry. It is not enough that the property was acquired while the parties were together. Nor is it enough that the claimant could have earned or saved money. The claimant must establish a contribution to acquisition. In [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006), testimony about a buy-and-sell business and a sari-sari store did not persuade the Supreme Court that Erlinda actually contributed money to the riceland purchase. Earning activity and acquisition contribution were not treated as equivalent.[^21]

Money, property and industry are alternative forms of contribution expressly recognized by Article 148. The statute therefore does not limit cognizable contributions to cash payments. But the inclusion of industry does not dispense with proof that the activity contributed to acquisition. In [*Mallilin v. Castillo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37522), a claimant alleged that jointly managed business profits funded properties registered to the other partner. The Supreme Court identified Article 148 as the specific legal framework and remanded for proceedings on the merits. The remand did not establish that the alleged business contribution or a one-half interest had already been proved.[^22] [^23]

The distinction from Article 147 is deliberate in operation. Article 147 expressly deems care and maintenance of the family and household to be joint contribution by a party who did not participate in acquisition. Article 148 contains no corresponding deeming rule. The Supreme Court emphasized this difference in [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006) and applied it again in [*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330). Household care and moral or spiritual support therefore cannot, by themselves, satisfy Article 148's actual acquisition-contribution requirement. The discussion expressly identifying moral support in [*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330) occurs in the quoted appellate reasoning. The Supreme Court's own application rests on the absence of acquisition evidence and the insufficiency of the asserted administration.[^24] [^25]

Administration after acquisition must likewise be distinguished from contribution to acquisition. In [*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330), the assertion that the claimant administered the property was unsubstantiated. The Supreme Court further explained that administration by itself did not establish the claimed ownership because Article 148 does not equate management of an asset with contribution to its acquisition. This leaves the statutory recognition of industry intact while requiring a demonstrated connection between that industry and acquisition.[^26]

The burden lies on the party asserting the affirmative claim. [*Saguid v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) requires competent evidence and reliance on the strength of the claimant's own case. A deficient defense does not prove the claimant's alleged payments. This remains true when evidence is received ex parte. The absence of an opposing presentation does not entitle a claimant to everything demanded in the complaint. Favorable relief still requires proof that warrants it.[^27]

The same discipline applies when a claimant attributes the acquisition entirely to personal funds. In [*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293), Lupo Atienza was the president and general manager of two shipping corporations and Yolanda De Castro was their accountant. They lived together from 1983 while he was validly married to another woman. In 1992 he sued for partition of a Makati lot and house that Yolanda bought in 1987. He alleged that he paid for it with his exclusive funds and that the seller put the title in her name without his knowledge. The trial court declared the property co-owned in equal shares. The Court of Appeals reversed and declared Yolanda the exclusive owner.[^28] The Supreme Court denied Lupo's petition. It held that Article 148 governed because the parties could not marry each other and that proof of actual contribution was required. Lupo carried the burden of proof as plaintiff. He did not prove his own contribution. He attacked Yolanda's financial capacity and relied on bank records that the Supreme Court found irrelevant. The contract to sell, the deed of assignment of redemption and the deed of transfer ran between Yolanda and the vendor alone. Yolanda in turn proved that she earned her funds as an accountant and as a businesswoman in currency trading, money lending and jewelry retail. The proposition is evidentiary, not a rule that registration in one name makes contribution proof legally irrelevant.[^29]

The decisions distinguish several useful kinds of evidence. Receipts tied to construction materials can quantify a house contribution, as in [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645). Remittance records linked with the recipient's acknowledgments can support an acquisition claim, as in [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908). By contrast, a general assertion of earning capacity, identification of furniture in photographs or unsubstantiated administration does not alone establish the required connection. None of these examples creates an exclusive evidentiary checklist. They show the necessary movement from a relationship or financial possibility to a proved acquisition contribution.[^30] [^31] [^32]

## Proportional ownership and the limited equality presumption

Article 148 first makes common ownership proportional to the parties' respective contributions. Its presumption of equality addresses a different problem: actual joint contribution is established but the exact proportions cannot be determined. In [*Villanueva v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45770), involving Nicolas Retuya, Pacita Villanueva and the continuing marriage with Eusebia, the Supreme Court expressly required proof of actual joint contribution before the equal-share presumption could operate. A claimant cannot use inability to prove contribution as a reason to presume that contribution existed.[^33]

The facts of [*Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45770) show how the rule operates against a subsisting marriage. Nicolas Retuya married Eusebia in 1926 and began cohabiting with Pacita in 1936. Eusebia died in 1996 and Nicolas married Pacita that December. Eusebia had sued in 1988 for reconveyance of properties from Nicolas and Pacita. The disputed Lot 152 was bought in 1957 in Pacita's name, during the first marriage. The Supreme Court applied the presumption of conjugal ownership in Article 116 of the Family Code. A deed, title and tax declaration in Pacita's name did not overcome it. Proof that Pacita had the means to buy the lot did not prove that she paid for it with her own money. Long cohabitation did not sever the earlier marriage. Her Article 148 claim failed because she showed no actual contribution of her own.[^34]

The sequence can be stated as two separate questions. Did both parties actually contribute to acquisition of the property in question? If so, what proportions are established? An affirmative answer to the first question permits consideration of the statutory presumption when the evidence does not determine the second. A negative answer to the first means that Article 148 supplies neither co-ownership nor presumed equality. The Supreme Court reiterated the distinction in [*Vda. de Canada v. Baclot*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66514), where Sancho's cohabitation with Cresencia occurred during his subsisting marriage.[^35]

In [*Vda. de Canada*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66514), the Regional Trial Court ordered delivery of the disputed properties to the heirs of Sancho, largely on the testimony of his daughter and on the view that Cresencia lacked the means to acquire them. The Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the complaint. It identified nine properties, not eleven. It found that none was proved to belong to Sancho. All were registered in Cresencia's name except one in the name of Sanchito, the son of Cresencia and Sancho. The Supreme Court affirmed. It held that Article 148 required actual contribution, in contrast to Article 147, where care and maintenance of the family and household count as contribution. A certificate of title is not conclusive proof of ownership, but the claimant must still prove title as an owner. The lawful wife and her children had not shown that Sancho contributed. The Supreme Court rejected the assumption that Cresencia, a dressmaker, could not afford the properties.[^36]

[*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) demonstrates both branches within the same dispute. Gina alleged a PHP 70,000 contribution to completion of the house. The receipts in her name proved only PHP 11,413. The Supreme Court limited her interest in the house to that proven amount. It did not award half the house merely because the parties had cohabited or because she claimed a larger payment.[^37]

The personal properties in [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) were treated differently. Both parties had contributed to a joint deposit used to purchase them, but the exact amounts of their respective contributions were not sufficiently proved. The properties had an uncontested value of PHP 111,375. The Supreme Court presumed equal shares and fixed Gina's interest at PHP 55,687.50. Thus the same decision required a quantified contribution where receipts established it and used equality where actual joint funding was established but the proportions were not.[^38]

Article 148 expressly extends the same rule and presumption to joint deposits of money and evidences of credit. This extension retains the distinction between proof that both contributed and proof of the extent of each contribution. [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) is a concrete application involving a joint deposit and the personal properties purchased with it. The decisions cited here do not apply the extension to a particular negotiable instrument or other evidence of credit. The statutory extension should therefore not be enlarged into instrument-specific rules.[^39] [^40]

An award of equal shares in a different void-marriage case does not remove these limits. In [*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809), the Supreme Court sustained one-half of the specified retirement benefits and shares of stock on the basis of co-ownership rather than conjugal partnership. That decretal ruling does not establish automatic equal ownership of every salary, retirement benefit or financial asset under Article 148. The separate police-benefits decision in [*Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043) demonstrates why the governing relationship and the claimant's contribution still matter.[^41] [^42]

## Titles, separate assets and limits of ownership claims

Title and contribution must be analyzed together without making either conclusive beyond the decisions. A certificate in one partner's name does not invariably exclude an unnamed contributor. [*Atienza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293) expressly recognizes that possible co-ownership or trust interests are not foreclosed solely by issuance of a certificate to one person. The claim there nevertheless failed for want of sufficient acquisition evidence. [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) similarly permitted an accounting claim concerning real properties registered to Mahor where the remittance and acknowledgment evidence supported Benasa's contribution.[^43] [^44]

The reverse proposition is equally important. The appearance of both parties' names or a marital description does not automatically prove the contribution required by Article 148. [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645), citing the older *Adriano v. Court of Appeals*, explained that title in the names of persons in an adulterous relationship does not suffice without contribution evidence. This is a proposition from *Adriano*, as cited in [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645), rather than an independent reconstruction of the older case.[^45]

In [*Go-Bangayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55960), Benjamin Bangayan, Jr. was married to Azucena when Sally Go began living with him in February 1982. They signed a purported marriage contract on March 7, 1982 after Sally brought Benjamin to an office in Pasig. She assured him that it would not be registered. The Supreme Court held that no marriage existed because there was no license and no registration. It held that the parties lived together as husband and wife without the benefit of marriage and applied Article 148. Their relationship ended in 1994. The dispute then covered 44 registered properties.[^46]

[*Go-Bangayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55960) makes the title distinctions concrete. Of the seven remaining properties examined in the Supreme Court's ownership discussion, one was registered in Benjamin and Sally's names as spouses. Others were in one person's name followed by a description identifying that person as married to the other. Two were registered to Sally as a single individual. The Supreme Court explained that the descriptive married-to wording indicates civil status and does not prove co-ownership. It retained the requirement of actual contribution rather than treating the descriptive entry as an acquisition by both.[^47]

The same decision excluded the other 37 properties claimed by Sally because they represented properties conveyed by Benjamin's father to his children as advance inheritance. Such a source of title could not be transformed into jointly acquired property simply by placing the properties within the period of cohabitation. The relevant inquiry was the actual mode and source of acquisition.[^48]

The distribution that the Supreme Court affirmed shows how title and contribution interact. The two lots under TCT Nos. 61720 and 190860 belonged to Benjamin alone because he proved that he acquired them solely. The properties under TCT Nos. N-193656 and 253681 and CCT Nos. 8782 and 8783 belonged to Sally alone because Benjamin proved no contribution. Only the property under TCT No. 61722, registered to both as spouses, was owned in common in equal shares. Benjamin's half accrued to his conjugal partnership with Azucena under the second paragraph of Article 148. Sally's half remained with her because the Court of Appeals found no clear and convincing proof of bad faith. The trial court had found bad faith and had forfeited her share in two properties in favor of the children.[^49]

[*Joaquino v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45694) illustrates the protection of the existing valid marital estate. Rodolfo remained legally married to Lourdes while living with Milagros. The house and lot acquired in 1979 were registered in Milagros's name, but the evidence traced the financing to Rodolfo's salaries and a housing loan paid through those earnings and the related insurance proceeds. The Supreme Court sustained recovery for the lawful marital estate. Milagros had not substantiated actual payments of her own. Registration did not change the source or legal character of the funds.[^50]

The decision also explains why the absence of contribution cannot simply be cured by calling the transfer a gift. On the established facts, the registration amounted to a prohibited gratuitous transfer. The Supreme Court applied the donation prohibition and treated the registered holder as a constructive trustee for the persons deprived of ownership. Article 87 independently extends its prohibition on donations and gratuitous advantages to persons living as husband and wife without a valid marriage. A contribution analysis and a donation analysis concern different legal bases for acquisition. Failure under the former does not validate the latter.[^51] [^52]

The constitutional prohibition on alien landholding limits what any cohabiting partner may acquire. [*Borromeo v. Descallar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48948) involved Austrian Wilhelm Jambrich, his still-married Filipina partner Descallar and a later assignment to Filipino Borromeo. The Supreme Court found that Jambrich funded the purchases and that Descallar had not contributed. The Supreme Court described the relationship as adulterous because she was still married. It stated that no co-ownership exists in such a relationship and that each partner must prove actual contribution. Her name on the titles did not establish ownership through contribution. But proof that the alien supplied the price did not itself remove the constitutional landholding restriction.[^53]

The qualification in the actual judgment must be retained. The Supreme Court explained that Jambrich's acquisition would have been invalid if challenged before the later transfer to the qualified Filipino. It upheld the transfer to Borromeo because that subsequent conveyance cured the flaw and placed the land in qualified Filipino hands. The petition was granted and the trial judgment reinstated. Thus a claim of co-ownership cannot serve as a device for prohibited alien acquisition of land. [*Borromeo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48948) also cannot be reduced to a holding that an alien-funded acquisition makes every later qualified transfer ineffective. Nor does it hold that a foreign national is excluded from every kind of co-ownership or movable-property claim.[^54]

## Accretion to a valid marriage and forfeiture for bad faith

Once a contributor's share is established, the second paragraph of Article 148 determines its destination. If a party is validly married to another person, that party's share accrues to the absolute community or conjugal partnership existing in the valid marriage. The recipient is the applicable marital estate, rather than the married contributor as a freely disposable separate owner. This is distinct from a forfeiture in favor of the cohabitants' common children.[^55]

The rule presupposes a share proved under the acquisition provisions. It does not presume that every property held by an unmarried partner was partly bought by the married partner. Nor does it transfer the unmarried partner's proven share into the married partner's valid marital estate merely because the relationship was illicit. The amount attributable to each party must be established before the statutory disposition of that amount is applied. The actual-contribution holdings in [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006) and [*Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45770) prevent an unproved interest from being used as the starting point for accretion.[^56] [^57]

[*Joaquino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45694) presents an additional route to protection of the lawful marital estate. There the proof showed that the acquisition was funded by conjugal earnings and that the registered partner had not established an acquisition contribution. The result was not a compulsory half share for each cohabitant followed by transfer of one half to the lawful marriage. It was recognition of the property character shown by the funding evidence and recovery from the registered holder. Accretion cannot be used to reduce an already established conjugal asset to an arbitrary fraction.[^58]

The forfeiture clause addresses a party who acted in bad faith and is not validly married to another. Article 148 incorporates the manner of forfeiture in the final paragraph of Article 147. The share is forfeited in favor of the common children. On default or waiver by any or all of the common children or their descendants, each vacant share belongs to the respective surviving descendants. In the absence of descendants, the incorporated text directs the share to the innocent party. The forfeiture takes place upon termination of cohabitation.[^59]

Where neither cohabitant is validly married to another, there is no existing absolute community or conjugal partnership to receive an accruing share under Article 148's first disposition rule. The analysis turns instead on the bad-faith clause and its incorporated sequence of beneficiaries. Conversely, where a contributor is validly married elsewhere, the statutory accretion rule must be distinguished from the rule for a bad-faith contributor who is not validly married. The statute does not treat the two destinations as interchangeable sanctions.[^60]

The final paragraph expressly applies the forfeiture rules even when both parties are in bad faith. That provision must be read together with the incorporated order of beneficiaries. It does not create an innocent partner where neither is innocent. The decisions cited here do not resolve every terminal distribution problem where both parties are in bad faith and there are no qualified descendants. No residual recipient beyond the statutory wording should be invented to fill that gap.[^61]

The statutory forfeiture must also be separated from distribution of presumptive legitimes and from an unsupported agreement to allocate property to children. In the Fehr dispute, the Supreme Court rejected the purported three-way division because the alleged agreement was unsupported and Articles 50 and 51 did not justify the proposed allocation in that Article 147 case. Article 148's express incorporation of a forfeiture sequence supplies a different legal basis. Children do not acquire a fixed fraction of all property merely because the parents' union is void.[^62] [^63]

## Temporal application and protection of vested rights

Article 256 permits retroactive application of the Family Code only insofar as vested or acquired rights are not prejudiced or impaired. Accordingly, the fact that cohabitation or purchase began before the Family Code does not invariably defeat an Article 148 claim. But the later statute cannot be applied without addressing the express vested-right limitation.[^64]

[*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330) rejected the contention that Article 148 was unavailable merely because the cohabitation and acquisition preceded effectivity of the Family Code. The Supreme Court emphasized the absence of a demonstrated vested right that would be impaired. [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) likewise applied Article 148 to a relationship beginning in 1974 while retaining the qualification that vested rights remain unimpaired. The rule fills the earlier gap in regulation of property relations in adultery or concubinage, without erasing the statutory protection for acquired rights.[^65] [^66]

[*Vda. de Canada*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66514) restated the same qualification. The converse is reflected in [*Belcodero v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/oct1993/gr_89667_1993.html), where the Supreme Court affirmed a judgment recognizing the property as belonging to Alayo and Juliana's lawful conjugal partnership and said that the Family Code provisions could not be applied without impairing prior vested rights. These decisions must be read together. Retroactivity is neither categorically prohibited for older acquisitions nor unconditional whenever a later claimant invokes Article 148.[^67] [^68]

## Accounting, partition, administration and the scope of relief

Accounting can be necessary to identify what property was acquired with proven contributions and to determine the parties' shares. [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) did not end with an unexplained declaration of equal ownership. The Supreme Court ordered a complete accounting or inventory of money and properties entrusted from 1974 to 1999 and remanded for reception and evaluation of evidence concerning the disputed real properties. Its reasoning separated that justified real-property inquiry from the inadequately proved personal-property claim.[^69]

The difference between an interlocutory opportunity to prove ownership and a final determination of a share is material. The brokerage-property case [*Mallilin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37522) was remanded for merits proceedings. It should not be cited as though the alleged business contribution or the asserted one-half interest had already been adjudicated. Likewise, the letters-of-administration decision in [*San Luis v. San Luis*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40912) recognized that the claimant's capacity could arise from surviving-spouse status or a co-owner interest depending on proof of the divorce and marriage. It remanded for further proceedings rather than distributing a fixed share of the estate.[^70] [^71]

A proven interest may also lead to reimbursement or sale rather than continuing undivided possession. In [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645), the Supreme Court fixed Gina's house contribution at PHP 11,413 and her personal-property interest at PHP 55,687.50. It ordered reimbursement of PHP 67,100.50 and directed sale of the house at public auction if reimbursement was not made. The remedy followed the amounts proved and the particular decretal terms. The judgment should not be enlarged into a decision on the rights of Gina's lawful husband, which the extracted disposition did not adjudicate.[^72]

The relationship between partition and nullity must be kept equally precise. [*Valdes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980) affirmed application of Civil Code co-ownership rules to the properties in the Article 147 setting before it. [*Diño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33909) held that the decree in an Article 36 case need not await liquidation, partition and distribution. Its reasoning distinguished the Article 50 and procedural-rule consequences for marriages addressed under Articles 40 and 45. Those distinctions prevent conversion of a rule about an Article 36 decree into a universal rule governing every void subsequent marriage.[^73] [^74]

[*Soto v. Reyes-Soto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70372) reaffirmed the point in 2026. The Supreme Court found Article 147 applicable and declined to compel partition as a precondition to nullity. The parties could pursue partition by agreement or judicial proceedings. It affirmed the appellate judgment and denied the petition. [*Soto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70372) is therefore relevant to the general co-ownership framework and the timing of relief, but its own facts are not an instance of Article 148 cohabitation.[^75]

Other consequences require an independent basis. [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) specifically awarded PHP 100,000 as moral and exemplary damages and PHP 10,000 as attorney's fees. [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) sustained deletion of the moral-damages award for lack of basis. The retirement-benefits judgment in [*Buenaventura*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809) also deleted moral and exemplary damages, fees and litigation expenses while sustaining specified co-ownership awards. These outcomes do not support treating damages or fees as automatic incidents of an Article 148 dispute.[^76] [^77] [^78]

Finally, a property-recovery action does not necessarily settle filiation and heirship. [*Joaquino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45694) rejected an attempt to obtain a ruling on the children's status and hereditary claims in the ordinary recovery case where those matters were not properly put in issue. Article 148's reference to common children as forfeiture beneficiaries does not eliminate the need for a procedurally proper determination of the rights actually asserted. The scope of the judgment must remain tied to the issues pleaded and decided.[^79]

## Related Provisions

- [Article 35](../title-01-marriage/art-035.md), [Article 37](../title-01-marriage/art-037.md) and [Article 38](../title-01-marriage/art-038.md) identify relevant void marriages and impediments. [Article 36](../title-01-marriage/art-036.md) requires a separate analysis of legal capacity for property classification.
- [Article 40](../title-01-marriage/art-040.md), [Article 52](../title-01-marriage/art-052.md) and [Article 53](../title-01-marriage/art-053.md) concern prior-marriage nullity, registration and subsequent marriage. They must be read with [Article 50](../title-01-marriage/art-050.md) and [Article 51](../title-01-marriage/art-051.md) when distinguishing the consequences discussed in [*Diño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33909).
- [Article 87](art-087.md) prohibits specified donations and gratuitous advantages, including between persons living as husband and wife without a valid marriage.

Article 256 supplies the vested-right limitation on retroactivity. The Civil Code co-ownership provisions discussed in [*Valdes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980), [*Diño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33909) and [*Soto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70372) govern partition in their respective settings. The constitutional landholding limitation is addressed here only through the decision in [*Borromeo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48948). These cross-references do not add holdings beyond those of the decisions cited on this page.[^80] [^81]

## Key Cases

- **[*Belcodero v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/oct1993/gr_89667_1993.html)** (G.R. No. 89667, October 20, 1993): Alayo Bosing left his lawful wife Juliana in 1946 and lived with Josefa. A lot he agreed to buy in 1949 was titled in Josefa's name in 1959 at his request. The Supreme Court affirmed the appealed decision and agreed that the lot belonged to the conjugal partnership of Alayo and Juliana. It added that Articles 147 and 148 could not be applied without impairing prior vested rights.[^82]
- **[*Valdes v. Regional Trial Court*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33980)** (G.R. No. 122749, July 31, 1996): The marriage of Antonio Valdes and Consuelo Gomez was declared void under Article 36. The parties then disputed the liquidation of their property. The Supreme Court explained that a void marriage falls under Article 147 or Article 148 according to the parties' capacity and exclusivity. It held that the trial court could settle these incidental matters and affirmed the clarified orders of May 5 and October 30, 1995.[^83]
- **[*Agapay v. Palang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006)** (G.R. No. 116668, July 28, 1997): Miguel Palang and Erlinda Agapay bought a riceland in both names on May 17, 1973. They went through a marriage ceremony on July 15, 1973 while Miguel's first marriage still subsisted. Erlinda testified about a buy-and-sell business and a sari-sari store. The Supreme Court denied her petition because she did not prove that she paid for the land. It held that without actual contribution there is neither co-ownership nor a presumption of equal shares.[^84]
- **[*Tumlos v. Spouses Fernandez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330)** (G.R. No. 137650, April 12, 2000): The Fernandez spouses sued to eject Guillerma Tumlos from the premises. She claimed co-ownership because of her relationship with Mario Fernandez, who was married to Lourdes. The Supreme Court denied her petition. It held that cohabitation and unsubstantiated administration do not prove contribution to acquisition. It also held that Article 148 may reach earlier cohabitation when no vested right is impaired.[^85]
- **[*Mallilin v. Castillo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37522)** (G.R. No. 136803, June 16, 2000): Eustaquio Mallilin, Jr. claimed that properties registered in the name of Ma. Elvira Castillo were bought with profits of their customs brokerage business. He invoked an implied trust under Article 1452 of the Civil Code. The Supreme Court held that Article 148 specifically governed their relationship. It reversed the amended Court of Appeals decision and remanded the case for proceedings on the merits.[^86]
- **[*Cariño v. Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043)** (G.R. No. 132529, February 2, 2001): SPO4 Santiago Cariño married Susan Nicdao in 1969 without a marriage license. He later married Susan Yee while the first marriage subsisted. After his death, Susan Yee sued for half of his death benefits. The Supreme Court held that Article 147 governed the first union and Article 148 governed the second. It reversed the lower courts and dismissed her complaint.[^87]
- **[*Saguid v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645)** (G.R. No. 150611, June 10, 2003): Gina Rey was validly married to another man when she lived with Jacinto Saguid. She claimed a P70,000 contribution to their house, but receipts proved only P11,413. Both had funded a joint account that bought personal properties worth P111,375. Their exact shares were not proved. The Supreme Court declared her co-owner of the house to the extent of P11,413 and of the personal properties to the extent of P55,687.50. It ordered reimbursement of P67,100.50 or sale of the house at public auction.[^88]
- **[*Mercado-Fehr v. Fehr*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47393)** (G.R. No. 152716, October 23, 2003): Elna Mercado-Fehr and Bruno Fehr bought Suite 204 of LCG Condominium on installment in 1983 and married in 1985. Their marriage was later declared void for psychological incapacity. The Supreme Court held that the unit was acquired during their cohabitation and was common property under Article 147. It rejected a three-way division with the children because the record did not support it. It remanded the case to the Regional Trial Court of Makati for liquidation under the rules on co-ownership.[^89]
- **[*Villanueva v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45770)** (G.R. No. 143286, April 14, 2004): Eusebia Retuya sued her husband Nicolas, Pacita Villanueva and their son Procopio to recover properties. The Supreme Court held that long cohabitation does not sever a subsisting marriage. It held that property acquired during that marriage is presumed conjugal. It rejected the Article 148 claim because Pacita's actual contribution was not proved. The disputed lot had been bought in 1957 in her name, during the first marriage. It denied the petition.[^90]
- **[*Joaquino v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45694)** (G.R. No. 154645, July 13, 2004): Rodolfo Reyes stayed married to Lourdes while living with Milagros. A house and lot bought in 1979 was registered in Milagros's name. Rodolfo's salaries, a housing loan and insurance proceeds paid for it. The Supreme Court upheld recovery for the lawful marital estate. It held that the transfer was a prohibited donation and that Milagros held the property as constructive trustee. It also held that filiation and succession could not be decided in the recovery case.[^91]
- **[*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809)** (G.R. Nos. 127358 & 127449, March 31, 2005): Noel Buenaventura sought nullity of his marriage to Isabel Singh Buenaventura for her psychological incapacity. The Supreme Court sustained her award of half of his retirement benefits and shares of stock. It sustained the award on the basis of co-ownership and not conjugal partnership. It deleted the moral and exemplary damages, attorney's fees and litigation expenses. The decision should not be read as a rule of automatic equal shares under Article 148.[^92]
- **[*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293)** (G.R. No. 169698, November 29, 2006): Lupo Atienza was validly married to another woman when he lived with Yolanda De Castro. He claimed co-ownership of a Makati lot and house that she bought in 1987 and argued that she lacked the means to buy it. The trial court granted equal shares and the Court of Appeals reversed. The Supreme Court denied his petition. He did not prove his contribution and the purchase documents favored her. She proved earnings as an accountant and a businesswoman. It noted that a title in one name does not by itself rule out co-ownership or a trust.[^93]
- **[*San Luis v. San Luis*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40912)** (G.R. No. 133743, February 6, 2007): Felicidad San Luis sought letters of administration of Felicisimo's estate. The petitioners challenged her capacity to file. The Supreme Court held that her capacity could arise as surviving wife or as co-owner under Article 148, depending on proof of the divorce and the later marriage. It denied the petition and remanded the case for further proceedings.[^94]
- **[*Acre v. Yuttikki*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45344)** (G.R. No. 153029, September 27, 2007): Evangeline Yuttikki married Sofronio Acre while he was still married to Beatriz. Beatriz and the other petitioners sued to recover properties. They alleged that Sofronio alone acquired them. The Supreme Court held that the bigamous union fell under Article 148 and that co-ownership needs actual joint contribution. It denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^95]
- **[*Borromeo v. Descallar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48948)** (G.R. No. 159310, February 24, 2009): Wilhelm Jambrich, an Austrian, paid for three lots and a house titled in the name of Antonietta Descallar, who was still married. He later assigned his rights to a Filipino, Borromeo. The Supreme Court found that Descallar did not contribute. It held that the ban on alien landholding did not defeat the transfer because the land had passed to a qualified Filipino. It reinstated the trial court judgment for Borromeo.[^96]
- **[*Diño v. Diño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33909)** (G.R. No. 178044, January 19, 2011): The trial court declared a marriage void under Article 36. It ordered that the decree issue only after liquidation, partition and distribution of the property. The Supreme Court held that Article 50 and Section 19(1) of the Rule apply only to marriages declared void under Articles 40 and 45. It held that they do not apply to a marriage void under Article 36. It modified the decision so that the decree issues upon finality.[^97]
- **[*Go-Bangayan v. Bangayan, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55960)** (G.R. No. 201061, July 3, 2013): Benjamin Bangayan, Jr. was married to Azucena when he began living with Sally Go in 1982. Their simulated marriage of March 7, 1982 was held non-existent. The Supreme Court held that Article 148 governed. It excluded 37 properties that came from Benjamin's father as advance inheritance. Of the seven other properties, it affirmed a distribution of two to Benjamin, four to Sally and one to both in equal shares, with Benjamin's half accruing to his conjugal partnership. It held that "married to" on a title describes civil status and does not prove contribution. It affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^98]
- **[*Vda. de Canada v. Baclot*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66514)** (G.R. No. 221874, July 7, 2020): Sancho Canada lived with Cresencia Baclot while his marriage to the petitioner subsisted. The trial court had ordered delivery of the properties to Sancho's heirs. The Court of Appeals reversed and found that none of nine disputed properties was proved to belong to Sancho. All were registered in Cresencia's name except one in the name of Sanchito. The Supreme Court denied the petition and rejected the argument that Cresencia, a dressmaker, lacked the means to buy them. It held that Article 148 applied and that actual contribution must be proved before equality is presumed. It restated that retroactive application is allowed if vested rights remain unimpaired.[^99]
- **[*Tan-Andal v. Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507)** (G.R. No. 196359, May 11, 2021): The marriage of Mario and Rosanna was void for psychological incapacity. Both were capacitated to marry and lived exclusively together until 2000. The Supreme Court held that Article 147 governed their property relations. It reinstated the trial court decision. The case marks the boundary with Article 148.[^100]
- **[*Benasa v. Mahor*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908)** (G.R. No. 236659, August 31, 2022): Bernard Benasa, a seafarer, lived with Presentacion Mahor from 1974 to 1999 while she remained married to another man. The Supreme Court held that cohabitation has no fixed duration and that his absences abroad did not defeat it. It remanded the case for an accounting and for evaluation of ownership of the real properties. It rejected his personal-property claim. It awarded P100,000 as moral and exemplary damages and P10,000 as attorney's fees.[^101]
- **[*Soto v. Reyes-Soto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70372)** (G.R. No. 249759, April 22, 2026): Soto challenged the judgment declaring his marriage to Cassandra Reyes-Soto void under Article 36. The issue was whether partition must come first. The Supreme Court held that Article 147 applied and that partition is incidental to nullity. The parties may partition by agreement or in a judicial proceeding. It denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^102]

## Suggested Answers

A woman cohabits with a man who remains validly married to another. A riceland is conveyed to both of them, but her testimony that she operated a small business does not establish that she paid any part of its price. May she claim half because the deed names her and the man later went through a marriage ceremony with her? No. Following [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006), Article 148 requires actual contribution to acquisition. The conveyance and invalid ceremony do not supply it. If actual contribution is not proved, there is neither Article 148 co-ownership nor a presumption of equal shares.[^103]

A still-married woman and her cohabiting partner dispute their house and personal properties. She alleges PHP 70,000 toward construction, but receipts establish only PHP 11,413. Both contributed to a joint account used to buy personalty worth PHP 111,375, although their exact deposits cannot be determined. What amounts are supported? On the facts of [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645), her proved house contribution is PHP 11,413. The established joint funding permits equal shares in the personalty, or PHP 55,687.50 each. The Supreme Court ordered reimbursement of PHP 67,100.50, with sale of the house if unpaid. That result demonstrates the difference between an unproved contribution and an unproved proportion. It does not decide an unlitigated claim of the woman's lawful husband.[^104]

An Austrian funds land and a house registered to his still-married Filipina partner, who proves no purchase contribution. He later assigns his rights to a qualified Filipino. Does the Filipina necessarily own everything because the alien could not acquire Philippine land? No. Under the particular facts of [*Borromeo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48948), registration alone did not establish her contribution or ownership. The alien's acquisition was constitutionally defective, but the subsequent conveyance to a qualified Filipino cured the flaw and the Supreme Court sustained that transferee's claim. A co-ownership claim cannot itself authorize prohibited alien landholding. In that decision the later qualified transfer decided the outcome.[^105]

# Quotes

**Actual contribution precedes any presumption**

> "Under Article 148, only the properties acquired by both of the parties through their actual joint contribution of money, property or industry shall be owned by them in common in proportion to their respective contributions. It must be stressed that actual contribution is required by this provision, in contrast to Article 147 which states that efforts in the care and maintenance of the family and household, are regarded as contributions to the acquisition of common property by one who has no salary or income or work or industry. If the actual contribution of the party is not proved, there will be no co-ownership and no presumption of equal shares."[^106]

**Cohabitation alone does not establish ownership**

> "Under Article 148 of the Family Code, a man and a woman who are not legally capacitated to marry each other, but who nonetheless live together conjugally, may be deemed co-owners of a property acquired during the cohabitation only upon proof that each made an actual contribution to its acquisition. Hence, mere cohabitation without proof of contribution will not result in a co-ownership."[^107]

**Administration is distinct from acquisition**

> "Likewise, her claim of having administered the property during the cohabitation is unsubstantiated. In any event, this fact by itself does not justify her claim, for nothing in Article 148 of the Family Code provides that the administration of the property amounts to a contribution in its acquisition."[^108]

**The claimant must prove the case even on ex parte evidence**

> "As in other civil cases, the burden of proof rests upon the party who, as determined by the pleadings or the nature of the case, asserts an affirmative issue. Contentions must be proved by competent evidence and reliance must be had on the strength of the party's own evidence and not upon the weakness of the opponent's defense. This applies with more vigor where, as in the instant case, the plaintiff was allowed to present evidence ex parte. The plaintiff is not automatically entitled to the relief prayed for. The law gives the defendant some measure of protection as the plaintiff must still prove the allegations in the complaint. Favorable relief can be granted only after the court is convinced that the facts proven by the plaintiff warrant such relief. Indeed, the party alleging a fact has the burden of proving it and a mere allegation is not evidence."[^109]

**A claimed contribution is limited by the receipts**

> "In the case at bar, the controversy centers on the house and personal properties of the parties. Private respondent alleged in her complaint that she contributed P70,000.00 for the completion of their house. However, nowhere in her testimony did she specify the extent of her contribution. What appears in the record are receipts in her name for the purchase of construction materials on November 17, 1995 and December 23, 1995, in the total amount of P11,413.00."[^110]

**Equal shares follow proof of an actual joint deposit**

> "On the other hand, both parties claim that the money used to purchase the disputed personal properties came partly from their joint account with First Allied Development Bank. While there is no question that both parties contributed in their joint account deposit, there is, however, no sufficient proof of the exact amount of their respective shares therein. Pursuant to Article 148 of the Family Code, in the absence of proof of extent of the parties' respective contribution, their share shall be presumed to be equal. Here, the disputed personal properties were valued at P111,375.00, the existence and value of which were not questioned by the petitioner. Hence, their share therein is equivalent to one-half, i.e., P55,687.50 each."[^111]

**Cohabitation has no fixed statutory duration**

> "Thus, it would appear that cohabitation would require a significant period of staying together before a man and a woman may be said to be in cohabitation. Nonetheless, the law does not fix a period to determine this. Rather, the circumstances of the case must be taken into consideration, especially when it pertains to the civil law concept of cohabitation being referred to under Article 148 of the Family Code, by which contributions to the property regime are sought to be determined."[^112]

**Overseas work and the intention to return**

> "In this case, petitioner is a seafarer and has maintained his relationship with respondent as such. In the course of his employment, petitioner has to perform his work overseas and on such duration of contracts that will not allow him to stay in a dwelling for which he may spend a longer period of time with respondent. Under such situation, it becomes important to also take into consideration the intent of petitioner to return to the place where he considers as his residence or dwelling with respondent. As it would appear, this intent was manifested through his actions."[^113]

**Identification of personal property is not proof of purchase**

> "However, this Court cannot sustain petitioner's claim on the personal properties located in the property in Quezon City. The inventory and photos attached by petitioner encircling these, and claiming to be his, were self-serving and inadequate, for these only identified the property, but did not establish that these were actually purchased by him and not by respondent. It should be emphasized that petitioner's insistence that respondent was unemployed during the period of their illicit relationship and could not afford the subject properties as alleged by petitioner, is a patronizing assumption unsupported by evidence."[^114]

**Accounting and further evidence determine the actual shares**

> "Further, as this Court is not a trier of facts, this case must be remanded to the RTC for the accounting, reception of evidence, and evaluation thereof for the proper determination of the ownership and share of the parties in the properties mentioned above, which include the properties located in Quezon City, Tagaytay City, and Baliuag, Bulacan, covered by TCT Nos. N-223267, T-17493, and T-306188 based on Article 148 of the Family Code."[^115]

**Descriptive civil status on a title does not prove contribution**

> "As regards the seven remaining properties, we rule that the decision of the Court of Appeals is more in accord with the evidence on record. Only the property covered by TCT No. 61722 was registered in the names of Benjamin and Sally as spouses. The properties under TCT Nos. 61720 and 190860 were in the name of Benjamin with the descriptive title “married to Sally.” The property covered by CCT Nos. 8782 and 8783 were registered in the name of Sally with the descriptive title “married to Benjamin” while the properties under TCT Nos. N-193656 and 253681 were registered in the name of Sally as a single individual. We have ruled that the words “married to” preceding the name of a spouse are merely descriptive of the civil status of the registered owner. Such words do not prove co-ownership. Without proof of actual contribution from either or both spouses, there can be no co-ownership under Article 148 of the Family Code."[^116]

**A title in one name does not by itself exclude co-ownership**

> "True, the mere issuance of a certificate of title in the name of any person does not foreclose the possibility that the real property covered thereby may be under co-ownership with persons not named in the certificate or that the registrant may only be a trustee or that other parties may have acquired interest subsequent to the issuance of the certificate of title."[^117]

**Equal shares presuppose proof of actual joint contribution**

> "The presumption of equality of contribution arises only in the absence of proof of their proportionate contributions, subject to the condition that actual joint contribution is proven first. Simply put, proof of actual contribution by both parties is required, otherwise there is no co-ownership and no presumption of equal sharing."[^118]

**Proof of contribution is the threshold requirement**

> "Simply put, the ownership of the properties jointly acquired by the parties who are cohabiting under the circumstances provided is relative to their respective contributions, requiring actual proof. In the absence of proof of their quantifiable actual contribution, their contributions are deemed equal. However, if proof of actual contribution per se was not shown, co-ownership will not arise."[^119]

**The constitutional limitation on alien landholding**

> "The capacity to acquire private land is dependent on the capacity "to acquire or hold lands of the public domain." Private land may be transferred only to individuals or entities "qualified to acquire or hold lands of the public domain." Only Filipino citizens or corporations at least 60% of the capital of which is owned by Filipinos are qualified to acquire or hold lands of the public domain. Thus, as the rule now stands, the fundamental law explicitly prohibits non-Filipinos from acquiring or holding title to private lands, except only by way of legal succession or if the acquisition was made by a former natural-born citizen."[^120]

**An Article 36 void marriage can remain within Article 147**

> "Here, Mario and Rosanna are parties to a void marriage due to psychological incapacity. When they were married in 1995, Mario was 33 years old while Rosanna was 31. There is no showing that the marriage was incestuous or void due to public policy. They likewise lived exclusively with each other as husband and wife until they separated in 2000. Being capacitated to marry each other and having lived exclusively with each other albeit under a void marriage, Article 147 of the Family Code governs their property relations."[^121]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Quick Diagnostic Checklist for Article 148 Claims.** Work through these questions in order before you assign any share:
> 1. **Who are the parties and what is their legal capacity?** Identify any existing valid marriage. A subsisting marriage to another person puts the union under Article 148, as in [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006), [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645), [*Atienza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293) and [*Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45770).
> 2. **Is Article 147 the right regime instead?** Capacitated partners who lived exclusively together stay under Article 147, even in a void marriage. See [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507), [*Mercado-Fehr*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47393) and the first union in [*Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043).
> 3. **Which property is claimed and when was it acquired?** Examine each asset separately. [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) reached different results for the house and the personal properties.
> 4. **What did each party actually contribute to the acquisition?** Money, property or industry must be traced to the asset. [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006), [*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330) and [*Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45770) reject cohabitation alone.
> 5. **Are the proportions proved?** If both contributed but the amounts are unknown, equality is presumed. If one contribution is not proved, there is no co-ownership.
> 6. **Where does each proved share go?** A validly married contributor's share accrues to the marital estate. A bad-faith contributor who is not validly married forfeits the share as Article 147 provides.
> 7. **Does the claim touch an older acquisition?** Article 148 may apply, but vested rights must stay unimpaired.
> 8. **Does the requested relief match the judgment actually rendered?** An accounting remand is not a final award of one half.

### Choosing the Regime

> [!NOTE]
> **Capacity and exclusivity decide the regime.** Article 147 asks whether the partners were capacitated to marry each other and lived exclusively together. Article 148 covers cohabitation outside that class. [*Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043) applied both articles in one case. The first union, void for lack of a marriage license, fell under Article 147. The later bigamous union fell under Article 148. Do not treat every person who went through a void ceremony as holding the same property rights.

> [!TIP]
> **Do not import Article 147 rules into Article 148.** Article 147 deems care and maintenance of the household a joint contribution and gives equal shares in wages and salaries. Article 148 has no such rule. [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006) and [*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330) refused to count moral support or household care as a contribution to acquisition. [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) applied the equal-wages and household-care rules only because Article 147 governed.

### Proving Actual Contribution

> [!TIP]
> **Evidence that worked and evidence that did not.** Compare the decisions before you advise a claimant.
> - **Worked:** Receipts for construction materials fixed a house contribution at P11,413 in [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645). Remittance slips, passbooks, letters and a photograph marked "bought from my allotment" supported the real-property claim in [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908).
> - **Did not work:** Testimony about a buy-and-sell business and a sari-sari store in [*Agapay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35006). A claim that the other party lacked the means to buy in [*Atienza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293). An unsubstantiated claim of administration in [*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330). An inventory and photographs that only identified furniture in [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908).
> - **Lesson:** Earning capacity and affection are not proof of payment. The claimant must link money, property or industry to the particular asset.

> [!NOTE]
> **The claimant carries the burden even on ex parte evidence.** [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) stressed that a plaintiff who presents evidence ex parte is not automatically entitled to everything prayed for. The claimant must rely on the strength of the claimant's own evidence and not on the weakness of the defense. [*Atienza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293) and [*San Luis*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40912) repeat the same burden.

> [!TIP]
> **Read the title with care.** Title in one name does not always exclude another contributor. [*Atienza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293) said a certificate in one person's name does not foreclose co-ownership or a trust. Title does not prove contribution either. [*Go-Bangayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55960) held that "married to" on a title only describes civil status. Always trace the source of the funds behind the title. [*Joaquino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45694) did so and traced the price to the married partner's salaries and a loan.

### Shares, Accretion and Forfeiture

> [!NOTE]
> **Two questions, two answers.** First ask whether both parties actually contributed. If the answer is no, Article 148 gives neither co-ownership nor presumed equality. If the answer is yes, ask whether the proportions are proved. [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) shows both branches. Proved receipts fixed the house share. An unquantified joint deposit gave equal shares in the personal properties at P55,687.50 each.

> [!TIP]
> **Accretion and forfeiture have different destinations.** If a contributor is validly married to another, the proved share accrues to the absolute community or conjugal partnership of that marriage. If a bad-faith contributor is not validly married to another, the share is forfeited as Article 147 provides. That order favors the common children, then their descendants and then the innocent party. The same forfeiture rules apply when both parties are in bad faith. The decisions cited here do not say who receives the share when no one qualifies. Do not invent a recipient.

### Older Acquisitions and Remedies

> [!NOTE]
> **Retroactivity is allowed but limited.** [*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330) applied Article 148 to earlier cohabitation because the claimant showed no vested right. [*Atienza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293) and [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) applied it to cohabitation that began before the Family Code, since Article 148 filled the gap left by Article 144 of the Civil Code. [*Belcodero*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/oct1993/gr_89667_1993.html) went the other way and refused to apply Articles 147 and 148 where prior vested rights of the lawful conjugal partnership would be impaired.

> [!TIP]
> **Match the remedy to the judgment.** [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) ordered an accounting and a remand for the real properties. It did not award half of everything. [*Mallilin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37522) was remanded for trial on the merits and did not prove the claimed business contribution. [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) ordered reimbursement of P67,100.50 or sale of the house at public auction. [*Diño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33909) and [*Soto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70372) held that partition need not precede the decree of nullity in the Article 147 setting. Damages and attorney's fees need their own basis. [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908) awarded them. [*Saguid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47645) upheld the deletion of moral damages.

### Common Traps

> [!NOTE]
> **Five errors to avoid in problems and pleadings.**
> 1. Assuming that cohabitation alone creates co-ownership. [*Tumlos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54330) and [*Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45770) reject this.
> 2. Treating long cohabitation as ending a prior valid marriage. [*Villanueva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45770) held that property acquired during that marriage is presumed conjugal.
> 3. Reading a remand as a win on the merits. See [*Mallilin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37522) and [*Benasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68908).
> 4. Using an Article 147 outcome as authority for Article 148. [*Mercado-Fehr*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47393), [*Tan-Andal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68507) and the first union in [*Cariño*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50043) concern Article 147.
> 5. Using [*Buenaventura*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809) as a rule of automatic equal shares. It sustained co-ownership awards in its own setting.

> [!NOTE]
> **Landholding capacity is a separate question.** [*Borromeo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48948) did not make an alien acquisition valid merely because the alien supplied the price. Its result depended on the later conveyance to a qualified Filipino. Keep that fact when you use the decision.
