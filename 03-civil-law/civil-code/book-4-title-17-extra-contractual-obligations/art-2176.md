> ART. 2176. Whoever by act or omission causes damage to another, there being fault or negligence, is obliged to pay for the damage done. Such fault or negligence, if there is no pre-existing contractual relation between the parties, is called a quasi-delict and is governed by the provisions of this Chapter. (1902a)[^civil-code]

# Explanation

## Concept and Requisites of a Quasi-Delict

Article 2176 defines a quasi-delict or *culpa aquiliana* as an independent source of obligation under Article 1157(5) of the Civil Code.[^civil-code] [^2] It obliges any person who causes damage to another through an act or omission attended by fault or negligence to pay for the damage done when no pre-existing contractual relation governs the transaction.[^civil-code] [^3] Responsibility under Article 2176 is entirely separate and distinct from the civil liability arising from criminal negligence under the Revised Penal Code, subject to the prohibition in Article 2177 against recovering damages twice for the same act or omission.[^civil-code] Under Article 2178, the general rules in Articles 1172 to 1174 on court-regulated negligence liability, the standard of diligence of a good father of a family and fortuitous events also apply to a quasi-delict.[^civil-code]

Supreme Court decisions formulate the requisites of a quasi-delict as either three or five elements. In *Miguel Kim v. Slimmers World International* and *Mark Erquiza Laza v. Standard Insurance Co., Inc.*, the Supreme Court stated three requisites:

1. damage suffered by the plaintiff;
2. an act or omission of the defendant constituting fault or negligence; and
3. a causal connection between the act or omission and the damage sustained, which serves as the proximate cause.[^4] [^5]

In *St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corporation*, the Supreme Court listed five elements:

1. an act or omission;
2. the presence of fault or negligence in the performance or non-performance of the act;
3. injury;
4. a causal connection between the negligent act and the injury; and
5. no pre-existing contractual relation.[^6]

The first sentence of Article 2176 is substantially derived from Article 1902 of the 1889 Spanish Civil Code.[^7] In *Caravan Travel and Tours International, Inc. v. Abejar*, the Supreme Court explained that the provision stresses the passive subject of the obligation and does not restrict the active subjects who may sue.[^8] Any person who suffers actual material or moral damage from a culpable act may recover damages under Article 2176, even if the plaintiff is not a biological parent or legal heir of the deceased victim.[^9] The Supreme Court thus allowed an aunt who exercised substitute parental authority under Articles 216, 220 and 233 of the Family Code and continued supporting her ward past age 18 to recover actual and moral damages for the ward's death in a vehicular mishap.[^family-code] [^11] Where the damaged property is insured, an insurer that pays the insured's loss is subrogated under Article 2207 of the Civil Code to the insured's cause of action under Article 2176 against the tortfeasor.[^civil-code] [^12]

## Distinction Between Quasi-Delict and Breach of Contract

Negligence is the omission of the degree of diligence required by the nature of the obligation and corresponding to the circumstances of the persons, time and place under Article 1173 of the Civil Code.[^civil-code] [^13] Whether a negligent act is governed by Article 2176 (*culpa aquiliana*) or by Articles 1170, 1172 and 1173 (*culpa contractual*) depends on whether the duty breached arose from a pre-existing contract or from the general duty of care owed to all persons.[^14]

In *Dr. Genevieve L. Huang v. Philippine Hoteliers, Inc.* and *Mother Goose Special School System, Inc. v. Spouses Palaganas*, the Supreme Court summarized three operational differences between *culpa aquiliana* and *culpa contractual*:[^15] [^16]
1. **Nature of the negligence.** In a quasi-delict, negligence is direct, substantive and independent. In breach of contract, negligence is merely incidental to the performance of an existing obligation.[^17] [^18]
2. **Availability of the employer's diligence defense.** In a quasi-delict, proof that a parent, guardian or employer exercised the diligence of a good father of a family in selecting and supervising dependents or employees is a complete defense under the last paragraph of Article 2180. In breach of contract, that defense is not a complete defense.[^19] [^20]
3. **Burden of proof and presumption of negligence.** In a quasi-delict, no general presumption of negligence exists and the injured party must prove the defendant's fault by a preponderance of evidence. In breach of contract, negligence is presumed once the plaintiff proves the existence of the contract and its non-fulfillment, shifting the burden to the obligor to prove that it exercised due care.[^21] Because this shift alters the defense, a plaintiff who litigated solely on quasi-delict in the trial court cannot switch to a breach-of-contract theory for the first time on appeal.[^22]

In *Orient Freight International, Inc. v. Keihin-Everett Forwarding Co., Inc.*, the Supreme Court clarified when Article 2176 may apply despite a pre-existing contract between the parties.[^23] Under the doctrine that an act that breaks a contract may also be a tort, Article 2176 applies only if the complained act would have constituted an actionable tort even if no contract existed between the parties, or if the breach was attended by bad faith or oppressive conduct violating Article 21 of the Civil Code.[^24] Where a trucking subcontractor negligently reported a hijacking after the forwarder that hired it asked for an investigation, the duty to report existed before the negligent act. Articles 1170, 1172 and 1173 therefore governed rather than Article 2176.[^25] Similarly, in *Mother Goose Special School*, a school's failure to protect a pupil who was punched 11 times during class hours violated its built-in contractual undertaking to maintain a safe learning environment, rendering the school liable for *culpa contractual* without recourse to the Article 2180 defense of due diligence in selecting teachers.[^26]

## Article 2176 Distinguished from Human Relations Torts Under Articles 19, 20 and 21

Article 2176 sits among the sources of obligation and differs from the human relations rules in Articles 19, 20 and 21.[^27] The Supreme Court explained both points in *St. Martin Polyclinic*:

An action for damages due to the negligence of another may be instituted on the basis of Article 2176 of the Civil Code, which defines a quasi-delict:

Article 2176. Whoever by act or omission causes damage to another, there being fault or negligence, is obliged to pay for the damage done. Such fault or negligence, if there is no pre-existing contractual relation between the parties, is called a quasi-delict and is governed by the provisions of this Chapter.

The elements of a quasi-delict are: (1) an act or omission; (2) the presence of fault or negligence in the performance or non-performance of the act; (3) injury; (4) a causal connection between the negligent act and the injury; and (5) no pre-existing contractual relation.

As a general rule, any act or omission coming under the purview of Article 2176 gives rise to a cause of action under quasi-delict. This, in turn, gives the basis for a claim of damages. Notably, quasi-delict is one among several sources of obligation. Article 1157 of the Civil Code states:

Article 1157. Obligations arise from:

(1) Law;  
(2) Contracts;  
(3) Quasi-contracts;  
(4) Acts or omissions punished by law; and  
(5) Quasi-delicts.

Article 2176 is not an all-encompassing enumeration of all actionable wrongs which can give rise to the liability for damages. Under the Civil Code, acts done in violation of Articles 19, 20, and 21 will also give rise to damages. 

Article 19. Every person must, in the exercise of his rights and in the performance of his duties, act with justice, give everyone his due, and observe honesty and good faith.

Article 20. Every person who, contrary to law, willfully or negligently causes damage to another, shall indemnify the latter for the same.

Article 21. Any person who willfully causes loss or injury to another in a manner that is contrary to morals, good customs, or public policy shall compensate the latter for the damage.

Article 19, known to contain what is commonly referred to as the principle of abuse of rights, sets certain standards which must be observed not only in the exercise of one's rights, but also in the performance of one's duties. Case law states that "[w]hen a right is exercised in a manner which does not conform with the norms enshrined in Article 19 and results in damage to another, a legal wrong is thereby committed for which the wrongdoer must be held responsible. But while Article 19 lays down a rule of conduct for the government of human relations and for the maintenance of social order, it does not provide a remedy for its violation. Generally, an action for damages under either Article 20 or Article 21 would then be proper." Between these two provisions as worded, it is Article 20 which applies to both willful and negligent acts that are done contrary to law. On the other hand, Article 21 applies only to willful acts done contra bonos mores.

Article 19 is the general rule which governs the conduct of human relations. By itself, it is not the basis of an actionable tort. Article 19 describes the degree of care required so that an actionable tort may arise when it is alleged together with Article 20 or Article 21.

Article 20 concerns violations of existing law as basis for an injury. It allows recovery should the act have been willful or negligent. Willful may refer to the intention to do the act and the desire to achieve the outcome which is considered by the plaintiff in tort action as injurious. Negligence may refer to a situation where the act was consciously done but without intending the result which the plaintiff considers as injurious.

Article 21, on the other hand, concerns injuries that may be caused by acts which are not necessarily proscribed by law. This article requires that the act be willful, that is, that there was an intention to do the act and a desire to achieve the outcome. In cases under Article 21, the legal issues revolve around whether such outcome should be considered a legal injury on the part of the plaintiff or whether the commission of the act was done in violation of the standards of care required in Article 19.

Article 2176 covers situations where an injury happens through an act or omission of the defendant. When it involves a positive act, the intention to commit the outcome is irrelevant. The act itself must not be a breach of an existing law or a pre-existing contractual obligation. What will be considered is whether there is "fault or negligence” attending the commission of the act which necessarily leads to the outcome considered as injurious by the plaintiff. The required degree of diligence will then be assessed in relation to the circumstances of each and every case. 

Thus, with respect to negligent acts or omissions, it should therefore be discerned that Article 20 of the Civil Code concerns "violations of existing law as basis for an injury", whereas Article 2176 applies when the negligent act causing damage to another does not constitute "a breach of an existing law or a pre-existing contractual obligation."[^28]

A negligence claim that rests on neither a law nor a contract is governed by Article 2176.[^29] In *St. Martin Polyclinic*, a recruitment agency sued an accredited medical clinic for damages after the clinic certified a prospective overseas worker as fit for employment and the worker purportedly tested positive for hepatitis C in Saudi Arabia.[^30]

Article 2176 governs when the claimed negligent act breaches no law and no contract.[^31] The Supreme Court held that the courts below "erroneously anchored their respective rulings on the provisions of Articles 19, 20, and 21 of the Civil Code" because the agency "did not proffer (nor have these courts mentioned) any law as basis for which damages may be recovered due to petitioner's alleged negligent act."[^32] It reasoned that the claimed negligent act "was not premised on the breach of any law" and that "no pre-existing contractual relation was averred to exist between the parties", so "Article 2176 - instead of Articles 19, 20 and 21 - of the Civil Code should govern."[^33]

The claim failed on the evidence even under the correct article.[^34] The Supreme Court held that "the petition is GRANTED" and dismissed the complaint "for lack of merit."[^35]

## Standard of Care, Foreseeability and Proximate Cause

The test of negligence is whether the defendant in doing the alleged negligent act used that reasonable care and caution which an ordinarily prudent person would have used in the same situation.[^36] [^37] In *Cresencia Achevara v. Ramos*, the Supreme Court held that foreseeability is the fundamental test of negligence.[^38] A person is negligent when they fail to guard against a reasonable probability of harm that an ordinarily prudent person would have foreseen under the circumstances, though the law does not require guarding against a bare possibility of remote accident.[^39]

Negligence alone does not create liability under Article 2176 unless it is the proximate cause of the plaintiff's injury.[^40] Proximate cause is that cause which in natural and continuous sequence, unbroken by any efficient intervening cause, produces the injury and without which the result would not have occurred.[^41] Where damage occurs without a breach of legal duty or without proof of proximate causation, the loss is *damnum absque injuria* and cannot be shifted to the defendant.[^42] [^43]
- In *Kim v. Slimmers World*, a 59-year-old gym member complained of a headache after completing her 12th personal training session and died three days later of cerebral hemorrhage and severe hypertension.[^44] The Supreme Court absolved the fitness center and its trainers because the member had declared in her application that she was not hypertensive, the gym nurses promptly took her blood pressure and brought her to the hospital and the plaintiff presented no medical or expert evidence showing that the workout was the proximate cause of her stroke.[^45]
- In *St. Martin Polyclinic*, the Supreme Court dismissed the recruitment agency's claim because a foreign blood test taken in Saudi Arabia more than two months after the clinic's local examination did not prove that the worker had already contracted Hepatitis C on the date the clinic certified him as fit to work.[^46]
- Under Article 2179 of the Civil Code, a plaintiff whose own negligence was the immediate and proximate cause of the injury cannot recover damages, whereas merely contributory negligence only mitigates the award.[^civil-code] [^47] In *Achevara*, a driver knowingly operated a mechanically defective owner-type jeep that wiggled on the highway and encroached on the lane of an oncoming passenger jeepney.[^48] The passenger jeepney driver saw the zigzagging vehicle but did not immediately veer to the rightmost portion of the road or stop at the right shoulder.[^49] The Supreme Court ruled that the doctrine of last clear chance did not apply because the negligence of both drivers was contemporaneous and the passenger jeepney driver no longer had the opportunity to avoid the collision.[^50] Both drivers' acts were proximate causes of the crash, so Article 2179 barred recovery.[^civil-code] [^51]

## Burden of Proof, Evidentiary Presumptions and *Res Ipsa Loquitur*

Because the law presumes that every person takes ordinary care of their concerns, negligence is never presumed in an action under Article 2176 except where the law or established doctrine provides otherwise.[^52] [^53] The plaintiff bears the burden of proving fault and causation by a preponderance of evidence.[^54] [^55]

Philippine law recognizes two major evidentiary aids that shift the burden of evidence in quasi-delict actions:
1. **Statutory traffic-violation presumptions.** Under Article 2185 of the Civil Code, a person driving a motor vehicle is disputably presumed to be negligent if they were violating any traffic regulation at the time of the mishap.[^civil-code] In *De Belen v. Fuchs*, a jeepney driver encroached on the outer left lane of a curved road at nighttime.[^56] He violated Section 37 of Republic Act No. 4136 and was presumed negligent.[^ra-4136-1964] [^58]
2. **The doctrine of *res ipsa loquitur*.** As a procedural rule of evidence, *res ipsa loquitur* substitutes for direct proof of negligence when three requisites concur:
   1. the accident is of a kind that ordinarily does not occur in the absence of someone's negligence;
   2. the instrumentality or agency that caused the injury was under the exclusive control and management of the person charged with negligence; and
   3. the injury suffered must not have been due to any voluntary action or contribution on the part of the person injured.[^59] [^60]
   - In *Maitim v. Aguila*, the Supreme Court applied *res ipsa loquitur* where a Ford W-150 Chateau Wagon sideswiped a six-year-old girl on a shared townhouse driveway and dragged her for about three meters.[^61] She was on her way to board her family's car.[^62] The vehicle was under the driver's control and driving in a shared driveway does not ordinarily injure children without a lack of care.[^63] The Supreme Court found no contributory negligence on the part of the girl or her mother.[^64]
   - Conversely, in *Huang v. Philippine Hoteliers*, the Supreme Court rejected *res ipsa loquitur* where a poolside guest lifted a heavy hinged wooden countertop after pool hours to reach an interior house telephone and the countertop fell on her head.[^65] Her own voluntary act of lifting the unlatched door leaf intervened and defeated the second and third requisites of the doctrine.[^66]

## Direct Liability, Vicarious Liability, the Registered-Owner Rule and Owner-in-Vehicle Liability

Under Article 2180 of the Civil Code, the obligation imposed by Article 2176 is demandable not only for one's own acts or omissions, but also for those of persons for whom one is responsible, including minor children, wards, employees, students and special agents of the State.[^civil-code] When two or more persons are liable for a quasi-delict, their responsibility is solidary under Article 2194.[^civil-code]
- **Direct and immediate employer liability and absence of agency.** An employer's liability under Article 2180 is direct, primary and solidary once the employee's negligence under Article 2176 is established, subject to the defense that the employer observed the diligence of a good father of a family in both the selection and supervision of the employee.[^67] [^68] However, Article 2180 requires an actual employer-employee or custodial relationship. In *Spouses Mamaril v. Boy Scout of the Philippines*, the Supreme Court held that a building owner that leased out paid parking spaces was neither directly liable under Article 2176 nor vicariously liable under Article 2180 when guards hired by an independent security agency mistakenly released a parked passenger jeepney to an unauthorized person, because the building owner committed no fault and was not the guards' employer.[^69]
- **Harmonization of Article 2176, Article 2180 and the registered-owner rule.** Under Section 5 of Republic Act No. 4136, all motor vehicles used on public highways must be registered.[^ra-4136-1964] [^70] In *Caravan Travel*, the Supreme Court harmonized the registered-owner rule with Articles 2176 and 2180 by holding that the source of a registered owner's liability remains Articles 2176 and 2180 of the Civil Code.[^71] Once a plaintiff proves that the defendant is the registered owner of the offending vehicle, a disputable presumption arises that the requirements of Article 2180 have been satisfied.[^72] The burden of evidence then shifts to the registered owner to prove one of the following:
   - that it had no employment relationship with the driver;
   - that the driver acted outside the scope of assigned tasks; or
   - that it exercised the diligence of a good father of a family in selecting and supervising the driver.[^73]

   Because the registered owner and the driver are solidarily liable under Article 2194, the negligent driver is merely a necessary party and not an indispensable party in a suit against the registered owner.[^74]
- **Owner-in-vehicle liability under Article 2184.** In *De Belen v. Fuchs*, the Supreme Court stressed that Article 2180 applies to motor vehicle mishaps only when the owner was **not** inside the vehicle at the time of the accident, as expressly stated in the second paragraph of Article 2184.[^civil-code] [^75] Because the jeepney owner and employer was riding aboard the passenger jeepney when his driver negligently encroached on the opposite lane and killed an oncoming tricycle driver, the owner's solidary liability was governed by the first paragraph of Article 2184 rather than Article 2180, as he could have prevented the misfortune through due diligence.[^76]
- **Special parental authority of schools.** In *University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*, the Supreme Court sitting En Banc held the university and its co-petitioners principally and solidarily liable under Articles 2176 and 2180 of the Civil Code and Articles 218 and 219 of the Family Code.[^civil-code] [^family-code] [^77] A student's gown caught fire during a candle-lit beauty pageant in the university's social hall.[^78] The Supreme Court found that no faculty member was present to supervise the event except the judges.[^79] It also found that the university failed to take safety measures.[^80]

## Damages Recoverable in Quasi-Delicts and the PHP 300,000.00 Death Indemnity

In quasi-delicts, the defendant is liable under Article 2202 of the Civil Code for all damages that are the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of, whether or not the defendant foresaw or could have reasonably foreseen them.[^civil-code] Moral damages may be recovered in quasi-delicts causing physical injuries under Article 2219(2) or death under Article 2206(3), while exemplary damages may be granted under Articles 2229 and 2231 if the defendant acted with gross negligence.[^civil-code] [^81]

In its November 25, 2025 En Banc decision in *University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*, the Supreme Court systematized the classification of tort damages in the Philippines into four classes:

- **Economic damages:** actual or compensatory and temperate damages.
- **Non-economic damages:** moral damages and civil indemnity *ex delicto* or *ex quasi-delicto*.
- **Punitive damages:** exemplary damages.
- **Nominal damages.**[^82]

Invoking equal protection and its judicial authority to adjust statutory damage floors to contemporary economic realities, the Supreme Court increased the minimum civil indemnity under Article 2206 for death resulting from a quasi-delict from PHP 50,000.00 (the rate set in 1997) to **PHP 300,000.00** and directed that this baseline be automatically recomputed every five years in the absence of remedial legislation.[^civil-code] [^83]

## Suggested Answers

**Q1: Hypothetical: A freight forwarder asked its subcontracted trucking company to report on a hijacked truck but received a negligent report of a simple breakdown that cost the forwarder a client. May the forwarder hold the company liable for a quasi-delict under Article 2176?**
No, the trucking company's liability is governed by Articles 1170, 1172 and 1173 on negligence in the performance of an obligation rather than Article 2176.[^95] Under *Orient Freight International, Inc. v. Keihin-Everett Forwarding Co., Inc.*, Article 2176 applies between contracting parties only when the negligent act would constitute an actionable tort independent of the contract or when the breach is attended by bad faith under Article 21.[^96] The duty to investigate and report arose when the forwarder asked for it and existed before the negligent act, so the negligence arose from performing that obligation and could not be sued upon as a quasi-delict.[^97]

**Q2: Hypothetical: A recruitment agency sued an accredited clinic with which it had no contract for certifying as fit a worker who tested positive for Hepatitis C abroad two months later, citing no violated statute and offering no proof of earlier infection. Will the action prosper under Articles 19, 20 and 21 or under Article 2176?**
The action must be evaluated under Article 2176 rather than Articles 19, 20 and 21 and it will be dismissed for lack of proven negligence and causation.[^98] In *St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corporation*, the Supreme Court held that Article 20 requires a violation of an existing statute and Article 21 requires a willful act *contra bonos mores*, whereas Article 2176 governs alleged negligence that breaches neither a statute nor a pre-existing contract.[^99] Because negligence is never presumed in a quasi-delict and a foreign test taken two months later does not prove that the clinic's earlier certification was erroneous or proximately caused the loss, the complaint fails.[^100]

**Q3: Hypothetical: An aunt who raised an 18-year-old pedestrian as substitute parent sued a tour company, the registered owner of the van that killed her, under Articles 2176 and 2180 and dropped the unlocated driver as a defendant. May the company obtain dismissal because the aunt is not a biological parent and the driver is an indispensable party?**
No, the tour company cannot obtain dismissal on either ground.[^101] Under *Caravan Travel and Tours International, Inc. v. Abejar*, the first sentence of Article 2176 does not restrict the active subjects who may sue, allowing any person who suffers actual loss or anguish, including an aunt who exercised substitute parental authority, to recover damages.[^102] Furthermore, proof of vehicle registration raises a disputable presumption of liability under Article 2180 and because joint tortfeasors are solidarily liable under Article 2194, the negligent driver is merely a necessary party whose non-inclusion does not prevent the action from proceeding against the registered owner.[^103]

**Q4: Hypothetical: An owner riding in the front seat of his own jeepney at night let his hired driver speed and encroach on the opposite lane, killing a tricycle driver, yet invokes the good-father-of-a-family defense under Article 2180 when sued. Which provision governs the owner's liability?**
The owner's solidary liability is governed by the first paragraph of Article 2184 of the Civil Code rather than Article 2180.[^civil-code] [^104] In *De Belen v. Fuchs*, the Supreme Court ruled that Article 2180 applies to motor vehicle mishaps only when the owner was not in the vehicle at the time of the accident.[^105] Because the owner was riding aboard the jeepney when his driver violated traffic regulations and encroached on the opposite lane, he is solidarily liable with the driver under Article 2184 for failing to prevent the misfortune through due diligence.[^106]

**Q5: Hypothetical: A six-year-old child walking along a shared residential townhouse driveway was hit and dragged three meters by a neighbor's van driven by the neighbor's chauffeur, while in a separate incident an adult hotel guest lifted a hinged wooden countertop after pool hours to reach an interior telephone and the countertop fell on her head. How does the doctrine of *res ipsa loquitur* apply to these two claims under Article 2176?**
The doctrine of *res ipsa loquitur* establishes a presumption of negligence in the driveway accident but does not apply to the hotel countertop incident.[^107] [^108] In *Maitim v. Aguila*, the Supreme Court applied *res ipsa loquitur* because driving in a shared driveway does not ordinarily injure a child without a lack of care and the vehicle was under the driver's control.[^109] By contrast, in *Huang v. Philippine Hoteliers, Inc.*, the Supreme Court held that *res ipsa loquitur* cannot be invoked where the plaintiff's own voluntary act of lifting the unlatched wooden countertop caused it to fall on her head.[^110]

# Quotes

> "Contrary to the findings of the RTC, moreover, Slimmers World et al. cannot be held liable for negligence based on a quasi-delict under Article 2176[48] of the Civil Code. Jurisprudence provides the following requisites to establish a quasi-delict: (1) the damage suffered by the plaintiff; (2) the act or omission of the defendant constituting fault or negligence; and (3) the causal connection between the act and the damage sustained by the plaintiff, or the proximate cause.[49]"[^111]

> "Settled is the rule that in actions based on quasi-delict, it is incumbent upon the plaintiff to prove the presence of the foregoing elements by preponderance of evidence.[50] They cannot rely on mere allegations but must present such evidence more convincing as worthy of belief than that which is offered in opposition thereto.[51] The law presumes that a person takes ordinary care of their concerns and that private transactions have been fair and regular. Hence, negligence cannot be presumed but must be proven.[52]"[^112]

> "If a contracting party's act that breaches the contract would have given rise to an extra-contractual liability had there been no contract, the contract would be deemed breached by a tort,[61] and the party may be held liable under Article 2176 and its related provisions.[62]"[^113]

> "However, if the act complained of would not give rise to a cause of action for a quasi-delict independent of the contract, then the provisions on quasi-delict or tort would be inapplicable.[65]"[^114]

> "In that regard, this Court finds it significant to take note of the following differences between quasi-delict (culpa aquilina) and breach of contract (culpa contractual). In quasi-delict, negligence is direct, substantive and independent, while in breach of contract, negligence is merely incidental to the performance of the contractual obligation; there is a pre-existing contract or obligation.[75] In quasi-delict, the defense of “good father of a family” is a complete and proper defense insofar as parents, guardians and employers are concerned, while in breach of contract, such is not a complete and proper defense in the selection and supervision of employees.[76] In quasi-delict, there is no presumption of negligence and it is incumbent upon the injured party to prove the negligence of the defendant, otherwise, the former’s complaint will be dismissed, while in breach of contract, negligence is presumed so long as it can be proved that there was breach of the contract and the burden is on the defendant to prove that there was no negligence in the carrying out of the terms of the contract; the rule of respondeat superior is followed.[77]"[^115]

> "Article 2176 covers situations where an injury happens through an act or omission of the defendant. When it involves a positive act, the intention to commit the outcome is irrelevant. The act itself must not be a breach of an existing law or a pre-existing contractual obligation. What will be considered is whether there is "fault or negligence” attending the commission of the act which necessarily leads to the outcome considered as injurious by the plaintiff. The required degree of diligence will then be assessed in relation to the circumstances of each and every case.[51] (Emphases and underscoring supplied)"[^116]

> "Thus, it is imperative to apply the registered-owner rule in a manner that harmonizes it with Articles 2176 and 2180 of the Civil Code. Rules must be construed in a manner that will harmonize them with other rules so as to form a uniform and consistent system of jurisprudence.[103] In light of this, the words used in Del Carmen are particularly notable. There, this court stated that Article 2180 "should defer to"[104] the registered-owner rule. It never stated that Article 2180 should be totally abandoned."[^117]

> "Therefore, the appropriate approach is that in cases where both the registered-owner rule and Article 2180 apply, the plaintiff must first establish that the employer is the registered owner of the vehicle in question. Once the plaintiff successfully proves ownership, there arises a disputable presumption that the requirements of Article 2180 have been proven. As a consequence, the burden of proof shifts to the defendant to show that no liability under Article 2180 has arisen."[^118]

> "We take this opportunity to increase the civil indemnity for death resulting from a quasi-delict. This amount was last increased in 1997, or 28 years ago, when it was set at PHP 50,000.00. Civil indemnity for death resulting from a crime was already increased to PHP 100,000.00 in 2013.[172] Given that the statutory basis for these indemnities is one and the same—Article 2206 of the Civil Code—the Court, in the interest of equal protection, finds no substantial distinction to differentiate between the amount of civil indemnity for death caused by a crime or quasi-delict."[^119]

> "In this light, the Court deems it proper to increase the award of civil indemnity for wrongful death to PHP 300,000.00, not by reason of the unpassed legislative proposals, but pursuant to the Court's established authority to modify and update the amounts of damages in accordance with prevailing circumstances. This authority emanates from the Court's inherent power to interpret and apply the law in a manner that ensures the just and equitable vindication of rights."[^120]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Focus:** Keep four core distinctions in mind whenever Article 2176 is tested:
> 1. ***Culpa Aquiliana* (Art. 2176) vs. *Culpa Contractual* (Arts. 1170, 1172 to 1173) (*Huang*; *Orient Freight*; *Mother Goose*):** In a quasi-delict, negligence is direct, substantive and independent, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving fault and the employer's diligence of a good father of a family under Article 2180 is a complete defense. In breach of contract, negligence is merely incidental to a pre-existing obligation, negligence is presumed from the breach and the Article 2180 diligence defense cannot exonerate the obligor. Article 2176 applies between contracting parties only if the act would constitute an actionable tort independent of the contract or involves bad faith under Article 21 (*Orient Freight*).
> 2. **Article 2176 vs. Human Relations Torts Under Articles 19, 20 and 21 (*St. Martin Polyclinic*):** Article 19 states a general standard of conduct and is not an independent cause of action by itself. Article 20 applies to willful or negligent violations of an existing statute. Article 21 applies only to willful acts done *contra bonos mores*. Article 2176 governs fault or negligence where the defendant's intent is irrelevant and the act breaches neither a positive statute nor a pre-existing contract.
> 3. **Harmonizing the Registered-Owner Rule, Article 2180 and Article 2184 (*Caravan Travel*; *De Belen*):** Proof that the defendant is the registered owner of the offending motor vehicle raises a disputable presumption that the requirements of Article 2180 have been met, shifting the burden of evidence to the owner (*Caravan Travel*). However, Article 2180 applies only when the owner was **not** in the vehicle; if the owner was riding inside the vehicle and could have prevented the mishap through due diligence, solidary liability attaches under the first paragraph of Article 2184 (*De Belen*).
> 4. **Updated PHP 300,000.00 Wrongful-Death Indemnity Floor (*USEP v. Sarate*):** Under the November 25, 2025 En Banc ruling in *University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*, the minimum civil indemnity under Article 2206 for death resulting from a quasi-delict was increased from PHP 50,000.00 to **PHP 300,000.00** (subject to automatic recomputation every five years), separate from economic (actual or temperate), moral and exemplary damages.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practical Application:** In tort and personal-injury litigation:
> - **Plead the proper theory at the trial level:** Never litigate solely on quasi-delict in the trial court if a pre-existing contract exists and then attempt to switch to *culpa contractual* on appeal. In *Huang v. Philippine Hoteliers, Inc.*, the Supreme Court barred that change of theory because it unfairly shifts the burden of proof and deprives the defendant of the Article 2180 defense without a trial opportunity.
> - **Non-heirs and dropping an unlocated driver:** Any person who suffers actual personal loss or anguish (such as an aunt who exercised substitute parental authority under the Family Code) has standing to sue under the first sentence of Article 2176 (*Caravan Travel*). Moreover, because the registered owner and the driver are solidarily liable under Article 2194, the driver is merely a necessary party and not an indispensable party, so the plaintiff may drop an unlocated driver and proceed directly against the registered owner (*Caravan Travel*).
> - **Proving medical or gym negligence:** Because negligence and causation are never presumed in an Article 2176 suit, always present competent medical or expert evidence linking the defendant's specific act or omission to the injury rather than relying on post-incident diagnoses or hearsay medical certificates (*Kim v. Slimmers World*; *St. Martin Polyclinic*; *Huang*).
