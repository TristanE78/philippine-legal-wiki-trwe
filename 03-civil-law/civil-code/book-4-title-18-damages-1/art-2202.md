> ART. 2202. In crimes and quasi-delicts, the defendant shall be liable for all damages which are the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of. It is not necessary that such damages have been foreseen or could have reasonably been foreseen by the defendant.[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 2202 of the Civil Code establishes the breadth of compensatory liability in crimes and quasi-delicts by making the wrongdoer answerable for all damages that are the natural and probable consequences of the wrongful act or omission even when those consequences were not foreseen or could not have been reasonably foreseen.[^civil-code]

1. **Covered sources of obligation:** Article 2202 applies strictly to crimes and quasi-delicts rather than contractual breaches governed by Article 2201 or bare litigation losses lacking an underlying wrong.[^civil-code]
2. **No foreseeability shield:** Unlike a good-faith contractual obligor under Article 2201, a defendant in a crime or quasi-delict remains liable for natural and probable damages regardless of foreseeability.[^civil-code]
3. **Proximate causation required:** Liability ends where an active supervening intervening cause breaks the causal link or where a party's involvement is too remote to constitute proximate cause.
4. **Proof still governs quantum:** Article 2202 defines causal reach but operates alongside Article 2199, requiring competent proof of pecuniary loss before actual damages may be awarded and shifting unproven pecuniary loss to temperate or nominal damages where warranted.

The sections below examine the statutory scope and boundaries of Article 2202, proximate causation and intervening causes, quasi-delict applications and evidentiary limits, criminal civil liability, a consolidated table of supporting case dispositions, related provisions, key cases and suggested bar answers.

## Scope, Architecture and Statutory Boundaries

Chapter 2, Title XVIII, Book IV of the Civil Code organizes actual or compensatory damages by source of obligation. Under [Article 2201](art-2201.md), a good-faith obligor in contracts and quasi-contracts answers only for natural and probable consequences that were foreseen or could have been reasonably foreseen when the obligation was constituted, while an obligor guilty of fraud, bad faith, malice or wanton attitude answers for all damages reasonably attributed to non-performance.[^2] Under Article 2202, by contrast, defendants in crimes and quasi-delicts answer for all damages that are the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of regardless of foreseeability.[^civil-code]

Because Article 2202 is anchored to crimes and quasi-delicts, a claimant cannot recover actual or compensatory damages under this provision when the claim rests on neither a contract or quasi-contract nor a crime or quasi-delict. The Supreme Court drew this boundary across election contests in [*Atienza v. COMELEC*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_108533_1994.html)[^3] and [*Malaluan v. COMELEC*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666),[^4] in litigation counterclaims in [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832)[^5] and in summary property destruction anchored on abuse of rights rather than delict or quasi-delict in [*Public Estates Authority v. Chu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43722).[^6] Where no contract, quasi-contract, crime or quasi-delict exists, recovery requires a specific statutory basis such as the human relations provisions in [Article 19](../preliminary-title/art-0019.md), [Article 20](../preliminary-title/art-0020.md) and [Article 21](../preliminary-title/art-0021.md) supported by proof of malice or bad faith.[^7] [^8]

In [*University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70367),[^9] the Supreme Court En Banc situated this rule inside a broader four-part classification of Civil Code damages into economic, non-economic, punitive and nominal categories, quoting Article 2202's wording to explain that economic actual or compensatory damages cover measurable financial impacts arising from the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission regardless of foreseeability. In [*People v. Teehankee, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/oct1995/gr_111206_1995.html),[^10] quoting *Heirs of Raymundo Castro v. Bustos*, as cited in [*People v. Teehankee, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/oct1995/gr_111206_1995.html), the Supreme Court likewise presented Article 2202 as the general rule for crimes and quasi-delicts that is supplemented by [Article 2206](art-2206.md) whenever a felony results in death.

### [*Atienza v. COMELEC*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_108533_1994.html): Exclusion of Election Protest Expenses Absent Civil Code Conditions

In the 1988 municipal mayoralty election protest in Madrilejos, Cebu, the Regional Trial Court declared protestant Lou A. Atienza winner by 12 votes on April 12, 1989 and ordered protestee Antonio G. Sia to reimburse P300,856.19 in election protest expenses.[^21] After the May 11, 1992 elections rendered the protest moot, the COMELEC Second Division dismissed the appeal as moot and the COMELEC En Banc ruled on January 28, 1993 that the vacated trial court judgment was not revived and reversed the P300,856.19 reimbursement award for lack of any wrongful or negligent act or omission.[^22]

Dismissing the certiorari petition, the Supreme Court En Banc explained that actual or compensatory damages under the Civil Code properly arise in breaches of contracts and quasi-contracts under Article 2201 and in crimes and quasi-delicts under Article 2202 where the defendant answers for all natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of.[^23] Absent the conditions specified in Articles 2201 and 2202 or an express statute authorizing money claims for election protest expenses, a winning protestant cannot recover protest expenses as actual damages from the losing party.[^24] Because petitioner pointed to no authorizing statute and legislative repeal of earlier election-code bond provisions confirmed that no reimbursement lies without a wrongful act or omission, the Supreme Court dismissed the petition and affirmed the deletion of the P300,856.19 award.[^25] The decision establishes that Article 2202 does not authorize actual damages where no crime, quasi-delict or wrongful act is attributable to the opposing party.

### [*Malaluan v. COMELEC*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666): Statutory Basis Required for Damages in Election Contests

After the Regional Trial Court declared Luis Malaluan winner of the 1992 Kidapawan mayoralty protest and granted execution pending appeal on a P500,000.00 bond, the COMELEC First Division reversed, proclaimed protestee Joseph Evangelista winner and ordered Malaluan to pay P257,110.00 in actual damages (computed from P557,110.00 gross including attorney's fees, P15,154.00 xerox expenses and P169,456.00 unearned salary and emoluments for 14 months at P12,104.00 per month, less a contingent success fee) chargeable against his bond while disallowing moral and exemplary damages.[^26] The COMELEC En Banc affirmed in toto on May 5, 1995.[^27]

Granting certiorari even after the term expired on June 30, 1995, the Supreme Court En Banc quoted Article 2202 verbatim alongside Articles 2199 and 2201 to define the phrase in accordance with law under Section 259 of the Omnibus Election Code.[^28] Because actual damages under Articles 2201 and 2202 are appropriate only in breaches of contract or quasi-contract or on the occasion of crimes and quasi-delicts, a monetary claim in an election contest must be hinged on contract, quasi-contract, tortious act or omission, crime or an express statutory provision such as Articles 19, 20, 32 or 2208.[^29] None of those bases existed because judicial error by the trial court did not prove malicious intent by Malaluan. Moreover, a protestant seated through execution pending appeal is a *de facto* officer entitled to emoluments whose tenure inflicts *damnum absque injuria* on the eventual winner.[^30] The Supreme Court upheld the moot proclamation ruling but declared the P257,110.00 actual damages award null and void for grave abuse of discretion and excess of jurisdiction.[^31] The ruling confirms that Article 2202 limits actual damages to delictual or quasi-delictual wrongs and bars compensation for lawful *de facto* occupancy.

### [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832): Litigation-Filing Counterclaims Outside Article 2202

ABS-CBN sued VIVA Productions, Vicente Del Rosario and Republic Broadcasting Corp. (RBS) for specific performance and injunction over television film exhibition rights.[^32] Finding no perfected contract between ABS-CBN and VIVA, the Regional Trial Court dismissed the complaint and ordered ABS-CBN to pay RBS P107,727.00 counterbond premium, P191,843.00 print advertisements, P1 million attorney's fees, P5 million moral damages and P5 million exemplary damages, plus P212,000.00 attorney's fees to VIVA and costs.[^33] The Court of Appeals sustained RBS's actual damages of P107,727.00 and P191,843.00 while reducing moral damages to P2 million, exemplary damages to P2 million and attorney's fees to P500,000.00.[^34]

Granting ABS-CBN's Rule 45 petition, the Supreme Court recited Chapter 2, Title XVIII, Book IV of the Civil Code including Article 2202's verbatim rule for crimes and quasi-delicts and held that RBS's counterclaim for actual damages (originally alleged as P6,621,195.32) arose solely from ABS-CBN's filing of a civil complaint rather than from contract, quasi-contract, delict or quasi-delict.[^35] Consequently, the award could not be comprehended under the Civil Code law on actual damages and could rest only on Articles 19, 20 and 21 upon adequate proof of malice or bad faith.[^36] Finding no proof of malice or bad faith, noting that a corporation cannot recover moral damages because it has no nervous system and holding that an adverse litigation outcome alone is *damnum absque injuria*, the Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and deleted all awards to RBS while preserving the unappealed P212,000.00 attorney's fees in favor of VIVA, with no pronouncement as to costs.[^37] The decision establishes that expenses incurred in defending a non-malicious lawsuit fall outside Article 2202.

## Proximate Causation, Foreseeability and Intervening Causes

Article 2202 pairs an expansive foreseeability rule with a strict causal requirement. On one hand, the second sentence removes foreseeability as a defense once proximate causation is established: the defendant remains liable for all natural and probable consequences even if the exact downstream harm was not foreseen or could not have been reasonably foreseen.[^civil-code] [^38] On the other hand, the wrongful act or omission must remain the proximate cause of the injury.

The Supreme Court applies this causation filter in both directions. An intervening act by a third person or a natural force does not cut off liability when the risk was reasonably foreseeable or when the defendant's prior negligence set the danger in motion, as seen in [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Company and Intergames, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921),[^39] [*Davao del Norte Electric Cooperative v. Heirs of Victorino Lucas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68895)[^40] (where strong winds and a flying galvanized iron sheet did not excuse a utility's poorly maintained sagging power lines) and [*National Power Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42798)[^41] (where heavy rains did not constitute a fortuitous event after negligent dam management). Equally, antecedent gross negligence that acts as the root cause of later humiliation or investigation triggers Article 2202 liability in [*Go v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/may1991/gr_68138_1991.html),[^42] and employer failure to repair grounded factory machines supports full quasi-delict death damages in [*Europa v. Hunter Garments Mfg. (Phil.) Inc.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jul1989/gr_72827_1989.html).[^43] By contrast, where a party's involvement is purely financial and too remote as in [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Company and Intergames, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921),[^44] or where an active supervening fraud breaks the causal connection as in [*Banzon v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jul1989/gr_47258_1989.html),[^45] the resulting injury is not a natural and probable consequence under Article 2202.

### [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Company and Intergames, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921): Organizer Liability, Non-Efficient Intervening Cause and Sponsor Remoteness

Eighteen-year-old minor Rommel Abrogar participated in the 1st Pop Cola Junior Marathon organized by Intergames, Inc. and sponsored by Cosmos Bottling Company along an unblocked Quezon City highway, where a recklessly driven passenger jeepney bumped and killed him.[^46] On May 10, 1991, the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 83 held Cosmos and Intergames solidarily liable (with cross-claim reimbursement for Cosmos) for P28,061.63 actual damages (also cited as P28,061.65), P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and 10 percent attorney's fees on the total of P178,061,63 (printed thus in the trial court judgment, yielding P17,806.16) while denying loss of earning capacity.[^47] On March 10, 2004, the Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the complaint on no-negligence and assumption-of-risk grounds.[^48]

Explicitly citing and quoting Article 2202 verbatim, the Supreme Court held Intergames liable for all damages that were the natural and probable consequences of its negligence in staging a junior marathon on an unblocked road without adequate safety coordination.[^49] The reckless jeepney driver's act was not an efficient intervening cause because the danger of vehicular traffic on an unblocked highway was reasonably foreseeable and anticipated by Intergames.[^50] By contrast, the Supreme Court absolved mere financial sponsor Cosmos because its sponsorship lacked a direct or immediate causal connection and was too remote to be the proximate cause of death.[^51] Applying Article 2202 together with [Article 2206](art-2206.md), [Article 2231](art-2231.md), [Article 2211](art-2211.md) and [Article 2208](art-2208.md) (while distinguishing the P8,000.00 monthly or P96,000.00 yearly benchmark used for graduating university students in quoted precedent), the Supreme Court partly affirmed the Court of Appeals as to Cosmos, reversed as to Intergames and reinstated the Regional Trial Court judgment against Intergames with modifications ordering Intergames to pay additionally P113,484.52 for Rommel's loss of earning capacity (computed on minimum wage for a non-working minor), 10 percent attorney's fees on the total amount inclusive of earning capacity and interest, 6 percent annual interest on actual, moral, exemplary damages and loss of earning capacity from May 10, 1991 until full payment, compounded 6 percent annual interest from finality until full payment and costs of suit.[^52] The decision defines both the reach of Article 2202 against a negligent organizer despite a foreseeable third-party act and its causal boundary protecting a remote financial sponsor.

### [*Go v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/may1991/gr_68138_1991.html): Antecedent Negligence as Root Cause of Unforeseen Harm

Solidbank Baguio branch manager Agustin Y. Go allowed an impostor to open a savings account and deposit and withdraw two altered United States treasury checks worth $1,810.00 and $913.40 (P20,565.69 total) payable to Floverto Jasmin despite patent discrepancies in payee name, address and personal details against the depositor sheet.[^53] Over a year later, when the checks returned altered, Go filed an estafa complaint with the Philippine Constabulary against actual American retiree Floverto Jazmin, causing the septuagenarian to be summoned and investigated in Lingayen and Benguet before investigators confirmed an impostor had committed the fraud.[^54] The Court of First Instance of Pangasinan, Branch II ordered Go and the bank solidarily to pay P6,000.00 moral damages, P1,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation costs and costs, plus P3,000.00 exemplary damages against Go alone with 6 percent annual interest.[^55] On January 24, 1984, the Intermediate Appellate Court disallowed moral and exemplary damages and ordered Go and the bank solidarily to pay P3,000.00 nominal damages with 6 percent annual interest and P1,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation costs.[^56]

Affirming the Intermediate Appellate Court with costs against petitioners, the Supreme Court recited both sentences of Article 2202 verbatim and ruled that while honest error in reporting a crime does not by itself warrant moral damages, Go's antecedent gross negligence in failing to notice patent discrepancies was the root cause of the swindling and of the resulting inconvenience, humiliation and embarrassment suffered by Jazmin.[^57] Because Article 2202 dispenses with foreseeability in quasi-delicts, Go was liable for those downstream consequences. Solidbank was also solidarily liable under paragraph 5 of [Article 2180](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2180.md) for failing to prove due diligence in selecting and supervising its employee.[^58] The case illustrates how Article 2202 holds a negligent actor liable for unforeseen downstream harm traced to antecedent negligence as root cause.

### [*Banzon v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jul1989/gr_47258_1989.html): Active Supervening Fraud Breaking the Causal Chain

Maximo R. Sta. Maria obtained 1952 crop loans from Philippine National Bank guaranteed by surety Associated Insurance & Surety, Inc., with Antonio R. Banzon as indemnitor.[^59] When Sta. Maria fell behind on payments, Associated obtained an advance 1957 judgment (P30,257.86 plus 12 percent interest, P593.76 premiums and stamps and 15 percent attorney's fees) without first paying PNB, levied on and bought Banzon's two Caloocan lots for P44,000.00 and left one lot exposed to levy and house demolition by Associated's own creditors, even while Sta. Maria kept paying PNB directly and reduced principal to P15,446.44 by 1963.[^60] Banzon and wife Rosa Balmaceda sued the Sta. Marias for damages. The Court of First Instance ordered the Sta. Marias solidarily to pay P6,750.00 and P10,000.00 in attorney's-fee actual damages, P150,000.00 lot value if recovery became impossible, P10,000.00 house value plus P200.00 monthly unrealized income from April 1970, P50,000.00 moral damages, P15,000.00 attorney's fees and costs.[^61] The Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. No. 54075-R and absolved the Sta. Marias.[^62]

Denying the petition for review with no pronouncement as to costs, the Supreme Court applied the rule that a defendant is liable only for damages that are the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of.[^63] Because the Sta. Marias' non-payment lacked bad faith and was merely the remote cause setting events in motion while Associated's premature execution and rank fraud constituted active supervening forces breaking the causal chain, the indemnitors' losses were not the natural and probable consequence of the debtors' default and recourse lay against Associated instead.[^64] The decision establishes the causal ceiling of Article 2202 when an active supervening wrongful act severs proximate causation.

## Quasi-Delict Applications: Catastrophic Injury, Environmental Harm and Proof Limits

In quasi-delicts, Article 2202 works in tandem with [Article 2199](art-2199.md) and [Article 2200](art-2200.md). While Article 2202 extends liability to all natural and probable consequences covering both *daño emergente* (loss already suffered) and *lucro cesante* (profits failed to be obtained), Article 2199 restricts actual or compensatory damages to pecuniary loss duly proved with a reasonable degree of certainty rather than speculation or hearsay.[^65] [^66]

This two-part framework operates consistently across vehicular collisions, utility hazards and environmental torts:
- **Receipted and actuarial proof succeeds:** In [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163),[^67] [*Davao del Norte Electric Cooperative v. Heirs of Victorino Lucas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68895)[^68] and [*Fegarido v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68422),[^69] receipted repair, medical and funeral expenses and income-tax-supported earning capacity were sustained while unsubstantiated daily income or unproved earning capacity claims were denied. In [*Mercury Drug Corp. v. Spouses Huang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43927),[^70] actuarial projections grounded in medical prognosis and monthly care costs established lifelong life care cost and impaired earning capacity. In [*Sanggacala v. National Power Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67780),[^71] tax declarations, flood photographs, similar judgments and uncontroverted testimonies substantiated multi-year palay crop losses from dam flooding while unproved yearly rentals and unauthorized just-compensation claims were deleted.
- **Defective or speculative proof triggers deletion or fallback damages:** In [*De Vera, Jr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52770),[^72] quoting the trial court's reliance on Article 2176 in relation to Article 2202, the Supreme Court affirmed the deletion of unproven future penalties and redemption charges as speculative damages barred by Article 2199 and affirmed nominal damages instead. Where actual pecuniary loss is undeniable but its exact peso value lacks competent proof, courts substitute temperate damages under [Article 2224](art-2224.md), as in [*National Power Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42798),[^73] [*Josefa v. Manila Electric Company*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57240)[^74] (where an internal accounting summary was excluded as hearsay) and [*Public Estates Authority v. Chu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43722)[^75], or nominal damages as in [*PNOC Shipping and Transport Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36413).[^76]

### [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163): Harmonizing Article 2202 Causation with Article 2199 Proof

A Mayamy bus registered to Elvira Lim and driven by Mariano C. Mendoza encroached into the opposite lane on Boni Serrano Avenue and hit the Isuzu Elf truck owned by Spouses Leonora and Gabriel Gomez, damaging the truck and injuring its occupants.[^77] On January 31, 2001, the Regional Trial Court awarded P142,757.40 for truck repairs, P1,000.00 daily unrealized income from March to November 1997, P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees and costs (while finding P11,267.35 in receipted medical expenses).[^78] On September 29, 2003, the Court of Appeals deleted the P1,000.00 daily unrealized income and maintained the remaining awards.[^79]

Partly granting the appeal by certiorari, the Supreme Court explicitly harmonized Article 2202 with Article 2199: although Article 2202 holds a quasi-delict defendant liable for all natural and probable consequences regardless of foreseeability, Article 2199 requires adequate compensation only for duly proved pecuniary loss, so a claimant must prove both natural-and-probable causation and the adequate amount of damage.[^80] Holding Mendoza and Lim solidarily liable, the Supreme Court maintained the P142,757.40 truck repair award and granted an additional P11,267.35 for receipted medical expenses shouldered by respondents (both with legal interest from January 31, 2001 until fully paid), left undisturbed the deletion of unsubstantiated P1,000.00 daily lost income, maintained P50,000.00 exemplary damages and costs of suit while deleting P100,000.00 moral damages (because uninjured vehicle owners could not claim under [Article 2219](art-2219.md)(2) without pleading and proof) and P50,000.00 attorney's fees.[^81] The decision states the governing two-pronged test combining Article 2202 causation with Article 2199 proof of amount.

### [*PNOC Shipping and Transport Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36413): Hearsay Bar on Price Quotations and Nominal Damages Fallback

Private respondent Maria Efigenia Fishing Corporation's vessel *M/V Maria Efigenia XV* sank after colliding with a tanker whose liabilities were assumed by PNOC Shipping and Transport Corp. due to the tanker captain's reckless navigation.[^82] Based on the fishing company general manager's testimony and unauthenticated 1987 price quotations dated 10 years after the collision, the Regional Trial Court of Caloocan City, Branch 121 ordered PNOC-STC to pay P6,438,048.00 actual damages with 6 percent annual interest from complaint filing, P50,000.00 attorney's fees and costs, which the Court of Appeals affirmed on October 14, 1992.[^83]

Modifying the Court of Appeals' decision with no pronouncement as to costs, the Supreme Court affirmed that in tort or quasi-delict actions actual damages cover all natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of, encompassing both *daño emergente* and *lucro cesante*, but held that actual loss must be proved with reasonable certainty through competent proof and the best evidence available rather than hearsay or guesswork.[^84] Because unauthenticated price quotations offered by a witness who did not prepare them were inadmissible hearsay, the Supreme Court deleted the P6,438,048.00 actual damages award for lack of evidentiary basis and awarded P2,000,000.00 in nominal damages (guided by complaint figures of P692,680.00 for nets, equipment and cargo and P600,000.00 net hull value after deducting P200,000.00 insurance from the P800,000.00 alleged vessel value), with unpaid docket fees on the amended complaint constituting a lien on the judgment.[^85] The ruling shows that Article 2202's broad causal coverage cannot rescue an actual damages award resting on hearsay documents.

### [*Mercury Drug Corp. v. Spouses Huang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43927): Lifelong Life Care Cost and Impaired Earning Capacity of a Paralyzed Minor

On December 20, 1996 along C-5 Highway in Taguig, Mercury Drug Corporation's 14,058-kilogram truck driven by Rolando J. Del Rosario, who drove with only a temporary receipt after prior license confiscation for reckless driving, swerved and struck the P300,000.00 car driven by seventeen-year-old student Stephen Huang, paralyzing him for life from the chest down.[^86] On September 29, 2004, the Regional Trial Court of Makati held Mercury Drug and Del Rosario solidarily liable for P2,973,000.00 receipted actual damages (December 1996 to December 1998), P23,461,062.00 life care cost, P10,000,000.00 impaired earning capacity, P4,000,000.00 moral damages, P2,000,000.00 exemplary damages and P1,000,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses.[^87] On February 16, 2006 (reconsideration denied March 30, 2006), the Court of Appeals affirmed all awards except reducing moral damages to P1,000,000.00.[^88]

Denying the petition and affirming the Court of Appeals with costs against petitioners, the Supreme Court invoked the rule that quasi-delict defendants are liable for all damages which are the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of.[^89] Because attending physicians gave a nil prognosis of walking or basic body functions and testified that Stephen required continuous lifelong rehabilitation to prevent fatal spinal-cord complications, the Supreme Court upheld the actuarial P23,461,062.00 life care cost based on monthly expenses and life expectancy.[^90] It also upheld the conservative P10,000,000.00 impaired earning capacity based on Stephen's age, health, leadership, academic achievements, university admissions and banking executive testimony that no bank hires persons with his disability, along with P2,973,000.00 receipted actual damages under Article 2199, P1,000,000.00 moral damages, P2,000,000.00 exemplary damages under Article 2231 for gross negligence and P1,000,000.00 attorney's fees.[^91] The landmark decision confirms that natural and probable consequences under Article 2202 encompass actuarially proved lifelong medical care and impaired future earning capacity of a permanently disabled student.

### [*National Power Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42798): Dam Flooding, Res Ipsa Loquitur and Temperate Damages

In October and November 1986, Lake Lanao water levels rose and washed away private respondents' lakeshore fishponds after National Power Corporation held back water at its Agus Regulation Dam above the 702-meter elevation mandated by Memorandum Order No. 398 and failed to maintain warning benchmarks.[^92] On July 29, 1991, the Regional Trial Court of Marawi City, Branch 9 awarded Hadji Abdul Carim Abdullah P410,000.00, Caris Abdullah P208,000.00 and Hadji Ali Langco and Diamael Pangcatan P260,000.00 in actual damages plus P20,000.00 litigation expenses and costs.[^93] On December 21, 1995 (reconsideration denied March 27, 1996), the Court of Appeals converted the awards into temperate damages of P350,000.00, P150,000.00 and P210,000.00 respectively plus P20,000.00 litigation expenses and costs.[^94]

Denying NPC's petition and affirming the Court of Appeals with costs against petitioner, the Supreme Court quoted both sentences of Article 2202 verbatim alongside [Article 2176](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2176.md).[^95] NPC's breach of Memorandum Order No. 398 and submerged benchmarks during ocular inspection triggered *res ipsa loquitur*, defeating its fortuitous-event defense because negligent dam management humanized the heavy rains and defeating *damnum absque injuria* because legal duty was violated.[^96] Because fishpond destruction was established but independent proof of exact actual loss was lacking, the Supreme Court affirmed the reduced temperate damages of P350,000.00, P150,000.00 and P210,000.00 under Articles 2224 and 2225 together with P20,000.00 litigation expenses and costs.[^97] The ruling demonstrates how Article 2202 causation pairs with temperate damages when environmental tort injury is proved but exact pecuniary loss lacks independent substantiation.

## Criminal Liability: Mandatory Adjudication, Consequential Expenses and Acquittal

In criminal prosecutions, Article 2202 operates alongside Article 100 and Article 104 of the Revised Penal Code and [Article 2206](art-2206.md) of the Civil Code to require full compensation for all natural and probable consequences of the offense.[^civil-code] [^98] [^99] The jurisprudence establishes five operational principles:
1. **Mandatory death indemnity and non-pecuniary awards:** The Supreme Court En Banc in [*People v. Oandasan, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61972)[^100] grounded mandatory civil indemnity for death directly in Article 2202. Across criminal cases, the Supreme Court has also invoked Article 2202 or its natural-and-probable-consequences rule to award civil indemnity ex delicto in child abuse under R.A. No. 7610 in [*Malto v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45300),[^101] moral damages to physically injured victims in [*People v. Enguito*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59165),[^102] loss of earning capacity in [*People v. Delavin*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/feb1987/gr_l-73762_1987.html)[^103] (and via *Heirs of Raymundo Castro v. Bustos* in [*People v. Teehankee, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/oct1995/gr_111206_1995.html)[^104]) and 6 percent legal interest under [Article 2211](art-2211.md) for delayed recovery in [*People v. Taguibuya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26305).[^105]
2. **Foreseeability-irrelevant consequential expenses:** Under the second sentence of Article 2202, proven expenses naturally resulting from the crime, such as an overseas daughter's burial travel expenses in [*People v. Dinamling*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50530)[^106], are recoverable even if unforeseen, provided they were actually shouldered by the claimants rather than a third-party employer.
3. **Strict receipt and relevance screening:** Unreceipted oral assertions, unsubstantiated doctor's prescriptions, aesthetic tomb upgrades, unrelated post-burial costs and non-immediate relatives' travel are excluded, as shown in [*People v. Degoma*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/may1992/gr_89404_1992.html),[^107] [*Fuentes, Jr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33525)[^108] and [*People v. Enguito*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59165).[^109]
4. **Mandatory judicial adjudication:** Trial judges have an express duty under Article 2202 to receive evidence on and adjudicate civil liability upon a guilty plea and cannot substitute a criminal fine for civil indemnity, as enforced in [*Corpuz v. Judge Siapno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47774).[^110]
5. **Survival after criminal acquittal:** Where acquittal rests on reasonable doubt or absence of criminal bad faith rather than non-existence of the wrongful act, Article 2202 still supports civil recovery of net lost prospective earnings under [Article 19](../preliminary-title/art-0019.md) and [Article 27](../preliminary-title/art-0027.md), as held in [*Llorente v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_85464_1991.html)[^111] (and in an independent quasi-delict suit after criminal acquittal in [*Fegarido v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68422)[^112]).

### [*People v. Oandasan, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61972): Mandatory Civil Indemnity for Death Grounded in Article 2202

Accused Mariano Oandasan, Jr. shot three victims, killing Edgardo Tamanu and Danilo Montegrico and inflicting near-fatal wounds on Mario Paleg.[^113] The Regional Trial Court and Court of Appeals convicted him of murder for Tamanu, homicide for Montegrico and frustrated homicide for Paleg, finding treachery only in Tamanu's shooting.[^114] Finding treachery in all three shootings, the Supreme Court En Banc elevated the convictions to two counts of murder (*reclusion perpetua* each) and one count of frustrated murder (8 years *prision mayor* to 14 years, 8 months and 1 day *reclusion temporal*).[^115]

Expressly citing and quoting Article 2202 verbatim, the Supreme Court En Banc held that the mandatory character of civil indemnity for death, awarded as a matter of course without proof beyond the fact of death and the accused's responsibility, derives from the legal obligation under Article 2202 to compensate the victim's heirs for death as the natural consequence of the criminal or quasi-delictual act or omission.[^116] Applying Article 2202, Article 2206, [Article 2230](art-2230.md) and *People v. Jugueta*, as cited in [*People v. Oandasan, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61972) (while rejecting a party stipulation capping Montegrico's civil indemnity at P150,000.00), the Supreme Court ordered Oandasan to pay the heirs of Montegrico and the heirs of Tamanu each P75,000.00 civil indemnity, P75,000.00 moral damages, P75,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 temperate damages while ordering him to pay Paleg P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P25,000.00 temperate damages, all earning 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until fully paid, plus costs of suit.[^117] The En Banc ruling anchors mandatory death indemnity directly to Article 2202.

### [*People v. Dinamling*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50530): Unforeseen International Burial Travel Expenses Under Article 2202

On June 8, 1995 in Cordon, Isabela, four armed accused robbed Spouses Charlie and Marilyn Pajarillo of P1,500.00 cash and store goods (P108.00 cigarettes, P96.00 sardines and P50.00 gum) and fatally shot Deogracias Acosta and Roger Malalay.[^118] The Regional Trial Court of Santiago City, Branch 35 convicted all four of robbery with double homicide and imposed the death penalty.[^119]

On automatic review, the Supreme Court En Banc affirmed convictions for robbery with homicide and death sentences and quoted both sentences of Article 2202 verbatim as applied to crimes, emphasizing that damages need not have been foreseen or reasonably foreseeable by the accused.[^120] On that basis, the Supreme Court held the four accused solidarily liable not only for P9,500.00 in receipted funeral and burial expenses for Acosta, but also for P13,402.50 in proven travel expenses incurred by Acosta's daughter Mary Grace Costales when she flew home from Malaysia for her father's burial.[^121] By contrast, the Supreme Court denied funeral and burial expenses for Malalay because his employer shouldered them.[^122] Ordering solidary liability, the Supreme Court directed restitution of P1,500.00 cash and the stolen merchandise (or their value) plus P10,000.00 exemplary damages to the Pajarillos, payment to Acosta's heirs of P75,000.00 death indemnity, P511,000.00 loss of earning capacity, P9,500.00 funeral expenses, P10,000.00 exemplary damages, P50,000.00 moral damages to widow Erlinda Acosta and P13,402.50 travel expenses to Mary Grace Costales together with payment to Malalay's heirs of P75,000.00 death indemnity, P160,000.00 loss of earning capacity and P10,000.00 exemplary damages, forwarding the records to the President upon finality.[^123] The decision is a classic application of Article 2202's second sentence allowing proven unforeseen consequential travel expenses of an immediate heir while barring double recovery of employer-paid burial costs.

### [*People v. Degoma*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/may1992/gr_89404_1992.html): Seven-Category Exclusion of Unproved Funeral Claims and Alms Offset

Efren Degoma and Marino Taborada conspired to rob Tagbilaran Friendly Bazaar on April 12, 1988, during which Taborada grappled with security guard Alexander Parilla while Degoma stole P200.00 and US$300.00 and fatally shot Parilla.[^124] On December 26, 1988, the Regional Trial Court of Tagbilaran, Bohol, Branch 2 sentenced both accused to *reclusion perpetua* for robbery with homicide and ordered them solidarily to pay the Bazaar owners P200.00 plus the peso equivalent of US$300.00 while ordering them to pay Parilla's heirs P36,000.00 death indemnity, P200,000.00 moral damages, P87,947.94 actual expenses, P5,000.00 attorney's fees and costs without subsidiary imprisonment.[^125]

Reviewing Taborada's appeal, the Supreme Court stated the rule that in delict the defendant is liable for all damages which are the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of, yet actual damages require competent proof with reasonable certainty rather than assumption or guesswork.[^126] Scrutinizing the P87,947.94 award, the Supreme Court disallowed seven categories: receipts pre-dating the killing, post-burial expenses after a long lapse unrelated to the wake or burial, purely aesthetic or social upgrades such as marble tomb lining, altered inflated receipts, unitemized expenses untied to the burial, ordinary expenses that would have been incurred anyway and expenses not borne by immediate heirs such as relatives' or in-laws' airfare, reducing gross proved expenses to P10,175.85.[^127] The Supreme Court further offset P6,400.00 in alms received by the heirs, leaving P3,775.85 (printed as P3,775,85 in the dispositive text) in net actual damages.[^128] Modifying the judgment, the Supreme Court held both accused solidarily liable for P3,775.85 actual damages, P10,000.00 moral damages (reducing the unexplained P200,000.00 award) and P50,000.00 death indemnity while affirming *reclusion perpetua*, the P200.00 plus US$300.00 equivalent to the Bazaar owners, P5,000.00 attorney's fees and trial costs, with no appeal costs.[^129] The case establishes strict evidentiary pruning and alms-offset rules for delictual actual damages under Article 2202.

### [*Corpuz v. Judge Siapno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47774): Mandatory Judicial Duty to Adjudge Civil Liability After Guilty Pleas

Clerk of Court Celestina B. Corpuz administratively charged Municipal Trial Court Presiding Judge Orlando Ana F. Siapno with Gross Ignorance of the Law for omitting civil damages in Criminal Cases Nos. 12527 and 13482 after the accused pleaded guilty, imposing only criminal fines of P33,900.00 and P49,700.00 against alleged property damages of P38,800.00, P34,700.00 and P15,000.00.[^130] Judge Siapno argued that the prosecution offered no evidence on civil liability. The Office of the Court Administrator recommended a P2,000.00 fine.[^131]

Expressly citing and quoting Article 2202 in full, the Supreme Court held that concomitant with a guilty verdict, a trial judge must adjudicate civil liability because every person criminally liable is also civilly liable for all natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of.[^132] Absent waiver, reservation or a prior separate civil action, the judge had a duty to require evidence on civil liability upon a guilty plea. Imposing a criminal fine vindicating the State could not substitute for civil indemnity to the offended party under Article 104 of the Revised Penal Code.[^133] Considering three prior administrative sanctions (indefinite suspension and two P5,000.00 fines), the Supreme Court found Judge Siapno guilty of Gross Ignorance of the Law, fined him P20,000.00 with a stern warning against repetition and dismissed all other unsubstantiated charges for lack of merit.[^134] The ruling confirms that adjudicating Article 2202 civil liability in criminal convictions is a mandatory judicial duty distinct from criminal fines.

### [*Llorente v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_85464_1991.html): Net Lost Prospective Earnings Surviving Criminal Acquittal

Philippine Coconut Authority Deputy Administrator David P. Llorente approved retirement clearances for three similarly situated employees with pending accountabilities by deducting obligations from their gratuities, yet refused for nearly five years (December 1981 to November 1986) to clear Herminigildo M. Curio over a P92,000.00 cash advance already deducted from another officer's gratuity and P10,714.78 in pending items.[^135] Without clearance, Curio failed to obtain P2,500.00 monthly employment at the Philippine Cotton Authority and Philippine Fish Marketing Administration.[^136] The Sandiganbayan Third Division acquitted Llorente of violating Section 3(c) of the Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act for lack of evidence of criminal bad faith, yet ordered him civilly liable under Articles 19 and 27 of the Civil Code for P90,000.00 compensatory damages.[^137]

Denying Llorente's petition with no pronouncement as to costs, the Supreme Court affirmed that criminal acquittal for absence of bad faith does not bar civil liability when the wrongful discriminatory act exists under Articles 19 and 27.[^138] Expressly citing Article 2202, which holds a defendant liable for all natural and probable damages, the Supreme Court upheld the P90,000.00 quantification: Curio proved lost P2,500.00 monthly salary over five years (P150,000.00 gross), from which deducting P1,000.00 monthly probable expenses (P60,000.00 over five years) yielded P90,000.00 in net actual damages.[^139] The decision confirms that Article 2202 supports net lost prospective earnings where civil liability survives criminal acquittal.

- [Article 2219](art-2219.md), [Article 2224](art-2224.md), [Article 2229](art-2229.md), [Article 2230](art-2230.md) and [Article 2231](art-2231.md): Govern moral, temperate and exemplary damages applied alongside Article 2202 in [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163),[^180] [*National Power Corp. v. ## Key Cases

- [*Banzon v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jul1989/gr_47258_1989.html): Surety prematurely executed against indemnitors' lots without paying the creditor bank while the debtors kept paying down their crop loans. The CFI held the debtors liable, but the Court of Appeals reversed and absolved them. Applying the natural-and-probable-consequences limit, the Supreme Court held that the surety's active supervening fraud broke causation from the debtors' remote default, denied the petition and affirmed absolution with no costs.[^185]
- [*Go v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/may1991/gr_68138_1991.html): A bank manager carelessly let an impostor negotiate altered treasury checks despite patent discrepancies and later filed an estafa complaint causing an innocent retiree to be investigated. The CFI awarded P6,000.00 moral, P3,000.00 exemplary and P1,000.00 attorney's fees, while the IAC reduced recovery to P3,000.00 nominal damages with 6 percent interest and P1,000.00 attorney's fees. Quoting Article 2202 in full, the Supreme Court held antecedent negligence was the root cause of the unforeseen humiliation, imposed solidary employer liability under Article 2180 and affirmed the IAC with costs.[^186]
- [*Llorente v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_85464_1991.html): A PCA deputy administrator selectively withheld a retiring employee's clearance for nearly five years, blocking P2,500.00 monthly employment. The Sandiganbayan acquitted him of anti-graft charges for lack of bad faith yet ordered P90,000.00 compensatory damages under Articles 19 and 27. Expressly applying Article 2202, the Supreme Court upheld the P90,000.00 net lost prospective earnings (P150,000.00 gross less P60,000.00 probable expenses) and denied the petition with no costs.[^187]
- [*People v. Degoma*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/may1992/gr_89404_1992.html): In a robbery with homicide, the RTC awarded P87,947.94 actual expenses, P200,000.00 moral damages and P36,000.00 death indemnity. Applying the delict natural-and-probable-consequences standard with strict evidentiary proof, the Supreme Court excluded seven categories of unproved, aesthetic or unrelated expenses, offset P6,400.00 in alms against P10,175.85 proved expenses and modified awards to P3,775.85 actual damages, P10,000.00 moral damages and P50,000.00 death indemnity while affirming *reclusion perpetua*, P200.00 plus US$300.00 equivalent and P5,000.00 attorney's fees.[^188]
- [*Atienza v. COMELEC*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_108533_1994.html): After an RTC ordered a losing election protestee to reimburse P300,856.19 in protest expenses, the COMELEC En Banc reversed for lack of any wrongful act. Applying Articles 2201 and 2202, the Supreme Court En Banc held that election protest expenses cannot be recovered as actual damages absent contractual breach, crime, quasi-delict or an express statute and dismissed the petition.[^189]
- [*Malaluan v. COMELEC*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666): The COMELEC reversed an RTC election protest decision executed pending appeal and ordered the ousted protestant to pay P257,110.00 actual damages. Quoting Article 2202 verbatim, the Supreme Court En Banc held election contest claims require a contract, quasi-contract, crime, quasi-delict or statutory basis, recognized the protestant as a *de facto* officer causing *damnum absque injuria* and voided the P257,110.00 award.[^190]
- [*PNOC Shipping and Transport Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36413): After a tanker collision sank a fishing boat, the RTC and CA awarded P6,438,048.00 actual damages based on unauthenticated price quotations. While confirming that tort actual damages under Article 2202's rule include *daño emergente* and *lucro cesante*, the Supreme Court barred hearsay price quotations, deleted the P6,438,048.00 award and granted P2,000,000.00 nominal damages with no costs.[^191]
- [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832): After dismissing ABS-CBN's contract suit, the RTC and CA awarded RBS actual damages for counterbond premiums and print ads plus moral, exemplary and attorney's fees. Reciting Article 2202 verbatim, the Supreme Court held that litigation-filing counterclaims lie outside actual damages for crimes or quasi-delicts and require proof of malice under Articles 19 to 21, reversing all awards to RBS while preserving unappealed P212,000.00 attorney's fees to VIVA.[^192]
- [*People v. Dinamling*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50530): Four armed robbers killed two victims during a store robbery and received death sentences from the RTC. Applying both sentences of Article 2202, the Supreme Court En Banc held foreseeability irrelevant and awarded not only P9,500.00 funeral expenses but also P13,402.50 proven international travel expenses for a daughter returning from Malaysia for her father's burial while denying employer-paid burial costs for the second victim.[^193]
- [*Corpuz v. Judge Siapno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47774): An MTC judge imposed only criminal fines of P33,900.00 and P49,700.00 after guilty pleas while omitting civil damages. Expressly quoting Article 2202, the Supreme Court held that judges must adjudicate civil liability upon conviction and cannot substitute fines for civil indemnity, fining the judge P20,000.00 for Gross Ignorance of the Law with a stern warning.[^194]
- [*National Power Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42798): Negligent water retention at the Agus Regulation Dam flooded lakeshore fishponds. The RTC awarded actual damages while the CA reduced awards to P350,000.00, P150,000.00 and P210,000.00 in temperate damages plus P20,000.00 litigation expenses. Quoting Article 2202 verbatim with Article 2176 and *res ipsa loquitur*, the Supreme Court rejected fortuitous-event and *damnum absque injuria* defenses, denied the petition and affirmed the CA with costs.[^195]
- [*Mercury Drug Corp. v. Spouses Huang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43927): A Mercury Drug truck paralyzed a seventeen-year-old student for life. The RTC awarded P2,973,000.00 receipted actual damages, P23,461,062.00 life care cost, P10,000,000.00 impaired earning capacity, P4,000,000.00 moral damages, P2,000,000.00 exemplary damages and P1,000,000.00 attorney's fees, which the CA affirmed while lowering moral damages to P1,000,000.00. Applying the natural-and-probable-consequences rule, the Supreme Court upheld those awards and denied the petition with costs.[^196]
- [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163): A bus encroached into the opposite lane and hit respondents' truck. The RTC awarded repair, daily unrealized income, moral, exemplary and attorney's fees while the CA deleted daily income. Harmonizing Articles 2202 and 2199, the Supreme Court held claimants must prove both natural-and-probable causation and adequate amount, maintaining P142,757.40 repairs, adding P11,267.35 receipted medical expenses with interest from January 31, 2001, maintaining P50,000.00 exemplary damages and costs and deleting moral damages and attorney's fees.[^197]
- [*People v. Oandasan, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61972): Reviewing convictions for three shootings elevated to two murders and one frustrated murder, the Supreme Court En Banc expressly grounded mandatory civil indemnity for death in Article 2202's obligation to compensate heirs for death as the natural consequence of the crime and fixed standardized civil indemnity, moral, exemplary and temperate awards with 6 percent annual interest from finality and costs.[^198]
- [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Company and Intergames, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921): A minor runner was killed by a reckless jeepney during a marathon staged on an unblocked highway. The RTC held organizer and sponsor solidarily liable while the CA dismissed the suit. Explicitly applying Article 2202, the Supreme Court held the jeepney's negligence was not an efficient intervening cause, absolved financial sponsor Cosmos as too remote and reinstated the RTC judgment against organizer Intergames with added P113,484.52 loss of earning capacity, 10 percent attorney's fees on the total, interest from May 10, 1991, compounded 6 percent interest from finality and costs.[^199]

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1.** Organizer Z staged a youth road race along an active city highway without closing traffic lanes or posting marshals at intersections. During the race, a speeding jeepney struck and killed Runner R, an 18-year-old non-working high school student. Beverage Company B had provided cash sponsorship for the event but took no part in planning, routing or securing the course. R's parents sued both Organizer Z and Beverage Company B for damages under Article 2202 of the Civil Code. Organizer Z argues that the jeepney driver's recklessness was an efficient intervening cause relieving it of liability and that no loss of earning capacity can be awarded because R was unemployed. Decide the liabilities of Organizer Z and Beverage Company B.

**Suggested Answer:** Organizer Z is liable for all natural and probable damages including loss of earning capacity, whereas Beverage Company B should be absolved. Under Article 2202 of the Civil Code, as applied in [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Company and Intergames, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921),[^200] a defendant in a quasi-delict is liable for all damages which are the natural and probable consequences of its negligent act or omission even if not foreseen. An intervening third-party act is not an efficient intervening cause when the risk it presents, here, a vehicular collision on an unblocked road, was reasonably foreseeable and anticipated by the original wrongdoer.[^201] Because Organizer Z's failure to secure the highway remained the proximate cause of R's death, it answers for receipted actual damages, moral and exemplary damages and loss of earning capacity computed from prevailing minimum wage under Article 2202 in relation to Article 2206 even though R was a non-working minor.[^202] Beverage Company B, however, cannot be held liable because mere financial sponsorship without participation in race organization or safety has no direct or immediate causal link and is too remote to be the proximate cause of death.[^203]

**Question 2.** During an armed robbery of a store, Accused X shot and killed Storekeeper S. At trial, S's heirs proved through official receipts P10,000.00 in funeral expenses and P15,000.00 in airfare paid by S's daughter who flew home from abroad specifically to attend her father's burial. The heirs also submitted receipts for P40,000.00 spent months later on marble tomb lining and admitted receiving P4,000.00 in funeral alms. Accused X objects that he could not have foreseen that S had a daughter living abroad and argues that the marble tomb lining should be disallowed and the alms deducted. Resolve each objection.

**Suggested Answer:** Accused X is liable for the daughter's proven overseas travel expenses, while the marble tomb lining must be disallowed and the alms offset against gross proved funeral expenses. Under the second sentence of Article 2202 of the Civil Code, as applied in [*People v. Dinamling*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50530),[^204] a criminal defendant is liable for all natural and probable consequences of the crime even if those damages were not foreseen or could not have been reasonably foreseen. Proven travel expenses incurred by an immediate heir returning from abroad for the victim's burial are recoverable consequential damages.[^205] However, as held in [*People v. Degoma*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/may1992/gr_89404_1992.html),[^206] actual damages in delict exclude purely aesthetic or social upgrades such as marble tomb lining and post-burial expenses after a long lapse. Alms received by the victim's heirs must also be deducted from gross proved funeral expenses.

**Question 3.** Truck Driver T negligently rammed Company C's delivery van and injured its driver. Company C sued T and his employer for quasi-delict, submitting official receipts for P150,000.00 in van repairs and presenting only an unauthenticated third-party price quotation and oral testimony to support an additional claim of P500,000.00 for unrealized business profits. Can Company C recover both P150,000.00 and P500,000.00 as actual damages under Article 2202?

**Suggested Answer:** Company C can recover the receipted repairs but cannot recover the unsubstantiated unrealized profits as actual damages. Under [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163),[^207] Article 2202 must be read with Article 2199: although a quasi-delict defendant answers for all natural and probable consequences regardless of foreseeability, a claimant seeking actual or compensatory damages must prove both natural-and-probable causation and the adequate amount of pecuniary loss. As held in [*PNOC Shipping and Transport Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36413),[^208] while actual damages cover both *daño emergente* and *lucro cesante*, unauthenticated price quotations are inadmissible hearsay and courts cannot award actual damages on speculation or guesswork. Consequently, only the receipted repair cost qualifies as actual damages, while the unsubstantiated lost-profit claim must be denied.

# Quotes

> "Article 2202 of the Civil Code provides that in crimes and quasi- delicts, the defendant shall be liable for all damages which are the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of. It is not necessary that such damages have been foreseen or could have reasonably been foreseen by the defendant. Article 2199 of the same Code, however, sets the limitation that, except as provided by law or by stipulation, one is entitled to an adequate compensation only for such pecuniary loss suffered by him as he has duly proved. As such, to warrant an award of actual or compensatory damages, the claimant must prove that the damage sustained is the natural and probable consequences of the negligent act and, moreover, the claimant must adequately prove the amount of such damage."[^209]

> "Under Article 2199 of the Civil Code, actual or compensatory damages are those awarded in satisfaction of, or in recompense for, loss or injury sustained. In actions based on torts or quasi-delicts, actual damages include all the natural and probable consequences of the act or omission complained of. There are two kinds of actual or compensatory damages: one is the loss of what a person already possesses (daño emergente), and the other is the failure to receive as a benefit that which would have pertained to him (lucro cesante). # Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Diagnostic Checklist for Article 2202 Claims**
> 1. **Confirm the source of obligation first:** Verify that the claim arises from a crime or quasi-delict rather than a contract governed by Article 2201, an election contest ([*Atienza v. COMELEC*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_108533_1994.html) and [*Malaluan v. COMELEC*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666)) or a non-malicious civil suit ([*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832)).
> 2. **Test proximate causation and intervening acts:** Ask whether the defendant's wrongful act remained the root cause despite foreseeable third-party conduct or weather ([*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Company and Intergames, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921), [*Go v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/may1991/gr_68138_1991.html) and [*Davao del Norte Electric Cooperative v. Heirs of Victorino Lucas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68895)) or whether an active supervening fraud broke the causal chain ([*Banzon v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jul1989/gr_47258_1989.html)).
> 3. **Dismiss foreseeability defenses in crimes and quasi-delicts:** Once proximate causation is proved, the defendant cannot evade liability by arguing that the exact loss, such as an overseas heir's burial airfare ([*People v. Dinamling*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50530)) or downstream investigation embarrassment ([*Go v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/may1991/gr_68138_1991.html)), was unforeseeable.
> 4. **Audit proof of quantum under Article 2199:** Confirm that every actual-damages peso is backed by official receipts, authenticated documents or actuarial medical and income records ([*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163), [*PNOC Shipping and Transport Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36413) and [*Mercury Drug Corp. v. Spouses Huang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43927)). If pecuniary loss is certain but exact figures fail strict proof, consider temperate or nominal damages ([*National Power Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42798) and [*Josefa v. Manila Electric Company*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57240)).
> 5. **Check criminal-to-civil survival and mandatory adjudication:** Trial judges must adjudge civil liability upon guilty pleas rather than imposing fines alone ([*Corpuz v. Judge Siapno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47774)), and acquittal on reasonable doubt or absence of criminal bad faith does not extinguish civil liability for natural and probable damages ([*Llorente v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_85464_1991.html) and [*Fegarido v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68422)).

### Common Traps

> [!NOTE]
> **Confusing Article 2201 Foreseeability with Article 2202 Liability:** Foreseeability limits recovery only for a good-faith contractual obligor under Article 2201. In crimes and quasi-delicts under Article 2202, unforeseeable consequences remain compensable so long as they naturally and probably flow from the wrong ([*People v. Dinamling*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50530) and [*Go v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/may1991/gr_68138_1991.html)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Assuming Article 2202 Relaxes Article 2199 Proof Rules:** Proving that an injury is a natural and probable consequence satisfies causation only. Courts consistently delete actual damages resting on unauthenticated price quotations, hearsay accounting summaries, unreceipted oral estimates or speculative future liabilities ([*PNOC Shipping and Transport Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36413), [*Josefa v. Manila Electric Company*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57240), [*Fuentes, Jr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33525) and [*De Vera, Jr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52770)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Treating a Criminal Fine or Adverse Lawsuit as Article 2202 Damages:** A criminal fine paid to the State never replaces civil indemnity owed to the victim under Article 2202 ([*Corpuz v. Judge Siapno*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47774)). Conversely, winning a civil suit or election protest does not trigger actual damages under Article 2202 without proof of a crime, quasi-delict, breach of contract or malicious abuse of right ([*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832), [*Atienza v. COMELEC*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_108533_1994.html) and [*Malaluan v. COMELEC*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666)).

### What Worked and Did Not Work in Decided Cases

- **What worked to establish major future pecuniary losses:** In [*Mercury Drug Corp. v. Spouses Huang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43927), uniform specialist prognoses, documented monthly rehabilitation expenses, actuarial life-expectancy computations and banking executive testimony sustained P23,461,062.00 in life care cost and P10,000,000.00 in impaired earning capacity. In [*Llorente v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_85464_1991.html), proving specific lost monthly salaries less probable living expenses established P90,000.00 in net lost prospective earnings.
- **What worked to prove environmental crop and flood losses:** In [*Sanggacala v. National Power Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67780), combining tax declarations, flood photographs, prior court awards for similarly situated farmers and uncontroverted testimonies sustained multi-year actual crop damages. Where exact fishpond figures lacked independent proof in [*National Power Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42798), proving dam-level violations and inundation still secured temperate damages of P350,000.00, P150,000.00 and P210,000.00.
- **What failed in wake, burial and medical actual-damages claims:** In [*People v. Degoma*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/may1992/gr_89404_1992.html), altered receipts, pre-death receipts, aesthetic marble tomb lining, delayed post-burial costs and non-immediate relatives' airfare were rejected and funeral alms were deducted. In [*People v. Enguito*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59165), doctor's prescriptions without payment receipts and unreceipted vigil or 40th-day expenses failed. In [*People v. Dinamling*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50530), burial costs already shouldered by the victim's employer were denied.
- **What failed to avoid liability through intervening-cause or assumption-of-risk defenses:** In [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Company and Intergames, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921), blaming a reckless jeepney on an unblocked race course failed for the organizer (though remoteness absolved the financial sponsor). In [*Davao del Norte Electric Cooperative v. Heirs of Victorino Lucas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68895), blaming strong winds and a flying metal sheet failed where the utility's power lines were already neglected.
