> ART. 2200. Indemnification for damages shall comprehend not only the value of the loss suffered, but also that of the profits which the obligee failed to obtain. (1106)[^civil-code]

# Explanation

## The two branches of indemnification

Article 2200 names two forms of pecuniary damage. *Damnum emergens* is the value of what the injured party has lost from an existing estate. *Lucrum cessans* is the benefit the obligee would have received but did not obtain. The Supreme Court describes both as components of actual or compensatory damages.[^2] [^3]

A claim should identify which branch each amount belongs to. Repair or replacement cost, destroyed property and money lost are examples of the first branch. Revenue or profits lost during a delay, breach or wrongful interference fall under the second. A single event may cause both kinds of loss, but each amount needs its own factual and evidentiary basis.

Article 2200 states what kinds of pecuniary harm may be indemnified. It does not dispense with proof. Article 2199 requires proof of the pecuniary loss for actual or compensatory damages. The Supreme Court has tied recovery under Article 2200 to evidence supporting the loss and its amount.[^4] [^5] A finding that the defendant acted wrongfully does not by itself establish every amount claimed.

In [*Filipino Society of Composers, Authors and Publishers, Inc. v. Anrey, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68646) (G.R. No. 233918), the Supreme Court stated that Article 2200 covers both the loss of what a person possesses and the failure to receive a benefit. The decision concerned unlicensed performance of copyrighted songs. The final disposition awarded temperate damages rather than the claimed license-fee measure, along with attorney’s fees and interest.[^6] This illustrates the difference between proving that a compensable injury occurred and proving a precise actual-damages amount.

## Existing loss and asset valuation

For a loss to existing property or funds, prove the claimant’s interest in the asset, the event that caused the loss and a defensible measure of value. The appropriate measure depends on the proven loss. The claim must connect the harm to the defendant’s actionable conduct.

The case [*Province of Bataan v. Escalada, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67917) (G.R. No. 181311) provides a substantial-loss example. The Province of Bataan and a paper-products company were held jointly and severally liable after machinery and equipment belonging to the claimant were taken and destroyed while litigation was pending. The trial court’s findings on the destruction and the Province’s responsibility were not effectively challenged. The Supreme Court affirmed liability and fixed actual damages at PHP 120 million, with interest as specified in its disposition.[^7] The result rested on identified machinery, findings of actual destruction and an uncontroverted causal account. It does not establish that a claimant’s estimate alone proves property loss.

Article 2200 is compensatory. The Supreme Court has explained that actual or compensatory damages repair the wrong and compensate for injury rather than impose a penalty.[^8] The claimant must distinguish restoration of an existing interest from punishment or an award untethered to measured harm.

[*Universal International Investment (BVI) Limited v. Ray Burton Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62634) (G.R. No. 182201) illustrates the boundary between lost profits and a decline in asset value. The claimant sought PHP 19,646,483.72 for a reduction in the market value of condominium units under Article 2200. The Supreme Court rejected that theory because the claimant did not allege that it marketed the units for profit through lease or sale. Article 2200 could not be used as a general substitute for the value of the units where the alleged decline was not shown to represent profits the claimant would have earned.[^9] The practical inquiry is what interest the claimant actually lost, not which damages label produces the largest figure.

Article 2200’s first branch also differs from separate statutory or employment compensation. [*Oceanmarine Resources Corporation v. Nedic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68469) (G.R. No. 236263) distinguished compensation payable under Article 1711 and the Labor Code’s State Insurance Fund from compensatory damages recoverable in an action for breach of contract or tort. The decision referred to Articles 2199 and 2200 when discussing the damages calculation in the underlying authorities. It cautions against treating every payment triggered by a death or workplace injury as Article 2200 damages.[^10]

## Lost profits require a grounded counterfactual

Lost profits are inherently counterfactual. The claimant must establish a reasonable basis to conclude that the benefit probably would have been received absent the breach or wrong. Then the claimant must provide a sufficiently definite method for estimating it. The Supreme Court has said absolute certainty is not required, but speculation, conjecture and surmise are insufficient. A reasonably definite standard may come from market value, established experience or direct inference from known circumstances.[^11]

[*San Miguel Foods, Inc. v. Magtuto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65431) (G.R. No. 225007) stated that standard in a dispute involving actual or compensatory damages. The Supreme Court awarded PHP 38,383.58 and clarified that a lost-profit amount need not be proven with absolute certainty when the record supports a reasonably definite estimate. The case rejects two extremes. A claimant need not predict the future with mathematical precision, but a court cannot fill evidentiary gaps with conjecture.[^12]

[*Central Bank of the Philippines v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/apr1975/gr_33022_1975.html)[^13] (G.R. No. L-33022) framed the question in two parts: did the breach cause profits not to be made, and is there a basis to determine those unearned profits with reasonable certainty? It recognized recovery of expected profit where the evidence established breach and a reasonable basis for finding that the claimant would have earned it. [*General Enterprises, Inc. v. Lianga Bay Logging Company, Inc.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1964/aug1964/gr_l-18487_1964.html)[^14] (G.R. No. L-18487) likewise stated the reasonable-certainty inquiry but modified the award before it, leaving only exemplary damages in the disposition. The statement of a legal test must be read alongside the result in the case.

[*Radio Communications of the Philippines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/feb1981/gr_55194_1981.html) (G.R. No. L-55194) described these as the two components of actual or compensatory damages, using *damnum emergens* for loss suffered and *lucrum cessans* for profits not obtained. It also reduced the total damages award in its disposition. Article 2200’s inclusion of lost profits therefore does not mean every asserted future earning is compensable.[^15]

## Evidence and methods of calculation

### Use business records and concrete indicators

A reliable lost-profit calculation begins with evidence of the business or earning activity that would have produced the benefit. Useful indicators in the extracted decisions include prior earnings, lease income, project studies, contracts, market prices, productive activity and testimony tested in the proceedings. The claim should explain the period of loss and show how the evidence supports the requested amount. This is an evidentiary approach from these decisions, not a universal formula.

[*San Mauricio Mining Company v. Ancheta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/jul1981/gr_47859_57132_1981.html) (G.R. Nos. L-47859 and L-57132) concerned a project whose operation was delayed after adverse claims were annotated on titles. The decision discussed a project study and the general manager’s testimony estimating gains during the delayed years. The quoted trial-court reasoning noted that the defendants did not show the projected gains were incorrect and had waived cross-examination on the study. The decision’s disposition included findings and monetary relief for the plaintiffs. The case shows how projections can be evaluated against project-specific evidence and contested testimony. It does not make an untested projection sufficient in every case.[^16]

In [*Consolidated Dairy Products Co. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/aug1992/gr_100401_1992.html) (G.R. No. 100401), the Supreme Court rejected the use of a single prior year’s profit as the measure for all future years. It instead used average yearly profits over a five-year period and applied that average to the remaining contract period, arriving at PHP 5,205,478.80. The calculation relied on demonstrated earnings over multiple years rather than an assumption that one favorable year would repeat unchanged.[^17]

[*Woodchild Holdings, Inc. v. Roxas Electric and Construction Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45536)[^18] (G.R. No. 140667) recognized PHP 3,900,000 in unearned income from a lease and treated it as recoverable under Articles 2200 and 2201. It provides an example of a lost contractual income stream rather than an abstract assertion of opportunity. [*Talampas v. Moldex Realty, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60637)[^19] (G.R. No. 170134) likewise awarded PHP 1,723,125.01 as the cost of opportunity lost after a unilateral contract termination, in addition to equipment rental for the specified period. A lost opportunity can qualify when the record identifies the opportunity and supports its value.

[*Bachelor Express, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/jul1990/gr_85691_1990.html) (G.R. No. 85691) addressed loss of earning capacity and considered evidence of a carpenter’s earnings, the seasonal nature of the work and deductions in arriving at the amount. The example shows the importance of grounding a future-earnings measure in the claimant’s actual work and circumstances.[^20]

### Deduct costs and account for contingencies

A projection of gross sales is not necessarily a projection of profit. A claimant should account for expenses required to earn the revenue and for evidence affecting the likelihood, timing or amount of performance. The extracted decisions support the need for a reasonable evidentiary basis, but the pack does not provide one accounting formula applicable to every business.

[*Isabelo Moran v. Mariano Pecson*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/oct1984/gr_l-59956_1984.html) (G.R. No. L-59956) makes the point. One partner sought damages based on profits that a partnership supposedly would have earned. The Supreme Court distinguished an earlier construction-business decision where the record showed a profitable business and actual profits from government contracts. In the case before it, there was no evidence that the partnership would have been profitable. The Court called the venture “a failure doomed from the start” and rejected speculative damages. Its disposition nevertheless ordered repayment of the unused PHP 6,000 contribution and payment of PHP 3,000, one half of net profits already earned from selling 2,000 posters.[^21] The holding distinguishes profits actually realized from projected profits unsupported by the venture’s record.

In [*Estate of Kneebone v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/nov1988/gr_l-77109_1988.html) (G.R. No. L-77109), the Supreme Court accepted that delayed payment prevented employees from investing retirement benefits as planned. The claimed investment profits were speculative and excessive, so a more realistic approximation was necessary. The Court affirmed an award equal to 14% of the retirement benefits as a fair and reasonable approximation covering actual and moral damages. That result rests on the case’s record and is not a general percentage formula for lost investment returns.[^22]

### Show the evidence, not only a conclusion

The Supreme Court has rejected income claims where the claimant supplied no receipts or other adequate proof. [*Siguenza v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1985/jul1985/gr_l-44050_1985.html) (G.R. No. L-44050) recounted a lost-income claim rejected as speculative because no receipt or other evidence proved it. It reiterated the need to present the best evidence obtainable, and described the challenged PHP 54,000 assertion and accompanying documents as inadequate.[^23]

[*Seavan Carrier, Inc. v. GTI Sportswear Corporation*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-62130_1984.html)[^24] (G.R. No. L-62130) deleted a PHP 2.4 million award for lost goodwill after reiterating the need for the best evidence obtainable. [*G.A. Machineries, Inc. v. Yaptinchay*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1983/nov1983/gr_l-30965_1983.html)[^25] (G.R. No. L-30965) deleted an award of PHP 54,000.48 in actual damages and stated that the plaintiff must prove the case to recover loss and prospective profits. These decisions show that an amount written into a claim or supported by inadequate materials does not become compensable because Article 2200 recognizes the category.

[*Rosendo C. Carticiano and Zacarias A. Carticiano v. Mario Nuval*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37032) (G.R. No. 138054) is a direct warning against vague opportunity claims. The Supreme Court deleted a PHP 100,000 award for lost “income or opportunities.” That result distinguishes a demonstrable earning stream from a broad claim that some unidentified opportunity was lost.[^26]

## Causation, foreseeability and the source of the obligation

Article 2200 does not itself establish liability. A claimant must identify the obligation or legal duty that was breached and show that the breach caused the claimed loss. The source of the obligation then constrains which consequences are recoverable.

In [*Orient Freight International, Inc. v. Keihin-Everett Forwarding Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63503) (G.R. No. 191937), the Supreme Court distinguished contractual negligence from quasi-delict. It explained that negligence in performing an obligation ordinarily gives rise to contractual liability, while an independent quasi-delict claim may exist in some circumstances if the act would support such a claim even without the contract. In discussing contractual damages, the decision reproduced Articles 2200 and 2201. Article 2200 permits prospective profits as a category. Article 2201 addresses whether the consequences are natural and probable and were or could have been foreseen when the obligation was constituted, with a broader rule for fraud, bad faith, malice or wanton conduct.[^27]

The distinction matters in practice. The same loss might be pleaded under different legal theories, but the theory determines the applicable liability rules. Article 2200 provides a damages component, not a shortcut around proving the underlying cause of action. [*Orient Freight*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63503) discussed Articles 2200 and 2201 in the context of contractual obligations.[^28]

[*Province of Bataan v. Escalada, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67917) (G.R. No. 181311) illustrates the sequence. The Supreme Court first relied on findings that the Province took and destroyed identified machinery and that it did not disprove its responsibility. It then affirmed PHP 120 million in actual damages. The case did not treat Article 2200 as proof of fault or causation by itself.[^29]

[*Oceanmarine Resources Corporation v. Nedic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68469) (G.R. No. 236263) also keeps compensation concepts distinct. It explained that Article 1711 compensation, now addressed through the Labor Code’s system, is different from compensatory damages recoverable through an action for breach of contract or tort. The decision discussed Articles 2199 and 2200 in describing the damages calculation in prior case law. A claimant must identify the remedy being sought and avoid merging separate statutory compensation with damages without a basis in the record.[^30]

## Avoid duplication and select the measure supported by proof

The same economic injury should not be recovered twice under different labels. In [*Security Bank and Trust Company v. Rizal Commercial Banking Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48743) (G.R. No. 170984), a bank’s separate claim for lost interest income on the unpaid balance was already covered by the 6% legal-interest award under Articles 2200 and 2209. The Supreme Court rejected an additional interest-income award on that basis while ordering payment of the remaining principal and other damages.[^31]

The distinction between actual damages and another remedy when loss is certain but its amount is not also matters. In [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060)[^32] (G.R. No. 225438), the Supreme Court deleted a PHP 30 million lost-profit award and substituted PHP 4 million in temperate damages. In [*Filipino Society of Composers, Authors and Publishers, Inc. v. Anrey, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68646)[^33] (G.R. No. 233918), it awarded temperate damages for unlicensed public performance rather than the claimed license-fee measure. These dispositions do not authorize a court to relabel an unsupported claim automatically. They illustrate that another category may fit when pecuniary injury is established but the exact amount claimed is not.

In [*Ricardo B. Bangayan v. Rizal Commercial Banking Corporation and Philip Saria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37779) (G.R. No. 149193), the Supreme Court recited the compensatory purpose of actual damages but affirmed dismissal of the claimant’s damages complaint. The standard is not satisfied by citing the statute alone.[^34]

A disciplined damages schedule should state, for every item, the loss category, legal basis, causal link, evidence and calculation. It should also identify related awards that might overlap. A claimant seeking both actual loss and lost profits should itemize them separately. A claimant seeking legal interest and lost interest income should explain why the amounts compensate different harm. The court can then award only what the evidence and applicable law support.

## Suggested Answers

**Q1: Hypothetical: A claimant proves that the defendant destroyed identified machinery, but claims projected profits without showing orders, prior operations or production capacity. What can Article 2200 support?**

The proved value of the machinery may fall within the value of loss suffered if evidence supports its amount and causation. The lost-profit claim needs a separate basis showing the claimant probably would have earned the profits and a reasonably definite method of estimating them. Proof of the destroyed asset does not automatically prove projected earnings. [*Province of Bataan v. Escalada, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67917)[^65] (G.R. No. 181311) involved identified machinery and a supported finding of destruction. [*San Miguel Foods, Inc. v. Magtuto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65431)[^66] (G.R. No. 225007) requires more than a bare projection.

**Q2: Hypothetical: A business has five years of earnings records, but one year was unusually profitable. The defendant’s breach prevents operation for the remaining contract term. How should the claimant frame lost profits?**

The claimant should connect the breach to the period of lost operation and explain why historical data provide a reasonable estimate of future net profits. [*Consolidated Dairy Products Co. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/aug1992/gr_100401_1992.html) (G.R. No. 100401) used an average across several prior years rather than assuming a single year’s profit would recur. The calculation should account for expenses and evidence that the remaining period would differ.[^67]

**Q3: Hypothetical: A new partnership claims several years of future profit based on an optimistic business plan, but it has no operating history or proof of demand. Is Article 2200 enough?**

No. Article 2200 recognizes lost profits as a compensable kind of loss, but the claimant must show a reasonable basis for concluding the profits would have been earned. [*Isabelo Moran v. Mariano Pecson*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/oct1984/gr_l-59956_1984.html) (G.R. No. L-59956) rejected projected profits for a venture with no evidence that it would be profitable. The same decision allowed recovery of an unused contribution and a share of profits already earned on poster sales.[^68]

**Q4: Hypothetical: Property has declined in market value, but the claimant has not shown that it was held for lease or sale as a source of profit. Can the reduction be claimed as lost profits?**

Not on those facts alone. In [*Universal International Investment (BVI) Limited v. Ray Burton Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62634) (G.R. No. 182201), the Supreme Court rejected the attempt to use Article 2200 to recover a reduction in condominium value where the claimant did not allege that it marketed the units for profit. The claimant must identify whether the asserted amount is loss to an existing asset or profit that would have been earned, then support that category with evidence.[^69]

**Q5: Hypothetical: A contract is terminated early. The claimant proves rent paid to obtain substitute equipment and a defined opportunity lost under the contract. May both amounts be included?**

Potentially, if each amount represents a distinct loss, was caused by the termination and is supported by evidence. [*Talampas v. Moldex Realty, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60637) (G.R. No. 170134) separately awarded equipment rent for a specified period and a cost-of-opportunity amount. The claimant should disclose any overlap and explain how each figure was calculated.[^70]

**Q6: Hypothetical: The principal sum is already earning legal interest, and the claimant also requests the same amount as lost interest income. What should the court examine?**

It should determine whether the lost-interest claim compensates an additional injury or duplicates the legal-interest award. [*Security Bank and Trust Company v. Rizal Commercial Banking Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48743) (G.R. No. 170984) treated the separate lost-interest claim as already covered by legal interest awarded under Articles 2200 and 2209. A second award for the same economic loss would duplicate compensation.[^71]

**Q7: Hypothetical: A claimant proves pecuniary injury, but the record cannot support the exact lost-profit figure demanded. Must the court award that figure or nothing?**

No. The exact claim should not be accepted without proof. The remedy must fit the injury and evidence. [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060)[^72] (G.R. No. 225438) deleted the unsupported PHP 30 million lost-profit award and substituted PHP 4 million in temperate damages. [*Filipino Society of Composers, Authors and Publishers, Inc. v. Anrey, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68646)[^73] (G.R. No. 233918) also awarded temperate damages instead of the claimed license-fee measure.

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Article 2200 diagnostic checklist:** Identify each amount as existing loss or profit not obtained. Establish the underlying obligation or wrong, prove causation, identify the evidence supporting each figure, explain the calculation, check foreseeability where Article 2201 applies and remove any amount already covered by another award.

### Separate loss from profit

> [!TIP]
> List damaged property, expenses and lost earnings as separate items. Tie each to its own facts and measure. Do not use a decrease in asset value as a lost-profit measure without showing the earning activity that would have produced the profit.

### Build the lost-profit counterfactual

> [!TIP]
> State what would have happened absent the breach. Support that account with operating history, contracts, orders, market indicators, project evidence or another grounded measure. Explain costs and assumptions.

### Test the quality of proof

> [!NOTE]
> Ask whether the evidence is the best reasonably available for the specific loss. A pleaded number or unsupported estimate does not establish actual damages.

### Match the remedy to the record

> [!TIP]
> If pecuniary injury is shown but the exact lost-profit amount is uncertain, explain why the requested actual-damages figure remains unproven and whether another damages category fits the proven facts.

### Check for overlap

> [!NOTE]
> Compare lost interest or opportunity costs with principal, legal interest and other awards. Identify and remove duplicate recovery.
