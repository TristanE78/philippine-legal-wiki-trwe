> ART. 2201. In contracts and quasi-contracts, the damages for which the obligor who acted in good faith is liable shall be those that are the natural and probable consequences of the breach of the obligation, and which the parties have foreseen or could have reasonably foreseen at the time the obligation was constituted.
>
> In case of fraud, bad faith, malice or wanton attitude, the obligor shall be responsible for all damages which may be reasonably attributed to the non-performance of the obligation. (1107a)[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 2201 determines which losses an obligor must answer for after breach of a contract or quasi-contract. The first paragraph measures the liability of an obligor who acted in good faith. The second reaches losses reasonably attributable to non-performance when the obligor acted with fraud, bad faith, malice or a wanton attitude.[^2]

**In brief**

1. Identify the obligation that went unperformed. Article 2201 concerns contracts and quasi-contracts.[^3]
2. For good faith, ask whether the loss was a natural and probable consequence of the breach. Then ask what the parties foresaw or could reasonably have foreseen when they constituted the obligation. Both conditions matter.[^4]
3. For fraud, bad faith, malice or a wanton attitude, identify the conduct that supports that finding. Trace the claimed loss to the non-performance.[^5]
4. Test the evidence for each item claimed. A record can show that a breach cost the claimant business yet leave the amount of its lost profits uncertain.[^6]

## The loss must fall within the applicable paragraph

The time specified in the first paragraph matters. The parties might learn after the breach that delay or nondelivery had serious consequences. The inquiry concerns what they foresaw or could reasonably have foreseen when they undertook the obligation. The second paragraph asks which losses may reasonably be attributed to the non-performance. Bad faith changes the reach of liability. Causation still matters.[^7]

Three questions recur in the decisions. What did the obligor undertake to do? Which loss did the breach cause? What evidence supports the relief claimed? The distinction between the last two questions matters when the loss consists of future sales or earnings.[^8]

## The source of the obligation controls the measure

An award cannot rest on the contractual measure simply because the lawsuit grew out of negotiations. In [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832)[^9] the counterclaim was based on the alleged wrongful filing of the complaint. The Supreme Court distinguished that claim from damages arising from contract or quasi-contract and found inadequate proof of malice or bad faith under Articles 19, 20 and 21.[^10]

## The source of liability in election protests

An election judgment can settle entitlement to office without creating an obligation to reimburse the winner’s expenses. In [*Atienza v. Commission on Elections*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_108533_1994.html)[^11] dismissal of the appeal after the term expired did not revive the vacated trial judgment awarding Atienza his protest expenses. He identified no specific law supporting his claim against Sia in the absence of a contract, quasi-contract or wrongful act.[^12]

Execution pending appeal also does not establish wrongful occupation of office. In [*Malaluan v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666)[^13] Malaluan assumed office under a trial court order after posting a bond. The Supreme Court found no breach of obligation, tort, crime or specific law supporting an award to Evangelista for fees, copying expenses and unearned emoluments. Expiration of the term mooted the issue of office but left the damages ruling open to review.[^14]

## Causation and intervening acts

### [*Banzon v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jul1989/gr_47258_1989.html): An intervening act can break the chain of causation

An unpaid crop loan set events in motion. The borrower had obtained the loans from a bank and a surety had guaranteed payment. The surety obtained judgment against an indemnitor and took two of his properties in execution without first paying the bank.[^15]

The indemnitors argued that payment by the borrower would have spared them the loss. The Supreme Court accepted that nonpayment was a remote cause. It identified the surety’s premature suit and ensuing fraud as the acts that directly caused the prejudice. The evidence also failed to show bad faith by the borrower and his wife.[^16]

On review of the appellate judgment absolving the couple, the Supreme Court denied the petition without pronouncement as to costs. The directions for restoration of property reproduced in the opinion came from an earlier proceeding.[^17]

## Delivery, possession and the source of the claim

A seller who knows that its goods are on another party’s premises may need to arrange access before delivery. In [*Asset Privatization Trust v. T.J. Enterprises*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48964)[^18] the seller’s failure to arrange removal left machinery beyond the purchaser’s reach. The Supreme Court rejected the defense of fortuitous event and expressly agreed with the appellate findings allowing actual damages as pleaded and proved even on the assumption that the obstruction qualified as such an event.[^19]

The claimant must establish a breach before the court can measure contractual damages. In [*Nakpil v. Manila Towers Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42756)[^20] tenants had prevented their lessor from repairing the building. The lessee did not abandon his premises and failed to prove constructive eviction or that city laborers took his belongings.[^21]

The source of the obligation also matters. In [*Public Estates Authority v. Chu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43722)[^22] the Supreme Court distinguished the measure for contractual loss from the land claimant’s cause of action for abuse of rights under Article 19. Destruction of pepper plants established pecuniary loss, but photographs and the claimant’s testimony did not prove the expenses and profits claimed.[^23]

## Completion costs and delay penalties

### [*Vil-Rey Planners and Builders v. Lexber, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61984): The price of finishing the work

The cost of hiring a replacement contractor can measure the loss caused by incomplete performance. In [*Vil-Rey Planners and Builders v. Lexber, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61984)[^24] the parties made the final half of the price payable when the contractor finished the work. An extension and another five days passed without completion.[^25]

The owner then paid another contractor P284,084.46 to finish the job. The Supreme Court found no clear showing of bad faith. It allowed the completion cost as a natural and probable consequence of the breach.[^26]

On the contractor’s and surety’s Rule 45 petitions, the Supreme Court modified the appellate judgment. The contractor and surety owed the owner P284,084.46 jointly and severally, with 6% annual interest from 17 February 1997 until full payment and attorney’s fees of 10% of that principal. The contractor had to indemnify the surety for whatever amount the surety paid the owner.[^27]

The owner also had to account for its own short payment. It owed the contractor P84,364.19 with 6% annual interest from 24 December 1996 until full payment. The owner and contractor could compensate the amounts due to the extent of their respective obligations.[^28]

### [*Continental Cement Corporation v. Asea Brown Boveri, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32714): What the repairer could foresee about a spare motor

An award for delayed repairs turns on what the repairer could reasonably foresee when it accepted the work. In [*Continental Cement Corporation v. Asea Brown Boveri, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32714)[^29] the cement producer sent a spare kiln drive motor for repair. The repairer failed to show that it had furnished the producer with the terms purporting to exclude consequential damages.[^30]

The purchase order imposed a daily penalty. Article 1226 allowed the producer to seek further damages when the repairer refused to pay the penalty. The producer still had to establish both the loss and its foreseeability.[^31]

Production reports from before the period in dispute did not show the decrease during the period claimed. A summary of expenses did not establish payment for labor or a crane. The motor’s use as a spare also mattered because the repairer could not reasonably have foreseen liability for those losses when it accepted the repair.[^32]

The trial court awarded production losses, crane and labor expenses and fees. The appellate court applied the exclusion clause and dismissed the complaint. On the producer’s petition the Supreme Court reversed and set aside the appellate decision and resolution.[^33]

The Supreme Court granted the petition. It ordered ABB to pay P129,329.75 for 131 days of delay at P987.25 per day, with 6% annual interest from filing of the complaint to finality and 12% annually thereafter until full payment. It denied production losses, labor expenses, crane rental and attorney’s fees and found no basis to hold the corporate officer personally liable.[^34]

## The basis for a claim of increased material costs

A contractor’s claim for higher material costs may fail on the terms of the contract and the evidence of what it billed. In [*H.L. Carlos Construction, Inc. v. Marina Properties Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46657)[^35] the contractor invoked Article 2201 on the theory that the delay in paying its bills left it to bear higher material costs. The contract allowed escalation only for labor. The disputed progress billing contained no material escalation claim and the contractor failed to link the extra work to an increase in material prices. The Supreme Court rejected that claim without deciding whether payment had been delayed.[^36]

The Supreme Court then applied the same measure against the contractor. By November 30, 1989 the contractor had accomplished only about 80 percent of the project and was already in delay. The owner had paid P31,435,187 of the P38,580,609 contract price, which left P7,145,422 unpaid. To finish the project the owner hired a second construction firm for P11,750,000. The Supreme Court held that the contractor was liable for the natural and probable consequences of its breach and fixed the owner’s actual damages at P4,604,579.[^37]

The contractor also owed liquidated damages for 92 days of delay under the time clause of the contract. On the contractor’s Rule 45 petition the Supreme Court partly granted relief. It awarded the contractor P1,196,202 in labor cost escalation and P79,340.52 for extra work. It affirmed the appellate decision in all other respects.[^38]

## Repair costs and the proof of resulting loss

The cost of correcting defective work may require an adjustment for property that can still be used. In [*Spouses Batal v. Spouses San Pedro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43723)[^39] a faulty boundary survey led the owners to build a fence on a right of way. The Supreme Court treated the surveyors’ negligence as culpa contractual and held them liable for the resulting damage under Articles 1170 and 2201.[^40] The Supreme Court adopted the lower courts’ valuation of the necessary demolition and reconstruction, which accounted for reusable iron grills.[^41]

A shutdown can cause an actual wage expense even when the claimant cannot prove the amount paid. In [*Adrian Wilson International Associates, Inc. v. TMX Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54215)[^42] the administrator’s failure to warn about the roof deprived the owner of an opportunity to have the contractor correct it before final payment. Vouchers and payroll summaries did not prove that employees had received the full sum claimed for the shutdown, so the Supreme Court substituted temperate damages for that part of the award.[^43]

## Direct expenses and proof of payment

### [*Ilao-Oreta v. Spouses Ronquillo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45236): Which expenses followed from the missed procedure

The date of breach and the hospital’s actual charge determine which medical expenses the patient can recover. In [*Ilao-Oreta v. Spouses Ronquillo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45236)[^44] the patient checked in for a scheduled laparoscopy and received preoperative treatment. Her doctor miscalculated the time difference on a return trip from Hawaii and reached Manila that evening.[^45]

The trial court awarded actual damages. On the spouses’ appeal the appellate court raised to P16,069.40 the actual damages for which the doctor and the hospital were jointly and severally liable. It also held the doctor liable for P50,000.00 in moral damages, P25,000.00 in exemplary damages and P20,000.00 in attorney’s fees.[^46]

The Supreme Court found the doctor negligent because she scheduled the procedure without considering the time difference between the Philippines and Hawaii. It did not find gross negligence. Before leaving she had left an admitting order with her secretary, told the patient how to prepare and instructed the hospital staff on the preoperative treatment. The Supreme Court accepted her argument that the situation presented no clear and apparent harm that even a careless person would perceive. The procedure was elective.[^47]

The Supreme Court then examined whether the claimed expenses followed from the missed procedure and whether the spouses had proved them.[^48]

Both lower courts included expenses that predated the breach. An unsigned list failed to substantiate fuel and food expenses. The hospital’s statement showed P2,416.50 in gross charges less P127.80 in unused medicine, leaving P2,288.70 payable.[^49]

The unused P2,711.30 of the spouses’ P5,000.00 deposit was refundable. The Supreme Court granted the doctor’s petition and reduced actual damages to P2,288.70 with 6% annual interest from the filing of the complaint on May 18, 1999. Interest runs at 12% annually from finality until satisfaction. It deleted P50,000.00 in moral damages, P25,000.00 in exemplary damages and P20,000.00 in attorney’s fees.[^50]

## Commercial losses and lost income

### [*Samhwa Company Ltd. v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jan1992/gr_74305_1992.html): The economic cost of leaving guarantees outstanding

An undertaking to free sellers from a debt can have measurable value before the creditor calls on a guarantee. In [*Samhwa Company Ltd. v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jan1992/gr_74305_1992.html)[^51] the sellers of Lotus shares bargained for release of their personal guarantees on the company’s bank loan. The purchasers failed without reasonable ground to obtain the release and to pay the debt within the stipulated period.[^52]

The trial court awarded actual damages. The court of appeals deleted P150,000.00 in exemplary damages on reconsideration because it found insufficient evidence of malice and bad faith. The Supreme Court accepted the factual findings on guaranty costs, a loss of P756,089.00 on the Marcopper shares and exchange differences of P382,870.00 on installments that should have been paid on time.[^53]

The sellers also owed P1,429,950.75 under the agreement. The trial court had offset that obligation against P2,250,627.30 in damages and arrived at P833,455.35. The Supreme Court made the same deduction and fixed the balance at P820,676.55. It denied the petition, affirmed the appellate decision of November 15, 1985 and resolution of April 9, 1986 and held Samhwa and Lotus jointly and severally liable to Sheff and Swiryn for P820,676.55.[^54]

The affirmed judgment also required the defendants to maintain the DBP obligation so as to free the sellers from further liability and immediately obtain the release of the sellers’ guarantees and Mariano Marcos’s collateral. The sellers had to deliver the 30% Lotus equity upon receiving the bank clearances. Costs remained chargeable to the defendants.[^55]

### [*Orient Freight International, Inc. v. Keihin-Everett Forwarding Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63503): Foreseeable loss of a brokerage customer

The loss of profits on a second contract can follow from a breach of the first. In [*Orient Freight International, Inc. v. Keihin-Everett Forwarding Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63503)[^56] a freight subcontractor led the forwarding company and its customer to believe that a report about their shipment concerned a stalled truck. The customer later terminated its brokerage agreement with the forwarding company for loss of confidence.[^57]

The lower courts treated the claim as one for quasi-delict. The Supreme Court disagreed. The duty to investigate and report arose when the forwarding company asked the subcontractor to look into the news report. The subcontractor was negligent in performing that obligation. Articles 1170, 1172 and 1173 on negligence in the performance of an obligation therefore governed. The Supreme Court also found no conscious intent to cause harm that would bring Article 21 into play.[^58]

The question was what the subcontractor could reasonably foresee if it failed to disclose what had happened. The possibility that a crime had been committed made accurate disclosure material to the relationship. The Supreme Court held the subcontractor liable for the profits the forwarding company lost when its customer terminated the agreement.[^59]

The forwarding company still had to support its computation. The trial court compared the figures in its monthly statement with its audited financial statement and reduced the claim. On review under Rule 45 the Supreme Court declined to disturb the supported assessment.[^60]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the appellate decision and resolution. Orient Freight owed Keihin-Everett PHP 1,666,667.00 in actual damages for lost net profits and PHP 50,000.00 in attorney’s fees. The trial court had denied exemplary damages because it found no gross negligence.[^61]

### [*Woodchild Holdings, Inc. v. Roxas Electric and Construction Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45536): Rent lost while the buyer waited for the seller to clear the site

A buyer may lose rent even before its warehouse opens. In [*Woodchild Holdings, Inc. v. Roxas Electric and Construction Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45536)[^62] the seller agreed to remove the squatters by September 29, 1991. They still occupied the property when the buyer demanded their removal the following April. Only then could the contractor begin construction and obtain a building permit.[^63]

The Regional Trial Court of Makati, Branch 57 ruled for the buyer and awarded P5,568,000 in actual damages and unrealized income. The Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the complaint. On review on certiorari the Supreme Court held that the corporate president lacked authority to burden the adjacent lot with a right of way or to agree to sell part of it. The buyer nevertheless recovered for the seller’s separate failure to clear the site.[^64]

The delay cost the buyer P1,712,980 in increased construction costs under Article 1170. It also lost P3,900,000 in rent under its lease to Ponderosa Leather Goods Company. The Supreme Court awarded the unearned income under Articles 2200 and 2201.[^65]

The Supreme Court affirmed the appellate decision with modification. It ordered the seller to pay the buyer P5,612,980 in actual damages and P100,000 in attorney’s fees. It made no award of costs.[^66]

## Evidence of business profits

A projected profit needs a basis in the business records or other evidence of profitability. In [*Gaw v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/mar1993/gr_70451_1993.html)[^67] a dispute over marketing white cement led both parties to claim damages. Tan failed to show even his business’s average actual profits. The Supreme Court deleted the appellate award of P100,000.00 on his counterclaim while leaving the award of attorney’s fees and costs intact.[^68]

## Pecuniary loss with an uncertain value

### [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060): Proof of loss and proof of its amount

A buyer can prove that nondelivery cost it sales yet fail to prove how much it would have earned. In [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060)[^69] the seller agreed to deliver 250,000 bags of fertilizer after the buyer opened a letter of credit. The seller received a copy of the credit but failed to deliver. The surety then paid the buyer PHP 5,000,000.00 under the performance bond.[^70]

The trial court ordered the return of the bond payment to the surety and the return of the security. The court of appeals set those orders aside, dismissed the complaint and awarded CONFED PHP 30,000,000.00 for lost profits. On review under Rule 45 the Supreme Court examined both what loss followed from the breach and what proof fixed its amount.[^71]

The failure to deliver deprived CONFED of opportunities to sell fertilizer to its buyers. The evidence persuaded the Supreme Court that CONFED suffered a pecuniary loss. It did not establish the amount of the lost profits with reasonable certainty. Article 2224 allowed temperate damages for the loss that the evidence did establish.[^72]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the appellate judgment with a modification. It deleted the PHP 30,000,000.00 award for lost profits and ordered the enterprise and Pete Nicomedes Prado jointly and solidarily to pay CONFED PHP 4,000,000.00 in temperate damages. The decree names the enterprise as VRB BIOPRO ENTERPRISES, although the caption identifies it as VHB Biopro Enterprises.[^73]

The surety’s payment of the bond stood. The appellate court had dismissed its counterclaims for lack of evidence. CONFED received neither moral damages nor attorney’s fees. The evidence did not clearly show bad faith in the seller’s breach and did not establish a factual basis for fees.[^74]

### [*Government Service Insurance System v. Spouses Deang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52673): The borrowers needed their title to obtain another loan

A lender’s duty to return a title can arise from the mortgage transaction itself. In [*Government Service Insurance System v. Spouses Deang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52673)[^75] the spouses paid off their housing loan and requested the owner’s duplicate title for use as security for a P50,000 private loan. GSIS could not find the title. It obtained a replacement through reconstitution proceedings.[^76]

GSIS sought review on certiorari after the Court of Appeals affirmed the judgment of the Regional Trial Court, Angeles City. Both tribunals treated the obligation as one arising from quasi-delict. The Supreme Court traced the duty to the pre-existing loan agreement and held that the duty to return the title arose upon release of the mortgage.[^77]

Good faith is presumed and bad faith must be proved. The Supreme Court therefore treated GSIS as an obligor in good faith. It found a causal link between the missing title and the inability to obtain the new loan. The spouses showed financial loss but failed to establish its amount with the certainty needed for actual damages. Economic hardship and anxiety did not establish a right to moral damages.[^78]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed with modification. It sustained P20,000 in temperate damages and deleted the P15,000 award of attorney’s fees. The surviving trial award carried legal interest from the filing of the complaint and costs of suit below. The Supreme Court made no award of costs on review.[^79]

## Lost employment income requires proof of continued employment

Losing the document needed to return to work can cause a pecuniary loss without establishing the earnings the claimant would have received. In [*LBC Express, Inc. v. Spouses Ado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42513)[^80] Euberto Ado, a mechanic who had worked in Bahrain, shipped five boxes to himself with an LBC import manager as the party to notify in Manila. An LBC employee suggested that he avail of the customs duty exemption and entrust his passport to her for submission to the Customs Office. He hesitated because the passport carried his re-entry visa to Bahrain. He gave it up after being assured that it would be forwarded to him with the boxes. The passport never came back and he could not return to Bahrain to report for work.[^81]

The Regional Trial Court of Naval, Biliran, Branch 16 held the two LBC companies solidarily liable for P480,000.00 in compensatory damages, P300,000.00 in moral damages and P30,000.00 in attorney’s fees. The compensatory award stood for the renewal of a two-year employment contract. The Court of Appeals affirmed.[^82]

The Supreme Court set out both paragraphs of Article 2201. It found that Ado’s two-year contract had expired before his vacation. Whether his employer would rehire him depended entirely on the employer. Ado offered only his own testimony and a certification from the employer on that point. The evidence did not show that he would be employed again on the same terms. The loss of the passport still caused some pecuniary loss. Its value was not proved, so the Supreme Court awarded temperate damages under Article 2224.[^83]

The Supreme Court sustained the award of moral damages. The failure to deliver the passport did not by itself amount to bad faith. The carrier’s later conduct did. It met the spouses’ repeated inquiries with various excuses. It informed Ado that the passport had been lost only in October 1996. The Supreme Court found that the carrier acted in bad faith and in wanton disregard of its contractual obligation.[^84]

The Supreme Court affirmed the appellate decision with modification. It deleted the compensatory award and granted the spouses P10,000.00 in temperate damages. It reduced the moral damages to P50,000.00 and the attorney’s fees to P15,000.00.[^85]

## Fraud and proof of profits

### [*G.A. Machineries, Inc. v. Yaptinchay*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1983/nov1983/gr_l-30965_1983.html): Fraud does not prove the amount of lost profits

A freight hauler bought a diesel engine represented as brand-new. It malfunctioned within a week and its serial number showed signs of tampering. The Supreme Court treated the misrepresentation as fraud or bad faith and sustained reimbursement of the purchase price.[^86]

Lost profits presented a separate question. The buyer multiplied earnings per trip by the number of trips he claimed the truck had missed. The preparer of the estimates was not identified beyond his name. The Supreme Court found the proof speculative even though Articles 2200 and 2201 allowed recovery for a proven loss of profits.[^87]

The Court of First Instance of Rizal had awarded damages and the Court of Appeals had affirmed. On the seller’s petition, the Supreme Court deleted ₱54,000.48 in actual damages. It ordered the seller to reimburse ₱7,590 with interest of 6% per annum from January 27, 1962 to July 29, 1974 and 12% per annum from July 30, 1974 until reimbursement.[^88]

The rest of the judgment stood. The buyer had to return the Fordson engine bearing serial number A-21219. The seller remained liable for ₱2,000 in attorney’s fees payable to the buyer’s counsel and for costs.[^89]

## What the parties promised to pay

The terms of the contract identify both the debtor and the loss for which that debtor may answer. In [*Integrated Realty Corporation v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jun1989/gr_60705_1989.html)[^90] the bank that issued the time deposits owed damages for its own delay in paying them after maturity. The Supreme Court treated the time deposits as a simple loan and not as a contract of deposit. The borrowers separately owed PNB the interest and penalties on their promissory notes. The bank had never signed those notes and the borrowers had themselves acted in bad faith in refusing payment.[^91]


A demand for stipulated damages can still require an assessment of the equities. In [*National Power Corporation v. National Merchandising Corporation*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/oct1982/gr_l-33819_1982.html)[^92] the purchaser invoked the second paragraph to claim ₱360,572.80 after sulfur failed to arrive. The Supreme Court rejected full recovery and set liquidated damages at the amount of the bidder’s bond. The article appeared in the purchaser’s argument. The majority gave the delay in disposing of the case as its reason for withholding interest.[^93]

## Fraud and stipulated damages

### [*Pamintuan v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-26339_1979.html): Fraud can defeat a contractual limit on damages

A seller agreed to supply imported plastic sheeting. The parties fixed liquidated damages of ₱10,000 for a violation of the contract. The courts below found that the seller delivered only part of the sheeting and overcharged the buyer.[^94]

The seller argued that the stipulation fixed the most he could owe. The Supreme Court relied on the fraud provision of Article 1226 together with Articles 1171 and 2201. It accepted the finding of fraud and allowed recovery of the damages proved. The buyer could not add the stipulated ₱10,000 to that recovery on these facts.[^95]

The appellate judgment had required delivery or payment if the seller could not deliver. The appellate judgment had set that alternative at ₱100,559.28. The Supreme Court reduced it to ₱90,559.28 with 6% interest a year from filing of the complaint. It otherwise affirmed with no costs in that instance.[^96]

## Consent to an assignment and proof of bad faith

A creditor seeking contractual damages must identify the obligation the defendant breached. In [*International Exchange Bank v. Rudy S. Labos and Associates, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68446)[^97] a bank treated a developer’s consent to successive assignments of condominium rights as evidence of double dealing. The developer had signed only the conforme portion of the first deed of assignment because the contract to sell required its consent. The Supreme Court held that the deed bound only the purchaser and the bank and imposed no obligation on the developer. The first assignment secured a loan and was in effect a mortgage. The developer’s later consent recognized the purchaser’s right to transfer its interest.[^98]

The bank failed to establish its claim that the developer intended to defraud it. The Supreme Court rejected the claim for contractual damages under Articles 1170 to 1173 in relation to Article 2201. It also found no basis to make the developer solidarily liable for the borrowers’ debt.[^99]

## Bad faith in sales and banking

### [*Sta. Lucia Realty and Development, Inc. v. Lumawag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69310): A seller’s failure to disclose an encroachment

A seller’s conduct before final payment can explain why its later refusal to deliver amounts to bad faith. In [*Sta. Lucia Realty and Development, Inc. v. Lumawag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69310)[^100] the seller knew that the owner of the adjoining property had built part of her home on the lot it agreed to sell to Edsel Lumawag. It accepted his full payment without telling him about the encroachment. He demanded the deed, title and possession but the seller failed to deliver.[^101]

The housing board, the Office of the President and the court of appeals found bad faith. The Supreme Court held that the seller had failed to preserve the lot in a deliverable state and had unjustifiably refused to perform its obligations. It cited Article 2201 for the rule that an obligor in bad faith answers for all damages reasonably attributable to the non-performance. It sustained the award of moral and exemplary damages against the seller.[^102]

The position of Lourdes Pearce, the adjoining builder, differed from that of the seller. She believed the house stood on her lot. Her failure to update her relocation survey and consult the surveyors before construction nevertheless contributed to the damage. A final appellate judgment had also settled the developer’s liability for moral and exemplary damages to her.[^103]

The Supreme Court denied the petition of AFP Retirement and Separation Benefits System in G.R. No. 223241. It ordered the seller to execute a deed of absolute sale and deliver the transfer certificate of title covering the entire Lot 20, Block 7 of Citadella Subdivision to Lumawag without prejudice to his rights under Article 448. The seller must pay him PHP 100,000.00 in moral damages, PHP 30,000.00 in exemplary damages and PHP 30,000.00 in attorney’s fees and the costs of suit.[^104]

Pearce must pay Lumawag PHP 30,000.00 in nominal damages and PHP 10,000.00 in attorney’s fees. The developer must reimburse her for expenses or losses she may incur that the indemnity under Articles 546 and 548 does not cover. All damages and attorney’s fees earn 6% annual interest from finality until full payment.[^105]

Lumawag had 30 days from receipt of the decision to exercise his Article 448 option and communicate it to the seller. He could appropriate the improvement upon paying Pearce the proper indemnity or oblige her to pay the price of the lot if its value was not considerably more than the building’s.[^106]

The Supreme Court partly granted the developer’s petition in G.R. No. 222897. It deleted the developer’s liability for moral and exemplary damages to Pearce under the final April 10, 2012 appellate judgment. It remanded that case to the housing board, now the Department of Human Settlements and Urban Development, to determine the fair price of the lots, the expenses incurred and the value of the materials for purposes of Articles 448, 546 and 548.[^107]

### [*BPI Family Bank v. Franco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46696): An unjustified freeze and the bank’s purpose

A depositor can establish an unjustified freeze without proving the dishonest purpose that would enlarge the bank’s liability. In [*BPI Family Bank v. Franco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46696)[^108] the bank refused to release Franco’s funds while seeking to protect itself against a suspected fraud. The court that issued the attachment had yet to acquire jurisdiction over Franco when the bank garnished his deposits.[^109]

The trial court awarded nominal damages. The appellate court treated the bank’s conduct as bad faith. The Supreme Court found that the bank meant to hold the funds as security for its intended action and found no fraud or motive of self-enrichment.[^110]

The Supreme Court partly granted the bank’s petition. It deleted P63,189.00 in unearned time-deposit interest, P200,000.00 in moral damages and P100,000.00 in exemplary damages. It restored P10,000.00 in nominal damages and retained P75,000.00 in attorney’s fees because the refusal to release the funds compelled Franco to litigate.[^111]

The bank also owed P76,500.00 in interest on P450,000.00 from May 18, 1990 to October 31, 1991 and the P498,973.23 savings balance as of May 18, 1990 with interest under its guidelines. Its P3,800,000.00 counterclaim remained dismissed. The Supreme Court made no pronouncement as to costs.[^112]

The trial court had set attorney’s fees at P30,000.00 and the appellate court granted P75,000.00. The Supreme Court also upheld the award to Franco of the P400,000.00 in Quiaoit’s account. Quiaoit himself had testified that the funds belonged to Franco.[^113]

## Loss of the use of banking services

The bank’s response after a supplementary card was reported lost illustrates the distinction between carelessness and bad faith. In [*Far East Bank and Trust Company v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/feb1995/gr_108164_1995.html)[^114] the bank also cancelled the principal card but failed to notify Luis Luna personally. The majority quoted earlier reasoning on Article 2201 and found no sufficient indication of deliberate harm or gross negligence amounting to bad faith. Luis could recover nominal damages for the invasion of his right when the restaurant declined his card.[^115]

The time of demand also matters. In [*Fidelity Savings and Mortgage Bank v. Cenzon*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/apr1990/gr_46208_1990.html)[^116] the depositors sought payment months after regulators had closed the bank. The trial court found no bad faith when the bank accepted the deposits. The Supreme Court retained the principal and contractual interest accrued to the closure date but removed moral and exemplary damages and attorney’s fees.[^117]

## Allegations of bad faith and findings after trial

A pleading can raise a contractual damages claim before anyone proves its amount. In [*Magat v. Medialdea*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1983/apr1983/gr_l-37120_1983.html)[^118] the seller alleged that the buyer refused to open a letter of credit for specially ordered radio equipment. Hypothetically admitting the allegations, the Supreme Court found the complaint sufficient. It reasoned that the loss of expected profits comes into being at the very moment of breach. It also found bad faith sufficiently alleged for moral and exemplary damages under Articles 2220 and 2232. It set aside the dismissal and remanded the case.[^119]

The distinction also mattered in [*Floresca v. Philex Mining Corporation*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1985/apr1985/gr_l-30642_1985.html)[^120] where miners’ heirs alleged that their employer deliberately failed to protect workers before a cave-in. The majority held that the regular court could hear the claim. The remaining heirs’ earlier receipt of compensation did not defeat the suit because they learned of the employer’s breach only after receiving benefits. Any greater award on remand would have to account for payments already received.[^121]

The evidence in [*Lirag Textile Mills, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/apr1975/gr_30736_1975.html)[^122] showed why the employer’s asserted ground for ending a fixed-term contract mattered. The employer claimed serious losses and knew its allegation was false. The Supreme Court identified that knowledge as bad faith and applied the second paragraph to the resulting breach. It dealt separately under Article 21 with the board chairman who had induced the employee to leave a permanent position.[^123]

## Substitute carriage and itinerary changes

### [*China Airlines Limited v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jul1992/gr_94590_1992.html): Foreseeable expenses after a missed flight

A carrier can owe the cost of replacement travel even when its employees tried to perform the contract. In [*China Airlines Limited v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jul1992/gr_94590_1992.html)[^124] the airline confirmed Ocampo’s request for an earlier return from San Francisco. Its employees nevertheless failed to board him on that flight.[^125]

The trial court treated Ocampo as a chance passenger and dismissed the complaint. On grounds of equity it ordered the airline to reimburse US$601.00. The Court of Appeals reversed. It found bad faith and added P200,000.00 in moral damages, P200,000.00 in exemplary damages and P50,000.00 in attorney’s fees.[^126]

The telexes explained why the evidence established breach without proving bad faith. The San Francisco office sought verification because the discounted ticket restricted changes. The Manila office sent an erroneous message but followed up to secure the revised booking. The evidence fell short of showing deliberate malice or negligence so gross as to amount to bad faith.[^127]

The Supreme Court allowed the peso value of the replacement Philippine Airlines ticket and US$1,500.00 for reasonable expenses caused by the delay. It reversed and set aside the July 25, 1990 appellate decision and ordered the carrier to pay Ocampo the peso equivalent of US$2,101.00 at the exchange rate prevailing at payment, P15,000.00 in attorney’s fees and costs. It dismissed the carrier’s trial court counterclaim.[^128]

### [*Zalamea v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_104235_1993.html): Replacement fares without double recovery

Confirmation had a different consequence when the carrier withheld its boarding priorities from its passengers. In [*Zalamea v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_104235_1993.html)[^129] three family members checked in together with confirmed reservations. TWA boarded the father on his daughter’s full-fare ticket and left behind the two passengers holding discounted tickets.[^130]

The RTC of Makati, Branch 145 found bad faith. The court of appeals recognized the breach but found neither fraud nor bad faith because United States regulations allowed overbooking. The Supreme Court held that the regulation had never been proved. Foreign laws must be alleged and proved like any other fact. Even if overbooking were allowed, TWA failed to show that its boarding priorities formed part of the contracts. It also failed to tell the family beforehand that confirmed passengers could be left behind. That omission was bad faith and warranted moral and exemplary damages.[^131]

The next TWA flight was full. Buying tickets on American Airlines therefore followed from TWA’s breach. Reimbursing the new tickets and also refunding the unused original tickets would have given the passengers transportation without paying any fare.[^132]

The Supreme Court granted the petition and modified the appellate judgment. TWA had to pay the petitioners US$918.00 or its peso equivalent at the time of payment for the American Airlines tickets Suthira and Liana bought from New York to Los Angeles. It also awarded P50,000.00 in moral damages, P50,000.00 in exemplary damages, P50,000.00 in attorney’s fees and costs of suit.[^133]

The trial court had also ordered refunds of the unused TWA tickets, including P8,934.50 for Liana’s ticket. The appellate court ordered refunds of US$159.49 each for the tickets of Suthira and Cesar. The Supreme Court upheld the refusal to refund Liana’s ticket. Her father had used it to board and the family had impliedly agreed to that arrangement. It found the trial awards of moral and exemplary damages excessive because only Suthira and Liana were left behind.[^134]

### [*Savellano v. Northwest Airlines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48535): The carrier changed the route without consulting the passengers

An emergency may justify a landing but leave the carrier bound by the rest of the itinerary. In [*Savellano v. Northwest Airlines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48535)[^135] engine trouble justified landing in Seattle. Northwest failed to show why three passengers then had to travel via Los Angeles and Seoul when they had agreed to connect through Tokyo. Some passengers continued to Tokyo.[^136]

The three passengers sought review under Rule 45 after the Court of Appeals reversed the trial judgment and dismissed their complaint. The Supreme Court found a breach in the change of stopping places without consent or proof of necessity. It considered the damages under Articles 1170 and 2201.[^137]

The passengers failed to prove that Northwest maliciously singled them out. The evidence showed that the reservation system assigned the routes by final destination. The failure to consult the passengers did not establish the bad faith needed for moral damages. Their claim for missing valuables also failed because they gave no timely notice of the loss.[^138]

The Supreme Court partially granted the petition and modified the appellate decision. It ordered Northwest to pay each of the three passengers P150,000 in nominal damages to vindicate the right to notice and consultation before a change in the contracted stop. It made no pronouncement as to costs.[^139]

## A carrier’s explanation for the breach

The reason for a carrier’s failure may determine which losses it must pay. In [*China Airlines, Ltd. v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jan1989/gr_73835_1989.html)[^140] an engine malfunction delayed Claudia Osorio’s flight from Manila by four days. The airline agreed to fly her through Taipei to San Francisco with an immediate connection to Los Angeles. The telex instructions reached San Francisco late. Airline staff asked her to wait while they verified her status with Manila. They also refused her food and overnight accommodation. She left the office without leaving a contact address. When authority for her ticket came that evening the staff could not reach her. She later flew to Los Angeles on a Western Airlines ticket she bought herself.[^141]

The trial court absolved the airline except for reimbursement of the $100.00 she spent as a rerouted passenger and the $56.00 Western Airlines fare. The Intermediate Appellate Court found a palpable breach and added P100,000 in moral damages, P20,000 in exemplary damages and attorney’s fees.[^142]

The Supreme Court found a breach because the airline failed to secure the promised connection. Telex was the established mode of communication between the two offices. The airline sent the messages more than 24 hours before her expected arrival. Its total reliance on telex was negligent but not so gross as to amount to bad faith. The staff acted from lack of information and not from ill will. Because the breach was in good faith, the airline answered only for the natural and probable consequences of the breach. Those consisted of the actual damages the trial court awarded. The Supreme Court eliminated the moral and exemplary damages and raised attorney’s fees to P10,000.[^143]

In [*Verzosa v. Baytan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1960/apr1960/gr_l-14092_1960.html)[^144] the passengers’ claim arose from their contract of carriage. The majority applied the distinction drawn in [*Fores v. Miranda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1959/mar1959/gr_l-12163_1959.html)[^145] between the employee’s negligence and the operator’s bad faith. The operator had faced neither an allegation nor proof of fraud or bad faith. The Supreme Court eliminated moral damages while leaving the compensation for actual loss in place.[^146]

## The effect of good faith in passenger carriage

### [*Fores v. Miranda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1959/mar1959/gr_l-12163_1959.html): Negligent carriage does not establish bad faith

A jeepney hit a bridge wall while descending at excessive speed. One passenger suffered a fractured upper arm. The driver pleaded guilty to serious physical injuries through reckless imprudence. The passenger’s civil claim against the operator arose from the contract of carriage.[^147]

The Supreme Court read Article 2201 alongside Article 2220. A negligent breach could support recovery for the passenger’s injuries while leaving the claim for moral damages unproved. Negligence may be so gross as to amount to malice, but the evidence must establish that fact. The majority distinguished the rules that govern the death of a passenger.[^148]

The operator sought review of the appellate judgment. The Supreme Court eliminated the ₱5,000 moral damages specified in the appellate resolution. The rest of the judgment stood, including ₱2,000 in actual damages as reduced by the Court of Appeals. It imposed no costs in that instance.[^149]

## Injury to a surviving passenger

A carrier’s liability for a passenger’s injury presents a different question from the survivor’s right to moral damages. In [*Sulpicio Lines, Inc. v. Sesante*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62210)[^150] the M/V Princess of the Orient sank near Fortune Island in Batangas on September 18, 1998. Napoleon Sesante, a police officer and lawyer, survived the sinking and sued the carrier for breach of contract and damages.[^151]

The Regional Trial Court, Branch 91 in Quezon City held the carrier liable for temperate damages, P1,000,000.00 in moral damages and costs. Its decree fixed the temperate damages at P400,000.00. The decision also recounts that the trial court reduced the temperate award on reconsideration from P500,000.00 to P300,000.00. Sesante died while the appeal was pending and his heirs substituted him. The Court of Appeals lowered the temperate damages to P120,000.00, which approximated the cost of his lost belongings.[^152]

The Supreme Court held that the action survived Sesante’s death because it involved a personal injury caused by the breach of the contract of carriage. The carrier invoked Article 2201 to argue that Article 1759 did not make it automatically liable. The Supreme Court distinguished the presumption of negligence under Article 1756 from the carrier’s liability for its employees under Article 1759.[^153]

The Supreme Court rejected the carrier’s reliance on the weather because the gross negligence of its captain caused the sinking. Moral damages in a contract of carriage ordinarily require the death of a passenger or proof of fraud and bad faith. They may also be awarded when the breach is wanton and deliberately injurious. The Supreme Court found that the carrier and its agents acted wantonly and recklessly.[^154]

The Supreme Court affirmed the appellate decision with modifications. The carrier must pay the heirs P1,000,000.00 in moral damages, P1,000,000.00 in exemplary damages and P120,000.00 in temperate damages. All amounts earn 6% interest per annum from finality until full payment. The carrier must also pay the costs of suit.[^155]

## Wanton breach in passenger carriage

### [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650): A deliberate risk to passengers does not prove lost wages

An unseaworthy vessel placed its passengers at risk as soon as it sailed. In [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650)[^156] the carrier began the voyage before it finished repairing one engine. The other engine failed at sea. Arroyo disembarked when the vessel returned to Cebu and traveled the next day.[^157]

The trial court dismissed the complaint and the counterclaim. The court of appeals reversed. On the carrier’s petition for review under Rule 45, the Supreme Court held that Article 698 of the Code of Commerce applied suppletorily to an interrupted voyage. The carrier’s knowing disregard of the vessel’s condition also required consideration of the Civil Code provisions on damages.[^158]

Arroyo could have reached his office in the afternoon had he remained aboard when the voyage resumed. Even the loss of half a day’s salary was unproved. The evidence did not establish that the employer withheld his pay or refused to excuse his absence. The carrier’s bad faith supported moral and exemplary damages despite the failure to prove lost earnings.[^159]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the judgment in CA-G.R. CV No. 39901 subject to setting aside P5,000.00 in attorney’s fees. The carrier owed Arroyo P20,000.00 in moral damages and P10,000.00 in exemplary damages. Costs were assessed against the carrier.[^160]

## Related Provisions

| Provision | Relationship to Article 2201 | Practical distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Article 1170](../book-4-title-01-obligations/art-1170.md) | Article 1170 fixes liability for fraud, negligence, delay or contravention of the tenor of the obligation. Article 2201 then measures the damages. | In [*Savellano v. Northwest Airlines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48535)[^183] the Supreme Court held the carrier liable under both articles for changing the stopping places without consent or proof of necessity. |
| [Article 1226](../book-4-title-01-obligations/art-1226.md) | A penal clause ordinarily takes the place of damages. Further damages remain recoverable when the obligor refuses to pay the penalty or acts fraudulently. | [*Continental Cement Corporation v. Asea Brown Boveri, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32714)[^184] allowed the claim for further damages because the repairer refused to pay the penalty. [*Pamintuan v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-26339_1979.html)[^185] allowed proven damages in place of the stipulated sum because the seller acted fraudulently. |
| [Article 2200](art-2200.md) | Indemnification includes the value lost and the profits the obligee failed to obtain. | A lost sale can fall within the scope of the breach even when the evidence fails to establish its net value. See [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060)[^186]. |
| [Article 2220](art-2220.md) | Moral damages for breach of contract require fraud or bad faith. | [*Fores v. Miranda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1959/mar1959/gr_l-12163_1959.html)[^187] read Article 2220 with Article 2201 and refused moral damages for a breach caused by the driver’s negligence. |
| [Article 2221](art-2221.md) | Nominal damages vindicate a right that was violated. | [*Far East Bank and Trust Company v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/feb1995/gr_108164_1995.html)[^188] found no bad faith and awarded nominal damages in place of moral and exemplary damages. |
| [Article 2224](art-2224.md) | Temperate damages address a pecuniary loss whose amount cannot be proved with certainty. | In [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060)[^189] the evidence established lost opportunities to sell fertilizer. The Supreme Court replaced the award for lost profits with temperate damages. |
| [Article 2232](art-2232.md) | Exemplary damages in contracts require wanton, fraudulent, reckless, oppressive or malevolent conduct. | [*China Airlines Limited v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jul1992/gr_94590_1992.html)[^190] found no such conduct and limited recovery to the natural and probable consequences of the breach. |
| [Article 19](../preliminary-title/art-0019.md) | A claim that rests on no contract, quasi-contract, delict or quasi-delict may fall under the abuse of rights. | [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832)[^191] and [*Public Estates Authority v. Chu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43722)[^192] placed the claims before them outside Article 2201 for that reason. |
| [Article 21](../preliminary-title/art-0021.md) | A person who is not a party to the contract may answer for willful injury contrary to morals. | [*Lirag Textile Mills, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/apr1975/gr_30736_1975.html)[^193] held the board chairman liable under Article 21 for inducing the employee to leave a permanent position. |

## Key Cases

- [*Orient Freight International, Inc. v. Keihin-Everett Forwarding Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63503)[^194] held the subcontractor responsible for lost profits when its misleading report foreseeably caused the loss of the customer’s trust and the termination of the brokerage agreement.
- [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060)[^195] deleted a lost-profits award because the buyer failed to establish the amount. Its inability to resell the undelivered fertilizer still supported temperate damages.
- [*Sta. Lucia Realty and Development, Inc. v. Lumawag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69310)[^196] sustained moral and exemplary damages against a seller who accepted full payment despite knowing that a neighbor had built on the lot and then failed to deliver it as agreed.
- [*Vil-Rey Planners and Builders v. Lexber, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61984)[^197] measures the consequence of incomplete work by the price the owner paid to finish it.
- [*Continental Cement Corporation v. Asea Brown Boveri, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32714)[^198] tests claimed production losses against what the repairer could anticipate when it accepted a spare motor.
- [*H.L. Carlos Construction, Inc. v. Marina Properties Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46657)[^199] measured the owner’s loss from the contractor’s delay and abandonment by the excess cost of hiring a second firm to finish the project.
- [*BPI Family Bank v. Franco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46696)[^200] examines the bank’s purpose in freezing a depositor’s funds before imposing the broader liability for bad faith.
- [*Ilao-Oreta v. Spouses Ronquillo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45236)[^201] identifies the expenses caused by a missed procedure and separates the hospital’s net charges from a refundable deposit.
- [*Woodchild Holdings, Inc. v. Roxas Electric and Construction Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45536)[^202] distinguishes two losses from the same delay in clearing a warehouse site. Increased construction costs fell under Article 1170. Lost rent under an existing lease fell under Articles 2200 and 2201.[^203]
- [*Savellano v. Northwest Airlines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48535)[^204] distinguishes the necessity of an emergency landing from the necessity of a later change in the agreed route. The passengers established a breach and recovered nominal damages despite failing to prove bad faith.[^205]
- [*Government Service Insurance System v. Spouses Deang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52673)[^206] traces a lost borrowing opportunity to the mortgagee’s failure to return the title. The spouses recovered temperate damages because the evidence established financial loss while leaving its amount uncertain.[^207]
- [*LBC Express, Inc. v. Spouses Ado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42513)[^208] refused lost wages that depended on an unproved renewal of employment. It awarded temperate damages for the lost passport and moral damages for the carrier’s bad faith after the loss.
- [*Sulpicio Lines, Inc. v. Sesante*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62210)[^209] sustained moral and exemplary damages for a surviving passenger because the carrier and its agents acted wantonly and recklessly.
- [*China Airlines Limited v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jul1992/gr_94590_1992.html)[^210]. The expense of substitute passage and reasonable delay expenses remained recoverable despite the failure to prove bad faith in handling a revised booking.
- [*Zalamea v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_104235_1993.html)[^211]. The carrier had to reimburse replacement fares caused by its undisclosed boarding priorities. A further refund would have given the passengers free transportation.
- [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650)[^212]. The carrier knowingly sailed with an unseaworthy vessel. Bad faith justified moral and exemplary damages, but the passenger still had to prove a loss of wages.
- [*Samhwa Company Ltd. v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jan1992/gr_74305_1992.html)[^213]. Guaranty costs, the loss in pledged shares and exchange differences measured the harm from leaving a bank undertaking unperformed. The sellers’ own debt reduced the collectible amount.
- [*Banzon v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jul1989/gr_47258_1989.html)[^214] distinguishes the event that occasioned a loss from the intervening fraud that directly caused it.
- [*G.A. Machineries, Inc. v. Yaptinchay*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1983/nov1983/gr_l-30965_1983.html)[^215] requires evidence of lost profits even when the seller fraudulently misrepresented the goods.
- [*Pamintuan v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-26339_1979.html)[^216] allows proven damages to exceed a stipulated sum where the seller performed fraudulently.
- [*Fores v. Miranda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1959/mar1959/gr_l-12163_1959.html)[^217] explains why a driver’s negligence alone does not establish the operator’s bad faith in a passenger’s contract claim.

## Suggested Answers

**Can a forwarding company recover profits it lost on a separate brokerage agreement?** In [*Orient Freight International, Inc. v. Keihin-Everett Forwarding Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63503)[^218] the subcontractor led the forwarding company and its customer to believe the shipment incident involved only a stalled truck. The customer later terminated the brokerage agreement for loss of confidence.[^219]

The termination was reasonably foreseeable in those circumstances. Financial records allowed the trial court to test and reduce the claim for lost net profits. The Supreme Court affirmed that assessment.[^220]

**Does a buyer lose every claim for damages if it cannot prove what it would have earned on resale?** In [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060)[^221] the buyer established that nondelivery deprived it of opportunities to sell fertilizer. Its evidence did not establish how much it would have earned.[^222]

The Supreme Court deleted the award for lost profits. It awarded temperate damages because the record established a pecuniary loss of uncertain amount.[^223]

**What can show bad faith when a seller accepts the balance of the price?** In [*Sta. Lucia Realty and Development, Inc. v. Lumawag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69310)[^224] the seller knew of an encroachment on the lot. It accepted full payment without telling the buyer and failed to deliver the deed and title. Those facts supported the finding of bad faith and the awards against the seller.[^225]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Focus: Reading the Problem**
>
> 1. Start with the undertaking. Identify who owed performance, what performance was due and how the obligor fell short.
> 2. Confirm that the obligation arises from a contract or a quasi-contract. A claim that rests on no contract, quasi-contract, delict or quasi-delict falls outside Article 2201. In [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832) the counterclaim rested on the filing of the suit. It had to be tested under Articles 19, 20 and 21 instead.
> 3. Decide which paragraph applies. Good faith is presumed. Bad faith must be proved.
> 4. Under the first paragraph fix the time for assessing foreseeability. The relevant circumstances are those when the obligation was constituted.
> 5. Under the second paragraph identify the conduct showing fraud, bad faith, malice or a wanton attitude. Then trace each loss to the non-performance.

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Focus: Good Faith or Bad Faith**
>
> - Negligence is not bad faith. In [*Fores v. Miranda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1959/mar1959/gr_l-12163_1959.html) the driver's reckless imprudence did not prove the operator's bad faith. Negligence may be so gross as to amount to malice. That fact must be shown in evidence.
> - A mistake in handling a booking can still be good faith. In [*China Airlines Limited v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jul1992/gr_94590_1992.html) the telexes showed a concerted effort to change the schedule. The carrier paid only the substitute fare and reasonable expenses.
> - Self-protection is not malice. In [*BPI Family Bank v. Franco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46696) the bank froze the funds to hold them as security. The Supreme Court found no fraud or motive of self-enrichment.
> - A knowingly false reason for the breach is bad faith. In [*Lirag Textile Mills, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/apr1975/gr_30736_1975.html) the employer alleged losses it knew to be untrue.
> - Conduct after the breach can show bad faith. In [*LBC Express, Inc. v. Spouses Ado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42513) the loss of the passport did not by itself prove bad faith. The excuses and the late notice did.
> - Silence about a known risk can be bad faith. In [*Zalamea v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_104235_1993.html) TWA never told the family that confirmed passengers could be left behind.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practical Application: Proving Each Item**
>
> - Proof that business was lost answers only part of the claim. In [*Orient Freight International, Inc. v. Keihin-Everett Forwarding Company, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63503) the financial statements allowed the trial court to test and reduce the claim for net profits. In [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060) the evidence established a lost opportunity to sell but left the amount uncertain.
> - Bring the records for the period in dispute. In [*Continental Cement Corporation v. Asea Brown Boveri, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32714) production reports for earlier months could not show a decrease in later months.
> - A summary or list is not a receipt. In [*Ilao-Oreta v. Spouses Ronquillo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45236) an unsigned list could not prove fuel and food expenses. In [*Adrian Wilson International Associates, Inc. v. TMX Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54215) payroll summaries and vouchers did not prove that the employees received their wages.
> - Fix the date of breach. Expenses incurred before the breach are not its consequences. The Supreme Court removed such expenses in [*Ilao-Oreta v. Spouses Ronquillo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45236).
> - Prove that the future income would have come. In [*LBC Express, Inc. v. Spouses Ado*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42513) the employment contract had expired and rehiring depended on the employer.
> - Fraud does not prove the amount of lost profits. In [*G.A. Machineries, Inc. v. Yaptinchay*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1983/nov1983/gr_l-30965_1983.html) the estimates of lost trips were speculative even though the seller misrepresented the engine.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practical Application: When Actual Damages Fail**
>
> - Ask for temperate damages in the alternative. The Supreme Court granted them where a loss was shown but its amount was not. See [*Bongcayao v. Confederation of Sugar Producers Cooperatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67060), [*Government Service Insurance System v. Spouses Deang*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52673) and [*Public Estates Authority v. Chu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43722).
> - Nominal damages vindicate a violated right when no loss is proved. In [*Savellano v. Northwest Airlines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48535) the passengers recovered nominal damages for the change of route without consultation.
> - Bad faith supports moral and exemplary damages but does not prove lost earnings. In [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650) the passenger recovered both kinds of damages but not half a day's salary.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practical Application: Stipulations, Set-Offs and Parties**
>
> - Read the penal clause. In [*Continental Cement Corporation v. Asea Brown Boveri, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32714) the daily penalty was recovered. The further damages failed for lack of proof and foreseeability.
> - Fraud can lift a stipulated ceiling. In [*Pamintuan v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-26339_1979.html) the buyer recovered the proven damages in place of the stipulated sum.
> - Net out what the claimant owes. In [*Samhwa Company Ltd. v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jan1992/gr_74305_1992.html) the sellers' own debt was deducted from their damages. In [*Vil-Rey Planners and Builders v. Lexber, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61984) the parties could compensate their mutual debts.
> - Sue the party bound by the obligation. In [*International Exchange Bank v. Rudy S. Labos and Associates, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68446) the developer's conforme did not make it a party to the deed. In [*Integrated Realty Corporation v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jun1989/gr_60705_1989.html) the issuing bank did not answer for interest under notes it never signed.
> - Identify the recipient and basis of each award. In [*Sta. Lucia Realty and Development, Inc. v. Lumawag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69310) the seller, the adjoining builder and the developer each had separate liabilities.

> [!NOTE]
> **Drafting Reminder for the Bench**
>
> Trace each award through every level of review. Name the court that affirmed, modified or reversed. State each amount with its payer, payee, basis and interest. When the decision recites two different figures for the same award, report both as the decision states them.
