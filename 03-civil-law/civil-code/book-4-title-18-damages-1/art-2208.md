> ART. 2208. In the absence of stipulation, attorney’s fees and expenses of litigation, other than judicial costs, cannot be recovered, except:
>
> (1) When exemplary damages are awarded;
>
> (2) When the defendant’s act or omission has compelled the plaintiff to litigate with third persons or to incur expenses to protect his interest;
>
> (3) In criminal cases of malicious prosecution against the plaintiff;
>
> (4) In case of a clearly unfounded civil action or proceeding against the plaintiff;
>
> (5) Where the defendant acted in gross and evident bad faith in refusing to satisfy the plaintiff’s plainly valid, just and demandable claim;
>
> (6) In actions for legal support;
>
> (7) In actions for the recovery of wages of household helpers, laborers and skilled workers;
>
> (8) In actions for indemnity under workmen’s compensation and employer’s liability laws;
>
> (9) In a separate civil action to recover civil liability arising from a crime;
>
> (10) When at least double judicial costs are awarded;
>
> (11) In any other case where the court deems it just and equitable that attorney’s fees and expenses of litigation should be recovered.
>
> In all cases, the attorney’s fees and expenses of litigation must be reasonable.[^civil-code]

# Explanation

## Scope, Dual Concept and Distinction from Judicial Costs

Article 2208 of the Civil Code prohibits a winning litigant from recovering attorney's fees and expenses of litigation other than judicial costs from the losing party unless the parties stipulated such recovery or the case falls under one of eleven enumerated exceptions. Every award of attorney's fees or litigation expenses, whether grounded on stipulation or on a statutory exception, must also be reasonable.

Philippine jurisprudence distinguishes two concepts of attorney's fees. In the ordinary concept, attorney's fees represent reasonable compensation paid by a client to counsel for legal services rendered under their employment agreement. In the extraordinary concept, attorney's fees under Article 2208 constitute an indemnity for damages ordered by a court to be paid by the losing party to the prevailing party. Because extraordinary attorney's fees belong to the client rather than to counsel unless they agree otherwise, a lawyer lacks standing as real party in interest to collect extraordinary fees directly from an employer after the clients compromise their claims, as the Supreme Court held in [*Ortiz v. San Miguel Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46105).[^2] Extraordinary attorney's fees operate as actual or compensatory damages rather than moral damages or judicial costs, so they must be specifically prayed for and cannot be inferred from a general prayer for equitable relief, as the Supreme Court ruled in [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650).[^3] Where a special statute independently authorizes attorney's fees, however, that special statute governs according to its own terms. In [*Prudential Guarantee and Assurance Inc. v. Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41756),[^4] the Court of Appeals had denied attorney's fees under Article 2208 for lack of bad faith, but the Supreme Court disagreed and awarded 10 percent attorney's fees under Section 244 of the Insurance Code upon finding unreasonable delay in paying marine insurance proceeds.

### Ordinary Versus Extraordinary Attorney's Fees

The leading formulation separating ordinary from extraordinary attorney's fees is [*Traders Royal Bank Employees Union-Independent v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34610).[^5] The union retained Atty. Emmanuel Noel A. Cruz on a P3,000.00 monthly retainer and obtained a final Supreme Court judgment awarding union members P175,794.32 in holiday pay differentials against Traders Royal Bank, with no award of attorney's fees against the bank. After finality, Atty. Cruz moved before the Labor Arbiter to determine his professional fees. The Labor Arbiter ordered the union to pay the law firm P17,574.43 (10 percent of P175,794.32) and the National Labor Relations Commission affirmed. The union argued that the labor tribunal altered a final judgment and that the P3,000.00 monthly retainer already covered the litigation.

The Supreme Court explained that ordinary attorney's fees are reasonable compensation paid by a client to a lawyer based on employment and agreement, whereas extraordinary attorney's fees are an indemnity for damages ordered by a court to be paid by the losing party under laws such as Article 2208 of the Civil Code and belong to the client unless they agreed otherwise. Because Atty. Cruz sought ordinary professional fees from his own client as an incident of the main labor action, the omission of extraordinary fees against the bank did not bar the Labor Arbiter from adjudicating counsel's fees after finality. The Supreme Court added that Article 111 of the Labor Code governs extraordinary fees against the losing party and is not the sole measure for ordinary client-counsel compensation, which rests on quantum meruit. The Supreme Court modified the National Labor Relations Commission resolution and ordered the union to pay Atty. Cruz P10,000.00 as attorney's fees for his legal services.

### Separating Counsel's Contingent Contract from Extraordinary Damages

In [*National Power Corp. v. Heirs of Macabangkit Sangkay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40020),[^6] the Supreme Court corrected a judgment that merged an ordinary contingent fee contract into an extraordinary Article 2208 award. After discovering that National Power Corporation had constructed an underground hydroelectric tunnel beneath their 227,065-square-meter land, the Heirs of Macabangkit Sangkay sued before the Regional Trial Court. The trial court ordered National Power Corporation to pay P113,532,500.00 as actual damages or just compensation at P500.00 per square meter (amid valuation references of P400.00, P500.00, P700.00 and P750.00 per square meter), P30,000.00 monthly rental from 1979 to July 1999 with 12 percent annual interest, P200,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and 15 percent of the total award as attorney's fees plus costs, after reducing a supposed 20 percent contingency fee to 15 percent. The Court of Appeals affirmed in toto.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court held that by referring to contingency fees and reducing them from 20 percent to 15 percent, the trial court dealt with an ordinary client-lawyer fee agreement yet improperly shifted it onto the losing defendant as extraordinary damages without stating factual, legal or equitable justifications in the text of the decision. Reaffirming that attorney's fees under Article 2208 are the exception and may still be withheld even under compelled litigation absent bad faith beyond an erroneous conviction of the righteousness of a party's cause, the Supreme Court exercised its supervisory authority to strike down the award even though National Power Corporation had not assigned it as error. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification, imposing 12 percent annual interest on the P113,532,500.00 just compensation from November 21, 1997 until full payment, deleting the P30,000.00 rental fee, P200,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and 15 percent attorney's fees against National Power Corporation, partly granting Atty. Macarupung Dibaratun's lien at 10 percent of P113,532,500.00 on quantum meruit, declaring Atty. Manuel D. Ballelos entitled to P5,000.00 on quantum meruit from six named heirs and assessing costs of suit against National Power Corporation.

### Attorney's Fees Versus Rule 142 Judicial Costs

Sitting En Banc in [*Go v. Saint Jude Catholic School Manila, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69952),[^7] the Supreme Court drew a bright line between Article 2208 attorney's fees and judicial costs under Rule 142 of the Rules of Court. Branch 97 of the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City had rendered a final judgment ordering Saint Jude Catholic School Manila, Inc., Fr. Marcelino Nicasio, SVD and Fatima De Guzman principally and solidarily (with Spouses Eugene and Joanne Lee subsidiarily liable) to pay Atty. Melita Go and Wayne Go PHP 21,128.00 actual damages, PHP 100,000.00 exemplary damages, PHP 100,000.00 attorney's fees and cost of suit. On execution, petitioners submitted a bill of costs claiming PHP 348,014.00 in additional costs of suit, including PHP 264,000.00 for their counsels' attendance at 41 hearings. The trial court denied the PHP 348,014.00 claim and petitioners filed a Rule 45 petition on a pure question of law.

The Supreme Court En Banc explained that prior to the effectivity of the Civil Code on August 30, 1950, attorney's fees could be recovered only as costs, whereas Article 2208 now allows recovery of reasonable attorney's fees as actual or compensatory damages under a stipulation or the eleven enumerated instances. Nothing in the law, however, permits simultaneous recovery of attorney's fees both as damages under Article 2208 and as judicial costs under Rule 142 beyond the exclusive fixed items in Rule 142, Sections 9 to 11. In the Regional Trial Court, Rule 142, Section 10 allows only a flat PHP 20.00 covering all counsel appearances down to final judgment and PHP 15.00 for the complaint or answer, which Section 6 declares has no relation to fees charged by counsel against a client. The Supreme Court further held that taxation of costs in superior courts belongs in the first instance to the clerk of court on five days' written notice under Rule 142, Section 8 rather than to the judge. The Supreme Court denied the petition and remanded the case to Branch 97, Regional Trial Court, Quezon City, ordering it to direct its clerk of court to assess costs of suit strictly under Rule 142.

### Bar Against Taxing Unadjudicated Attorney's Fees as Post-Judgment Costs

The rule against adding unadjudicated attorney's fees through a post-judgment bill of costs was laid down in [*Damasen v. Hernando*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/apr1981/gr_49995_1981.html).[^8] After private respondent Cecilia Quimang was acquitted by the Court of Appeals in a criminal case for grave oral defamation, complainant Segundina M. Damasen filed an independent civil action for damages before the Court of First Instance of Abra, Branch I. On August 27, 1970, Judge Juan Aquino dismissed the damages suit and ordered Damasen to pay Quimang P1,000.00 actual damages, P1,000.00 moral and exemplary damages and costs of the proceedings, without awarding attorney's fees. After the judgment became final, respondent Judge Harold M. Hernando approved Quimang's Bill of Costs on January 9, 1979, allowing P20.00 for defendant's court attendance, disallowing P50.00 for transcript and approving P2,500.00 as attorney's fees under Article 2208(1) because exemplary damages had been awarded in 1970.

On certiorari, the Supreme Court held that attorney's fees are not normally taxable as judicial costs. Even though exemplary damages had been granted in the final judgment under Article 2208(1), attorney's fees could not be adjudicated afterward in a Bill of Costs where the judgment proper and decretal portion contained no specific award for them. Adjudicating attorney's fees requires judicial discretion supported by proof of the amount (*Warner, Barnes v. Luzon Surety*, as cited in [*Damasen*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/apr1981/gr_49995_1981.html)[^9]) and express factual findings (*Estate of Buan v. Camaganacan*, as cited in [*Damasen*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/apr1981/gr_49995_1981.html)[^10]), whereas taxing a Bill of Costs is a ministerial function performed initially by the clerk of court. The Supreme Court set aside the January 9, 1979 Order and approval of the Bill of Costs, eliminated the P2,500.00 attorney's fees, limited recoverable costs to P15.00 for the answer and P20.00 for defendant's attendance and lifted its restraining order.

## Contractual Stipulations and Judicial Control of Reasonableness

The opening clause of Article 2208 permits parties to stipulate the recovery of attorney's fees and litigation expenses in a contract. When a promissory note, lease, deed of undertaking or credit agreement expressly provides for attorney's fees upon default or judicial enforcement, that clause operates as a penal clause in the nature of liquidated damages binding on the contracting parties. Government-owned or controlled corporations represented by the Office of the Government Corporate Counsel may likewise recover stipulated attorney's fees because litigation costs are borne directly by the agency or indirectly by the public, as the Supreme Court ruled in [*Pacific Mills, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/feb1992/gr_87182_1992.html).[^11]

Two statutory limits nevertheless restrain contractual stipulations. First, courts cannot award attorney's fees twice by adding an unstipulated percentage fee on top of a judgment balance that already incorporates the contractually stipulated fee, as the Supreme Court held in [*Spouses Consing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45955).[^12] Second, the final paragraph of Article 2208 mandates that in all cases the attorney's fees and expenses of litigation must be reasonable. Courts therefore retain supervisory power to reduce iniquitous or unconscionable fee awards, whether fixed by contract or computed as a statutory percentage of an increased principal award.

### Stipulated Fees as Penal Clause, Judicial Reduction and Non-Privy Surety Liability

In [*Trade & Investment Development Corporation of the Philippines v. Roblett Industrial Construction Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41596),[^13] the Supreme Court addressed stipulated fees, judicial moderation and a non-privy surety's liability. Roblett Industrial Construction Corporation and Roberto and Leticia Abiera executed a Deed of Undertaking in favor of Philguarantee (later Trade & Investment Development Corporation of the Philippines) stipulating 16 percent annual interest, 16 percent monthly-compounded penalty and 10 percent attorney's fees, backed by Paramount Insurance Corporation's surety bond for P11,775,611.35 at 18 percent annual interest without an attorney's fees clause. After Philguarantee paid the called Kuwaiti bid bond and sued, the Regional Trial Court held Roblett, the Abieras and Paramount solidarily liable for P11,775,611.25 plus interest and P100,000.00 attorney's fees and held Roblett and the Abieras solidarily liable for P18,029,219.78 plus interest. The Court of Appeals affirmed except in three respects. It ruled that Paramount's bond had expired without any claim made on it. It deleted the attorney's fees because the trial court had not justified them in the body of its decision. It also fixed the legal interest at 6 percent a year.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court held that courts retain authority to reduce stipulated attorney's fees, whether intended as an indemnity or a penalty, whenever iniquitous or unconscionable. Because the P11,775,611.35 principal had ballooned with roughly P37 million in interest so that 10 percent of principal plus interest and penalties would exceed the principal itself, the Supreme Court reduced the stipulated fee against Roblett and the Abieras to 10 percent of the principal debt only. As to Paramount, although it was not privy to the Deed of Undertaking and could not be bound by its contractual fee clause, Paramount remained solidarily liable with Roblett and the Abieras for P100,000.00 as reasonable attorney's fees. The Supreme Court rested that liability on Article 2208 and its instances where an award is just and equitable rather than on the Deed. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated the Regional Trial Court judgment with modifications ordering solidary payment of P11,775,611.25 (Paramount at 18 percent annual interest and Roblett and the Abieras at 16 percent annual interest plus 16 percent monthly-compounded penalty from June 5, 1990), P18,029,219.78 plus 12 percent post-finality interest against Roblett and the Abieras, 10 percent of P11,775,611.25 as attorney's fees plus costs against Roblett and the Abieras, P100,000.00 solidary attorney's fees against Paramount with Roblett and the Abieras, solidary reimbursement to Paramount by Roblett and Baltazar F. Benlot and 12 percent post-finality legal interest on all respective awards.

### Tempering a 25 Percent Stipulation on a Major Commercial Fraud Judgment

The Supreme Court exercised its moderation power over a stipulated percentage on a massive commercial judgment in [*Virata v. Ng Wee*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63192).[^14] Investor Alejandro Ng Wee placed funds totaling P213,290,410.36 at maturity through Westmont Investment Corporation (Wincorp) in transactions with Power Merge Corporation controlled by Luis Juan L. Virata, backed by agreements stipulating 3 percent monthly penalty interest, 20 percent liquidated damages and 25 percent attorney's fees. The Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch 39 ordered Virata, UEM-MARA Philippines Corporation, Wincorp and named Wincorp directors and officers (dismissing the complaint against Manuel Tankiansee) solidarily to pay P213,290,410.36 with 12 percent interest, 20 percent liquidated damages, 25 percent attorney's fees and P100,000.00 moral damages plus costs. The Court of Appeals affirmed with interest modification.

Resolving five consolidated Rule 45 petitions, the Supreme Court affirmed the nullification of the 3 percent monthly penalty interest and reduced liquidated damages from 20 percent to 10 percent under Article 2227 because the P213,290,410.36 base indebtedness was already staggering. Turning to attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that although Ng Wee established entitlement under Article 2208 and the contractual stipulation, Article 2208 mandates that in all cases attorney's fees and expenses of litigation must be reasonable. It therefore tempered the stipulated rate from 25 percent down to 5 percent of the total amount due. The Supreme Court partially granted G.R. No. 220926, denied the four other petitions, dismissed the complaint against Tankiansee and UEM-MARA, affirmed with modification ordering Virata, Wincorp, Antonio T. Ong, Anthony T. Reyes, Simeon Cua, Vicente and Henry Cualoping, Mariza Santos-Tan and Manuel Estrella solidarily to pay P213,290,410.36 (plus 12 percent annual interest from October 19, 2000 to June 30, 2013 and 6 percent from July 1, 2013 until full payment), 10 percent liquidated damages and 5 percent attorney's fees at the same interest rates, P100,000.00 moral damages (rejecting the P5,000,000.00 claim) and 6 percent additional post-finality interest, granted Virata's reimbursement cross-claim against Wincorp and its liable officers and assessed costs against the liable defendants.

### Moderating Stipulated Fees After a Pending-Appeal Restructuring

In [*Philippine Fisheries Development Authority v. Pascual*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70016),[^15] the Supreme Court laid down concrete factors for moderating stipulated attorney's fees when a debtor settles pending appeal. Assignee Mario Daniel Eduardo G. Pascual assumed Lucila S. Bautista's lease under a Contract of Lease whose Section 17 stipulated reasonable attorney's fees, litigation expenses and costs of suit if either party resorted to judicial proceedings. Branch 87 of the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City dismissed Pascual's complaint and ordered him to pay Philippine Fisheries Development Authority PHP 5,786,094.35 in rental arrears plus 6 percent legal interest from July 22, 2016 and PHP 300,000.00 as attorney's fees and costs of suit. While the appeal was pending before the Court of Appeals, Pascual entered into a Restructuring Agreement acknowledging PHP 7,804,835.27 in arrears payable at PHP 52,000.00 monthly. The Court of Appeals reversed, enjoined collection of Bautista's arrears and deleted attorney's fees.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court held that Article 2208 authorizes recovery of reasonable attorney's fees when contractually stipulated and that Philippine Fisheries Development Authority remained entitled to fees for litigation expenses already incurred notwithstanding the Restructuring Agreement. To determine a reasonable sum, the Supreme Court stated that courts may consider the legal extent of the work undertaken, the length of time elapsed to prosecute the case and any unjustified refusal by the losing party to pay a clearly demandable claim. Because Pascual voluntarily offered to settle the debt while the case was pending before the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court reduced the trial court's PHP 300,000.00 award to PHP 100,000.00. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated Branch 87's dismissal of the complaint for lack of cause of action with modification ordering Pascual to pay Bautista's rental arrears under the Restructuring Agreement, PHP 100,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of suit, with 6 percent annual legal interest on attorney's fees and costs from finality until full payment.

### Converting a Percentage Award to a Fixed Sum After Appellate Increase

The reasonableness requirement of Article 2208's final paragraph also controls percentage awards when an appellate court sharply raises the principal damages. In [*University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70367),[^16] student Cheryl Sarate died after her costume caught fire during an unsupervised campus beauty pageant. Branch 11 of the Regional Trial Court of Davao City initially ordered the university, its officials and faculty adviser Catherine Roble jointly and severally to pay Antonio and Rosita Sarate PHP 50,000.00 civil indemnity, PHP 200,000.00 exemplary damages, PHP 500,000.00 moral damages and 20 percent attorney's fees (20 percent of PHP 750,000.00), then absolved the university on reconsideration. The Court of Appeals set aside the reconsideration order and reinstated the original October 7, 2014 decision.

Affirming solidary liability En Banc, the Supreme Court substantially increased the principal awards to PHP 300,000.00 civil indemnity under Article 2206, PHP 5,000,000.00 moral damages and PHP 1,000,000.00 exemplary damages. Addressing attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that although the Civil Code permits attorney's fees when exemplary damages are imposed, attorney's fees and expenses of litigation must in all cases be reasonable. Because the damages had significantly increased, maintaining the trial court's 20 percent rate became unreasonable. The Supreme Court therefore found it just and equitable to modify the award to a fixed sum of PHP 150,000.00. It observed that this sum would have equaled the trial court's 20 percent rate had the original damages been fully affirmed. The Supreme Court denied the Rule 45 petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification ordering petitioners jointly and severally to pay PHP 300,000.00 civil indemnity, PHP 5,000,000.00 moral damages, PHP 1,000,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 150,000.00 attorney's fees, earning 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full satisfaction.

## General Prohibition, Textual Justification and Proof Requirements

The governing policy of Article 2208 is that no premium should be placed on the right to litigate. Attorney's fees and expenses of litigation are therefore the exception rather than the rule and are never granted automatically whenever a party wins a lawsuit. Because extraordinary attorney's fees partake of actual damages, judicial discretion under Article 2208 demands factual, legal and equitable justification grounded on the record rather than speculation or conjecture.

To enforce this discipline, jurisprudence mandates that the court explicitly state the factual, legal and equitable reasons for awarding attorney's fees in the body of its decision rather than mentioning the award for the first time in the dispositive portion. When a trial court omits body-text reasoning, the award violates both Article 2208 and Article VIII, Section 14 of the Constitution. Bare boilerplate asserting that a plaintiff was compelled to litigate also fails the statutory standard. Appellate jurisprudence reveals a sharp procedural boundary on curing a trial court's silence: in [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163),[^17] the Supreme Court held that the Court of Appeals should have disallowed a trial court's silent dispositive award instead of supplying Article 2208(1) on appeal, whereas in [*KLM Royal Dutch Airlines v. Tiongco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67711),[^18] the Supreme Court sustained 20 percent attorney's fees where the Court of Appeals conducted an independent assessment justifying the award under Article 2208(1) on validly affirmed exemplary damages.

### Factual, Legal and Equitable Justification in the Decision Body

The classic modern restatement of the body-of-decision rule is [*Scott Consultants & Resource Development Corp., Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_112916_1995.html).[^19] Scott Consultants sued Philippine Rock Products, Inc. before Branch 75 of the Regional Trial Court at San Mateo, Rizal. Branch 75 dismissed the complaint, dissolved the preliminary mandatory injunction and ordered Scott Consultants on counterclaim to pay P800,000.00 actual damages, P300,000.00 moral damages (written as P300,00.00 in the quoted order), P50,000.00 exemplary damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees and costs, remarking only that attorney's fees arising from the complaint were a compulsory counterclaim and reasonable under the circumstances. The Court of Appeals modified the decision by deleting moral damages while keeping actual damages, exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court held that attorney's fees are the exception rather than the rule and are not awarded every time a party wins a suit. Under Article 2208, including paragraph (11), the court's power to award attorney's fees demands factual, legal and equitable justification that cannot be left to speculation or conjecture. The court must explicitly state the legal reason in the body of the decision rather than solely in the dispositive portion. Because the trial court's brief remark contained neither a finding that an award was just and equitable nor a factual basis for P50,000.00, the Supreme Court partly granted the petition, deleted the awards of actual damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision in all other respects.

### Patent Nullity of Fallo-Only Attorney's Fees

In [*University of the Philippines v. Dizon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55112),[^20] the Supreme Court held that omitting body-text findings renders an attorney's fees award a patent nullity incapable of attaining finality. Stern Builders, Inc. and Servillano dela Cruz sued the University of the Philippines and its officials before Branch 80 of the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City. On November 28, 2001, Branch 80 ordered the university and its officials solidarily to pay P503,462.74 for the third billing, additional accomplished work and retention money, P5,716,729.00 actual damages, P10,000,000.00 moral damages, P150,000.00 plus P1,500.00 per appearance as attorney's fees and costs of suit. After the trial court and the Court of Appeals treated the university's notice of appeal as tardy, P16,370,191.74 of university bank funds were garnished and released.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court ruled that the university's appeal period had not lapsed under effective service and the retroactive fresh-period rule. It then struck down the unsubstantiated damages and attorney's fees. Because attorney's fees under Article 2208 are allowed in the concept of actual damages, their amounts must be factually and legally justified in the body of the decision rather than stated for the first time in the decretal portion. The trial court's lone conclusory sentence alleging an unjustified refusal to pay and a house re-mortgage failed Article 2199, Article 2208 and the constitutional mandate for a clear and distinct statement of facts and law, rendering the actual damages, moral damages and attorney's fees a conclusion without a premise and a patent nullity. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals, annulled the garnishment and release orders, deleted as void the P5,716,729.00 actual damages, P10,000,000.00 moral damages and P150,000.00 plus P1,500.00 per appearance attorney's fees (leaving the P503,462.74 principal standing subject to Commission on Audit action), ordered Stern Builders Corporation and Servillano dela Cruz to redeposit P16,370,191.74 within 10 days and assessed costs of suit against private respondents.

### Insufficiency of Boilerplate Compulsion-to-Litigate Recitals

In [*Philippine National Construction Corp. v. APAC Marketing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56034),[^21] the Supreme Court reviewed a petition directed solely at attorney's fees. APAC Marketing Corporation sued Philippine National Construction Corporation (PNCC) and two directors to collect P782,296.80, which PNCC paid while the case was pending. The trial court initially awarded P782,296.80 actual damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees plus P3,000.00 per court appearance and costs. On reconsideration, it modified actual damages to P220,234.083 (12 percent interest) while retaining the P50,000.00 acceptance fee and P3,000.00 appearance fee on a single sentence stating that the plaintiff was compelled to hire counsel and litigate by reason of an unjustified act. The Court of Appeals modified interest to 6 percent per annum from January 8, 1999 until full payment in January 2001, absolved directors Rogelio Espiritu and Rolando Macasaet and affirmed attorney's fees by simply quoting the trial court's single sentence.

The Supreme Court held that a rigid standard governs Article 2208 and requires courts to state factual, legal and equitable justification clearly and distinctly. The Court of Appeals made no independent finding and merely adopted a trial-court recital that was sorely inadequate under Article 2208, including paragraph (11). Because the lone discernible reason offered was that the claimant was forced to litigate to protect its interest without a compelling legal ground or showing of bad faith, the award failed. The Supreme Court granted the petition and modified the Court of Appeals decision by deleting the P50,000.00 acceptance fee and P3,000.00 appearance fee, with no pronouncement as to costs.

### Appellate Inability to Supply Missing Trial-Court Reasoning

In [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163),[^22] the Supreme Court enforced the body-of-decision rule even where exemplary damages were sustained. After a Mayamy bus driven by Mariano C. Mendoza and registered to Elvira Lim encroached into the opposite lane and collided with the Isuzu Elf truck of Spouses Leonora and Gabriel Gomez, the spouses sued for quasi-delict before Branch 172 of the Regional Trial Court of Valenzuela City. Branch 172 held Mendoza and Lim solidarily liable for P142,757.40 truck repair expenses, P1,000.00 daily unrealized income from March 7, 1997 to November 1997, P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees (appearing solely in the dispositive portion with nil discussion in the body) and costs of suit. The Court of Appeals deleted the P1,000.00 daily unrealized income but maintained the other awards, supplying Article 2208(1) on appeal because exemplary damages were awarded.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court held that from the opening sentence of Article 2208, attorney's fees remain the exception and require the trial court to state factual and legal findings in the text of its decision. Quoting *Spouses Agustin v. Court of Appeals* (as cited in [*Mendoza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163)[^23]), the Supreme Court ruled that when attorney's fees appear solely in the trial court's dispositive portion with nil discussion in the body, the Court of Appeals should disallow the award on appeal rather than supply a justification itself. Costs of suit, by contrast, properly followed the result under Rule 142, Section 1. The Supreme Court partially granted the appeal, declared Mendoza and Lim solidarily liable, maintained P142,757.40 truck repair expenses and granted P11,267.35 additional medical expenses with legal interest from January 31, 2001 until full payment, deleted the P100,000.00 moral damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees and maintained P50,000.00 exemplary damages and costs of suit.

### Appellate Cure Through an Independent Article 2208(1) Assessment

A notable procedural counterpoint to [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163)[^24] appears in [*KLM Royal Dutch Airlines v. Tiongco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67711).[^25] After Dr. Jose M. Tiongco's checked-in luggage went missing on an international itinerary ticketed through KLM Royal Dutch Airlines, Branch 10 of the Regional Trial Court of Davao City found breach of contract of carriage and ordered KLM to pay P3,000,000.00 nominal damages, P3,000,000.00 moral damages, P5,000,000.00 exemplary damages, P1,600,000.00 attorney's fees and costs, stating only that other just and equitable reliefs were prayed for and placing the fee amount in the dispositive portion. The Court of Appeals reduced moral damages to P1,000,000.00, exemplary damages to P300,000.00, nominal damages to P50,000.00 and attorney's fees to 20 percent of the total award, explaining that attorney's fees were warranted under Article 2208(1) because exemplary damages were awarded.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court agreed with KLM that the trial court failed to elaborate why Dr. Tiongco was entitled to attorney's fees. Nevertheless, the Supreme Court held that the Court of Appeals did not merely adopt the trial court's silent award but conducted an independent assessment explaining that attorney's fees were proper under Article 2208(1) on account of exemplary damages. Although brief, that independent appellate explanation supplied sufficient factual and legal justification. Twenty percent of the reduced total award was also reasonable. The Supreme Court denied KLM's petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification reducing moral damages to P300,000.00 (written as P300,000,00 in the dispositive portion) and exemplary damages to P100,000.00, awarding P50,000.00 temperate damages in lieu of nominal damages, maintaining 20 percent of the total monetary awards as reasonable attorney's fees, imposing 12 percent annual interest from January 16, 2006 to June 30, 2013 and 6 percent from July 1, 2013 until full payment and assessing costs against KLM.

### Deleting an Unexplained Multi-Million-Peso Fee Award

In [*Burgundy Realty Corp. v. Bella*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70154),[^26] the Supreme Court strictly applied the body-of-decision and bad-faith standards to a PHP 20,000,000.00 award. Daniela P. Bella, Geronimo Bella, Jr. and Rodrigo P. Bella sued developer Burgundy Realty Corp. over their share in a condominium joint venture project before Branch 65 of the Regional Trial Court of Makati City. Branch 65 ordered Burgundy to pay PHP 229,293,328.55 representing respondents' share plus PHP 20,000,000.00 for attorney's fees and litigation expenses solely in its dispositive portion. The Court of Appeals modified the monetary share to PHP 299,293,328.55 with 6 percent interest from finality while leaving the PHP 20,000,000.00 attorney's fees standing.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court reiterated that attorney's fees under Article 2208 require factual, legal and equitable basis stated in the body of the decision rather than merely in the dispositive portion. Even when a claimant is compelled to litigate or incur expenses, attorney's fees cannot be awarded absent sufficient showing of bad faith beyond an erroneous conviction of the righteousness of a party's cause. Because Branch 65 provided no factual or legal basis in the body of its decision and respondents failed to prove either entitlement to damages or bad faith on Burgundy's part in refusing their claim, the Court of Appeals erred in affirming the PHP 20,000,000.00 award. The Supreme Court partly granted the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals with modifications directing Burgundy to deliver and turn over 14 parking slots and enumerated condominium units (or if delivery is no longer possible, to pay prevailing market value determined by the Regional Trial Court with 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full payment) and deleted the award of attorney's fees.

## Enumerated Statutory Exceptions: Paragraphs (1) to (6), (9), (10) and (11)

Where no contractual stipulation authorizes fee-shifting, recovery must rest on one of the eleven statutory exceptions enumerated in Article 2208. Each paragraph establishes distinct legal prerequisites that the claimant must prove and the court must justify in its decision text.

### Paragraph (1): Award of Exemplary Damages

Article 2208(1) allows recovery of attorney's fees when exemplary damages are awarded. When the record establishes bad faith or deceit justifying exemplary damages, the Supreme Court sustains reasonable attorney's fees under paragraph (1). In [*Spouses Cesa v. Spouses Brucelas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69874),[^27] deceitful participation in a double sale of land supported PHP 50,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 200,000.00 attorney's fees. In [*University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70367),[^28] gross negligence warranted PHP 1,000,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 150,000.00 attorney's fees. Because paragraph (1) depends strictly on the validity of exemplary damages, the reversal of exemplary damages removes paragraph (1) as a legal ground.

### Collapse of Attorney's Fees Upon Reversal of Exemplary Damages

In [*Coca-Cola Bottlers Phils., Inc. v. Meñez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63737),[^29] the Supreme Court applied both the derivative limit of Article 2208(1) and the body-text justification rule. Ernani Guingona Meñez sued Coca-Cola Bottlers Phils., Inc. for damages after allegedly drinking kerosene from an opened Sprite bottle served at a Dumaguete City bar. Branch 39 of the Regional Trial Court of Dumaguete City dismissed the complaint for insufficiency of evidence and failure to establish chain of custody. The Court of Appeals reversed and ordered Coca-Cola to pay P200,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees and cost of suit with 6 percent interest from May 5, 1995 and 12 percent from finality, placing the P50,000.00 award solely in its dispositive portion without explanation in the body.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court reversed. First, the Court of Appeals provided no explanation in the body of its decision for the P50,000.00 attorney's fees, leaving only a surmise that it relied on exemplary damages. Second, because Meñez failed to prove chain of custody or gross negligence by the bottler, the reversal of exemplary damages eliminated Article 2208(1). Meñez also established no other ground under Article 2208. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and affirmed Branch 39's dismissal of the complaint for insufficiency of evidence.

### Paragraph (2): Compelled to Litigate with Third Persons or Incur Expenses

Article 2208(2) permits recovery when the defendant's act or omission has compelled the plaintiff to litigate with third persons or to incur expenses to protect his interest. Classic application of paragraph (2) occurs where a party's breach drags the claimant into a lawsuit filed by a third person, as in [*Escaño v. Ortigas, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43947),[^30] where petitioners failed to honor an undertaking to secure respondent's release from suretyships and left him impleaded in the creditor bank's collection suit. While some civil decisions have applied paragraph (2) to direct actions where a developer's default forced a buyer into prolonged litigation, such as [*Phinma Property Holdings Corp. v. Rivera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70021),[^31] two strict limits defined by the Supreme Court prevent paragraph (2) from swallowing the general rule: the third-person limitation established En Banc in [*Rizal Surety and Insurance Co. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/may1967/gr_l-23729_1967.html)[^32] and reaffirmed in [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730),[^33] and the bad-faith requirement formulated in [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832).[^34]

### Third-Person Litigation Limit and Boundary with Paragraph (4)

The foundational En Banc ruling construing Article 2208(2) and separating it from Article 2208(4) is [*Rizal Surety and Insurance Co. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/may1967/gr_l-23729_1967.html).[^35] After paying consignee Imperial Shirt Factory for four short-delivered bales of cotton remnants, subrogee Rizal Surety sought reimbursement from arrastre operator Manila Port Service (MPS) and carrier United States Lines Co. (U.S. Lines). Because MPS kept the claim under consideration, U.S. Lines furnished only unauthenticated verifax copies of tally sheets and the one-year prescriptive period was running, Rizal Surety sued both as alternative defendants under Rule 3, Section 13 in the Court of First Instance of Manila for P1,235.32. After MPS settled for P1,200.00 and the complaint was dismissed, the trial court and the Court of Appeals awarded U.S. Lines P300.00 attorney's fees on its counterclaim under Article 2208(2) and (4).

Reversing En Banc, the Supreme Court held that Article 2208(2) did not apply. The litigation expenses recoverable under the first clause of paragraph (2) are strictly those incurred in suing third persons, which means the expenses in the second clause refer to non-litigation expenses. Otherwise, the statutory distinction would lack justification. Moreover, a defendant sued in court cannot invoke paragraph (2) against the plaintiff: if sued necessarily, even a prevailing defendant recovers only judicial costs. If sued unnecessarily, the governing exception is Article 2208(4) for a clearly unfounded civil action. To be clearly unfounded under paragraph (4), the cause of action must be so untenable as to amount to gross and evident bad faith. Because neither MPS nor U.S. Lines admitted liability before suit and Rizal Surety faced prescription, impleading U.S. Lines in the alternative was reasonable and devoid of bad faith. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals decision awarding P300.00 attorney's fees to U.S. Lines, with no costs.

### Bad-Faith Requirement Beyond Erroneous Conviction of Cause

In [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832),[^36] the Supreme Court articulated the bad-faith limit on Article 2208(2) that subsequent decisions repeatedly quote. After film-licensing negotiations with VIVA Productions, Inc. broke down and VIVA licensed films to Republic Broadcasting Corporation (RBS), ABS-CBN sued VIVA, Vicente del Rosario and RBS before the Regional Trial Court. The trial court dismissed the complaint for lack of a perfected contract and ordered ABS-CBN to pay RBS P107,727.00 surety bond premium, P191,843.00 print advertisements, P1 million attorney's fees, P5 million moral damages and P5 million exemplary damages, plus P212,000.00 attorney's fees to VIVA. The Court of Appeals reduced RBS's moral damages to P2 million, exemplary damages to P2 million and attorney's fees to P500,000.00 on the ground that RBS was unnecessarily forced to litigate. ABS-CBN appealed while leaving VIVA's P212,000.00 attorney's fees unappealed.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court reversed every monetary award in favor of RBS. Reaffirming that attorney's fees generally cannot be recovered under Article 2208 because no premium should be placed on the right to litigate, the Supreme Court held that even when a claimant is compelled to litigate with third persons or to incur expenses to protect his rights, attorney's fees may not be awarded where no sufficient showing of bad faith is reflected in a party's persistence in a case other than an erroneous conviction of the righteousness of his cause. The Supreme Court also struck down actual damages and held that RBS, as a corporation, could not recover moral damages under Articles 2217 and 2219. The Supreme Court granted the petition and reversed the Court of Appeals decision except as to the unappealed P212,000.00 attorney's fees in favor of VIVA Productions, Inc., with no pronouncement as to costs.

### Paragraph (3): Criminal Cases of Malicious Prosecution

Article 2208(3) authorizes attorney's fees and expenses of litigation in criminal cases of malicious prosecution against the plaintiff. Because public policy protects free access to public authorities to report crimes, an accused's acquittal does not by itself establish malicious prosecution.

### Clear Preponderance of Deliberate False Accusation

The En Banc authority governing Article 2208(3) is [*Buenaventura v. Sto. Domingo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1958/mar1958/gr_l-10651_1958.html).[^37] Eight workers hired by estate administratrix Rufina Mandia gathered coconuts on land in Boac, Marinduque claimed by Damaso Sto. Domingo under a notarized deed of sale. Criminal charges were filed in the Justice of the Peace Court, where the eight hired workers were acquitted because the evidence did not warrant their inclusion in the complaint. The eight workers then sued Sto. Domingo and Chief of Police Filemon Ignacio for malicious prosecution before the Court of First Instance of Marinduque, seeking damages and attorney's fees. The trial court dismissed the complaint on March 8, 1956 for lack of sufficient evidence of malice.

Affirming En Banc, the Supreme Court held that under both the Civil Code provisions on moral damages and Article 2208(3) on attorney's fees and expenses of litigation, liability for malicious prosecution requires proof that the defendant acted deliberately and with knowledge that the criminal accusation was false and groundless. Submitting a complaint to public authorities does not make a private offended party liable for malicious prosecution because the State takes charge of prosecuting the offense. A Chief of Police who files a complaint in the Justice of the Peace Court is likewise shielded where the municipal judge determines prima facie probable cause. The Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's March 8, 1956 dismissal resolution with costs against plaintiffs-appellants.

### Paragraph (4): Clearly Unfounded Civil Action or Proceeding

Article 2208(4) allows recovery of attorney's fees in case of a clearly unfounded civil action or proceeding against the plaintiff. As the En Banc majority explained in [*Rizal Surety and Insurance Co. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/may1967/gr_l-23729_1967.html),[^38] a civil action is clearly unfounded under paragraph (4) only when the cause of action is so untenable as to amount to gross and evident bad faith. Thus, the Supreme Court sustained attorney's fees under Article 2208(4) and (11) in [*Trans-Pacific Industrial Supplies, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/aug1994/gr_109172_1994.html) where a borrower sued claiming full payment solely to capitalize on a bank's erroneous release of duplicate promissory notes despite its own written letters admitting P492,100.00 in unpaid accrued interest.[^39] Conversely, an action involving debatable issues or backed by an initial favorable ruling is not clearly unfounded, as the En Banc decisions in [*Malaluan v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666)[^40] and [*Angara v. Gorospe*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1957/apr1957/gr_l-9230_1957.html)[^41] confirm.

### Detailed Bad-Faith Findings Sustaining a Paragraph (4) Award

A leading modern application of Article 2208(4) is [*Spouses Timado v. Rural Bank of San Jose, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62143).[^42] After defaulting on their loan secured by two mortgages with Rural Bank of San Jose, Inc., Spouses Mamerto and Delia Timado filed a reformation suit to halt foreclosure and later filed indirect contempt petitions despite knowing that no temporary restraining order or injunction had been issued, while altering their signatures to mislead the court. In a joint decision in Civil Case No. IR-2974 and Special Civil Action No. IR-3187, the Regional Trial Court dismissed their complaints. In the reformation case, it ordered them to pay Teddy Monasterio P500,000.00 moral damages and P300,000.00 exemplary damages plus P50,000.00 acceptance fee and P1,500.00 per appearance. In the contempt case, it ordered them to pay P200,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 counsel services. The Court of Appeals deleted all moral damages, reduced exemplary damages and affirmed attorney's fees.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court deleted exemplary damages altogether because exemplary damages cannot stand once moral damages are eliminated without another compensatory predicate. Nevertheless, the Supreme Court sustained attorney's fees independently under Article 2208(4) because the trial court expressly stated its justification in the body of its decision supported by five concrete bad-faith findings, including filing indirect contempt with full awareness that no injunction existed and changing signatures to deceive the court. Exercising its moderation power, the Supreme Court partially granted the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals decision with modification deleting exemplary damages and fixing attorney's fees at a single sum of P100,000.00 and assessed costs against Spouses Timado.

### Paragraph (5): Gross and Evident Bad Faith in Refusing a Valid Claim

Article 2208(5) authorizes attorney's fees where the defendant acted in gross and evident bad faith in refusing to satisfy the plaintiff's plainly valid, just and demandable claim. Where an obligor actively obtains repeated credit extensions and then breaks its promise to pay earned premiums despite repeated demands, the Supreme Court sustains attorney's fees under Article 2208(2) and (5), as in [*Chartis Philippines Insurance, Inc. v. Cyber City Teleservices, Ltd.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67552).[^43] Likewise, in construction arbitration against a government instrumentality, the Supreme Court upheld P350,000.00 attorney's fees under Article 2208(5) where the agency refused valid claims in gross and evident bad faith in [*Tourism Infrastructure and Enterprise Zone Authority v. Global-V Builders Co.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64609).[^44] By contrast, resisting a claim in honest reliance on a colorable legal position does not constitute gross and evident bad faith.

### Untenable Defense Distinguished from Gross and Evident Bad Faith

In [*Petron Corporation v. National College of Business and Arts*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41209),[^45] the Supreme Court strictly defined the bad-faith threshold under Article 2208(5). Seven Manila lots previously mortgaged to Development Bank of the Philippines were sold to National College of Business and Arts (NCBA) while under levy. Petron later bought the sellers' undivided shares at an execution sale, secured final deeds of sale and intervened in NCBA's pending suit before Branch 30 of the Regional Trial Court of Manila. Branch 30 declared the levy and execution sale void, recognized NCBA's ownership and held Petron solidarily liable with the sellers and the bank for P100,000.00 exemplary damages and P150,000.00 attorney's fees under Articles 2208 and 2232. The Court of Appeals affirmed in toto.

On Rule 45 review limited to Petron's liability for exemplary damages and attorney's fees, the Supreme Court reversed. Construing Article 2208(5), the Supreme Court held that a losing party cannot be made to pay attorney's fees merely because a court rejects its legal position, because penalizing good-faith assertion of contending claims transgresses public policy. Even a clearly untenable defense does not justify attorney's fees unless it amounts to gross and evident bad faith. Petron's claim rested on final execution deeds of sale and was far from untenable. The trial court's bare remark that the levy and sale were void was sorely inadequate. Because Petron was not liable for compensatory damages in the form of attorney's fees, exemplary damages against Petron also fell. The Supreme Court granted the petition and modified the Court of Appeals and Regional Trial Court decisions by revoking Petron's liability for exemplary damages and attorney's fees.

### Paragraphs (6), (9) and (10): Legal Support, Crime-Based Civil Actions and Double Judicial Costs

Three enumerated paragraphs of Article 2208 govern specialized contexts: paragraph (6) applies to actions for legal support, paragraph (9) applies to a separate civil action to recover civil liability arising from a crime and paragraph (10) applies when at least double judicial costs are awarded. None of the decisions discussed on this page applies paragraph (9) or paragraph (10) in its own ruling. As to paragraph (6), in [*Ilano v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/feb1994/gr_104376_1994.html) the Supreme Court quoted the Court of Appeals' ruling that P10,000.00 attorney's fees were appropriate under Article 2208(6) and just and equitable in an action for compulsory recognition and legal support, concurring in and affirming that disposition absent proof of arbitrariness.[^46] Paragraphs (6), (9) and (10) otherwise appear only where Supreme Court decisions reproduce the statutory text of Article 2208 verbatim as background, as in [*Roxaco-Asia Hospitality Corp. v. Gulf Canary Construction and Development, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70194),[^47] [*Go v. Saint Jude Catholic School Manila, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69952),[^48] [*Coca-Cola Bottlers Phils., Inc. v. Meñez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63737),[^49] [*Alva v. High Capacity Security Force, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63711),[^50] [*Spouses Mercader v. Spouses Bardilas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62135),[^51] [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163),[^52] [*Philippine National Construction Corp. v. APAC Marketing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56034)[^53] and [*Petron Corporation v. National College of Business and Arts*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41209).[^54] Because every exception in Article 2208 remains governed by the opening rule and final reasonableness clause, any claim under paragraphs (6), (9) or (10) requires express justification in the body of the decision and a reasonable amount.

### Paragraph (11): Just and Equitable Cases

Article 2208(11) empowers courts to award attorney's fees and litigation expenses in any other case where the court deems it just and equitable. Paragraph (11) is a residual exception that requires concrete demonstration of equitable facts on the record rather than subjective sentiment. Where a government expropriator illegally occupies private land for decades without initiating expropriation proceedings and forces landowners to sue in inverse condemnation, the Supreme Court relied on the rule in Article 2208 that attorney's fees may be awarded where just and equitable and awarded PHP 200,000.00 as fair and reasonable attorney's fees in [*Heirs of Cipriano v. National Transmission Corp.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_255113_2025.html).[^55] Likewise, the Supreme Court invoked the just and equitable instances of Article 2208 to hold a non-privy surety solidarily liable for P100,000.00 reasonable attorney's fees in [*Trade & Investment Development Corporation of the Philippines v. Roblett Industrial Construction Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41596).[^56] Conversely, invoking paragraph (11) without factual demonstration results in deletion of the award.

### Bare Equitable Invocation as a Conclusion Without a Premise

The leading ruling disciplining judicial discretion under Article 2208(11) is [*Spouses Mercader v. Spouses Bardilas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62135).[^57] Adjoining Cebu City subdivision lot owners litigated ownership and extinguishment of a three-meter-wide road right of way in consolidated Civil Case Nos. CEB-12783 and CEB-13384. Branch 20 of the Regional Trial Court of Cebu City ruled for Spouses Mercader and ordered Spouses Bardilas to pay P100,000.00 moral damages. The Court of Appeals modified the judgment, declared Spouses Bardilas owners of the three-meter-wide road strip, dismissed Civil Case No. CEB-13384 and ordered Spouses Mercader to pay P20,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of suit simply stating that the fee award was made pursuant to paragraph 11 of Article 2208.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court deleted the P20,000.00 attorney's fees. Quoting *Eastern Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Margarine-Verkaufs-Union* and *Estate of Buan v. Camaganacan* (as cited in [*Mercader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62135)[^58]), the Supreme Court stressed that what is just and equitable under Article 2208(11) is not a mere matter of feeling but of demonstration borne out by findings of fact and law. Because the Court of Appeals expressed no factual or legal justification beyond citing paragraph 11 of Article 2208, the award was a conclusion without a premise that would place a prohibited premium on the right to litigate. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals judgment in Civil Case No. CEB-12783 with modification deleting the P20,000.00 attorney's fees and ordered Spouses Mercader to pay costs of suit.

## Labor and Seafarer Claims: Paragraphs (7) and (8) and Labor Code Article 111

Labor and maritime disputes frequently bring Civil Code Article 2208(2), (7), (8) and (11) into interaction with Article 111 of the Labor Code. Over time, Supreme Court jurisprudence developed distinct rules for wage-recovery actions, free Public Attorney's Office representation and seafarer disability indemnity claims, culminating in the 2024 doctrinal harmonization in [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730).[^59]

| Statutory Provision | Covered Claims | Bad Faith Threshold | Amount and Payee |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Labor Code, Art. 111** | Unlawful withholding and recovery of wages, not standalone disability or death indemnification claims | No proof of employer malice or bad faith required once lawful wages go unpaid | Maximum ceiling of 10 percent of recovered wages payable to the worker as extraordinary indemnity |
| **Civil Code, Art. 2208(7)** | Actions for recovery of wages of household helpers, laborers and skilled workers | No proof of employer malice or bad faith required when lawful wages are withheld | Reasonable amount harmonized with the 10 percent ceiling in labor proceedings |
| **Civil Code, Art. 2208(8)** | Statutory actions for indemnity under workmen's compensation and employer's liability laws (Title II, Book IV of the Labor Code), not contractual POEA-SEC claims under [*Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730) | Governed by the statutory indemnity nature of the claim | Reasonable amount subject to judicial moderation |
| **Civil Code, Art. 2208(2)** | Wrongful act compelling the plaintiff to litigate or incur expenses in relation to third persons | Requires showing of bad faith beyond erroneous conviction of cause | Reasonable amount justified in the decision body |

In actions for recovery of unpaid wages or illegal dismissal involving unlawful withholding of wages, Civil Code Article 2208(7) operates alongside Labor Code Article 111 as extraordinary indemnity payable to the employee without requiring proof of employer malice or bad faith. The 10 percent rate under Article 111 is a maximum ceiling rather than an automatic floor, so the Supreme Court may reduce it to a fixed reasonable sum when warranted by the scale of a collective award, as it did in [*Flight Attendants and Stewards Association of the Philippines (FASAP) v. Philippine Airlines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50154).[^60] Moreover, indigent employees represented by the Public Attorney's Office remain entitled to extraordinary attorney's fees, which are deposited in the National Treasury as a trust fund under Republic Act No. 9406.

By contrast, seafarer disability claims under the Philippine Overseas Employment Administration Standard Employment Contract (POEA-SEC) exposed sharp jurisprudential divergence. While Third Division decisions such as [*Nalica III v. Jebsen Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70212)[^61] still cited Article 2208(8) and Labor Code Article 111 to grant 10 percent attorney's fees in POEA-SEC disability suits, the First Division in [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^62] directly confronted the Supreme Court's vacillation and restricted Article 111 to wages, Article 2208(2) to third-person litigation and Article 2208(8) to statutory workmen's compensation laws rather than POEA-SEC contracts. Subsequent First Division rulings enforced [*Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^63] both in maritime disability litigation in 2026 in [*Arriesgado v. Multinational Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70389)[^64] and in land-based constructive dismissal in 2026 in [*ABC-RMO Security & Investigation Agency, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70571).[^65] Where bad-faith constructive dismissal concurrently involved unlawful withholding of wages under Labor Code Article 111 alongside Article 2208, however, the First Division sustained 10 percent attorney's fees in [*Gabrillo v. Tri-J Marketing, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70583).[^66]

### Harmonizing Labor Code Article 111 and Civil Code Article 2208(2), (8) and (11)

The watershed modern restatement of Article 2208 in labor and disability disputes is [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730).[^67] Pumpman Nicolas F. Bobiles sued local manning agency Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. and foreign principal V. Ships UK Ltd./Nordic American Tankers Limited for total permanent disability benefits after the company-designated physician failed to issue a valid final assessment within the extended period. The Accredited Voluntary Arbitrator in Buhi, Camarines Sur ordered petitioners solidarily to pay US$102,308.00 under the collective bargaining agreement plus 10 percent attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals modified the disability award to US$60,000.00 under the POEA-SEC and affirmed 10 percent attorney's fees under Labor Code Article 111 and Civil Code Article 2208.

Expressly addressing years of jurisprudential vacillation, the Supreme Court established four governing rules. First, Labor Code Article 111 applies strictly to unlawful withholding and recovery of wages, not standalone disability or death indemnification claims, expressly stating that the contrary reasoning in *Heirs of Aniban v. NLRC* (as cited in [*Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^68]) was inaccurate. Second, Civil Code Article 2208(2) embodies the wrongful act doctrine and requires that the defendant's wrongful act or omission compelled the plaintiff to litigate or incur expense in relation to third persons rather than in a direct lawsuit against the erring defendant, lest attorney's fees become the general rule and place a premium on the right to litigate. Third, Civil Code Article 2208(8) covers only statutory actions for indemnity under workmen's compensation and employer's liability laws (now Title II, Book IV of the Labor Code on Employee's Compensation and State Insurance Fund) and does not cover contractual POEA-SEC disability claims. Fourth, invoking the catch-all Article 2208(11) requires factual, legal or equitable justification beyond mere speculation (*Estate of Buan v. Camaganacan*, as cited in [*Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^69]). Finding none of those grounds present, the Supreme Court partly granted the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification subjecting the USD 60,000.00 award (or peso equivalent) to 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full payment while deleting the award of attorney's fees.

### Wage-Recovery Fees and Separation of Extraordinary from Contractual Union Fees

In [*Kaisahan at Kapatiran ng mga Manggagawa at Kawani sa MWC-East Zone Union v. Manila Water Co., Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32584),[^70] the Supreme Court clarified both the no-bad-faith rule for wage recovery and the boundary between extraordinary statutory awards and union-counsel fee contracts. The union and president Eduardo Borela sued Manila Water Company, Inc. before the National Labor Relations Commission for unpaid Amelioration Allowance (AA) and Cost of Living Allowance (COLA). Labor Arbiter Aliman D. Mangandog awarded AA, COLA, 6 percent interest and 10 percent attorney's fees. The National Labor Relations Commission deleted COLA, ordered payment of about P107,300,000.00 in accrued AA and maintained 10 percent attorney's fees despite a strike-settlement Memorandum of Agreement deducting 10 percent attorney's fees from union receivables (which the employer argued brought total fees to roughly P21.4 million). The Court of Appeals deleted the 10 percent attorney's fees for lack of bad faith and for supposedly violating Article 111's 10 percent cap.

Reversing on Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court held that under Article 2208(7) of the Civil Code, Article 111 of the Labor Code and Section 8, Rule VIII, Book III of the Implementing Rules, extraordinary attorney's fees in actions for recovery of wages require no showing of employer malice or bad faith, because a plain showing that lawful wages were not paid without justification suffices alongside express findings of fact and law. Moreover, the 10 percent extraordinary attorney's fees assessed against the losing employer as indemnity to the union members rest on a distinct obligation from the 10 percent ordinary attorney's fees deducted under the union's Memorandum of Agreement, so awarding extraordinary fees against the employer did not breach Article 111's 10 percent ceiling. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and reinstated the Labor Arbiter's award of 10 percent attorney's fees on the total judgment award, with no pronouncement as to costs.

### Free Public Attorney's Office Representation and Extraordinary Attorney's Fees

In [*Alva v. High Capacity Security Force, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63711),[^71] the Supreme Court resolved whether representation by the Public Attorney's Office (PAO) bars an award of extraordinary attorney's fees. Represented by the PAO, Joselito A. Alva sued High Capacity Security Force, Inc. and Armando M. Villanueva for illegal dismissal and unpaid benefits. The Labor Arbiter ordered reinstatement or separation pay, backwages and 10 percent attorney's fees. The National Labor Relations Commission modified the ruling to P30,000.00 nominal damages, P52,890.00 holiday, service incentive leave and 13th month pay and 10 percent attorney's fees, then later reconsidered. On certiorari, the Court of Appeals declared the dismissal illegal and ordered separation pay, backwages and statutory money claims computed by the Labor Arbiter with legal interest from finality, but dismissed Alva's claim for attorney's fees solely because he was represented by the PAO.

Reversing on Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court held that in labor cases attorney's fees under Article 111 of the Labor Code and the instances listed in Article 2208 of the Civil Code are extraordinary indemnity for damages belonging to the winning litigant upon a plain showing that lawful wages went unpaid without justification, without needing proof of employer malice or bad faith. The availment of free PAO services does not bar extraordinary attorney's fees because Section 16-D of Executive Order No. 292, inserted by Section 6 of Republic Act No. 9406, directs that attorney's fees adjudged against the adversary of a PAO client be deposited into the National Treasury as a trust fund for special allowances of PAO officials and lawyers. The Supreme Court distinguished *Lambo v. NLRC* as a ruling made under Executive Order No. 292 before Republic Act No. 9406 took effect (as cited in [*Alva*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63711)[^72]). The Supreme Court granted the petition and modified the Court of Appeals decision to integrate attorney's fees equivalent to 10 percent of the total monetary award.

## Arbitration, Election Contests, Public Officers and Temporal Boundaries

Article 2208 governs specialized forums and public-sector disputes with equal rigor. In Construction Industry Arbitration Commission (CIAC) proceedings, arbitral tribunals may award attorney's fees under Article 2208(5) when gross and evident bad faith is proven even against a government instrumentality, as in [*Tourism Infrastructure and Enterprise Zone Authority v. Global-V Builders Co.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64609).[^73] Where the CIAC Tribunal makes no finding of bad faith in a contractor's or surety's defense, however, merely losing on the merits cannot sustain attorney's fees or arbitration costs under Article 2208(2), as the Supreme Court ruled in [*Roxaco-Asia Hospitality Corp. v. Gulf Canary Construction and Development, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70194).[^74]

In election contests, the Supreme Court En Banc held in [*Malaluan v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666)[^75] that absent breach of contract, quasi-contract, tort, crime or specific statutory authority, damages cannot be awarded in election cases except attorney's fees under Article 2208. It further held that a differing appellate appreciation of contested ballots does not render an election protest clearly unfounded under paragraph (4). Regarding public officers, the En Banc majority in [*Angara v. Gorospe*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1957/apr1957/gr_l-9230_1957.html)[^76] ruled that a city officer entitled to representation by the City Attorney cannot shift private counsel's fees onto the opposing party without showing why the government lawyer could not defend her. It also held that an initial lower-court injunctive victory and a Supreme Court dissent negate a clearly unfounded civil action under Article 2208(4). Finally, as to temporal scope, the Supreme Court En Banc in [*Receiver for North Negros Sugar Co., Inc. v. Ybañez*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/aug1968/gr_l-22183_1968.html)[^77] held that Article 2208(11) of the new Civil Code does not apply retroactively to civil actions instituted before the Code took effect.

## Case Discussions

### Client Ownership of Extraordinary Attorney's Fees

In [*Ortiz v. San Miguel Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46105),[^78] Atty. Jose Max S. Ortiz represented employees in illegal dismissal suits (the Aguirre and Toquero cases) against San Miguel Corporation, where the National Labor Relations Commission awarded 10 percent attorney's fees. While San Miguel Corporation's certiorari petitions were pending before the Court of Appeals, all complainants except one executed Deeds of Release, Waiver and Quitclaim, from which San Miguel deducted and paid Atty. Ortiz 10 percent of the gross settlements. Atty. Ortiz then filed a Rule 45 petition in his own name claiming the difference between 10 percent of the original National Labor Relations Commission awards and the 10 percent already received under the quitclaims.

The Supreme Court held that attorney's fees awarded under Article 111 of the Labor Code and Article 2208(7) of the Civil Code belong to the extraordinary concept as indemnity for damages payable to the client rather than to counsel unless they agreed otherwise. Because the complainants did not agree to assign the National Labor Relations Commission award to counsel, they retained full authority to compromise or waive their claims without counsel's consent. Atty. Ortiz was therefore not a real party in interest under Rule 3, Section 2 to sue the employer. If counsel believed his compensation was deficient, his remedy was a separate collection suit against his own clients. The Supreme Court denied the petition and assessed costs against Atty. Ortiz.

### Specific Prayer and Proof Required Even With Exemplary Damages

In [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650),[^79] passenger Atty. Renato T. Arroyo sued inter-island carrier Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. for damages after M/V Asia Thailand departed Cebu with a repaired engine, stalled near Kawit Island and returned to port, where Arroyo disembarked and boarded another vessel the next day. Branch 24 of the Regional Trial Court of Cagayan de Oro City dismissed the complaint and counterclaim. The Court of Appeals reversed under Articles 1755, 2201, 2208, 2217 and 2232 of the Civil Code and ordered Trans-Asia to pay P20,000.00 moral damages, P10,000.00 exemplary damages, P5,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of suit, stating briefly that Arroyo was forced to litigate to claim moral and exemplary damages.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court affirmed moral and exemplary damages for recklessly dispatching an unseaworthy vessel, yet set aside the P5,000.00 attorney's fees. Under Article 2208, attorney's fees are recoverable only in the concept of actual damages rather than moral damages or judicial costs, so their amount must be proved and specifically prayed for instead of being inferred from a general prayer for just and equitable relief. Moreover, the Court of Appeals' brief statement fell short of the required factual, legal and equitable justification. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals decision subject to the modification setting aside the P5,000.00 attorney's fees and assessed costs against Trans-Asia.

### Independent Statutory Fee-Shifting Under Section 244 of the Insurance Code

In [*Prudential Guarantee and Assurance Inc. v. Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41756),[^80] shipowner Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. sued marine insurer Prudential Guarantee and Assurance Inc. before the Regional Trial Court of Cebu City to recover P8,395,072.26 representing the unpaid balance of fire loss to M/V Asia Korea after receiving P3,000,000.00 under a Loan and Trust Receipt. The trial court dismissed the complaint and ordered Trans-Asia to return P3,000,000.00 while denying attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals reversed, held the P3,000,000.00 (written as P3,000.000 in the quoted summary) to be partial policy settlement and ordered Prudential to pay P8,395,072.26 with double interest under Section 244 of the Insurance Code, while denying attorney's fees for lack of bad faith under Article 2208 of the Civil Code.

Resolving consolidated Rule 45 petitions, the Supreme Court held that the Court of Appeals erred in denying attorney's fees on the premise that bad faith was required under Article 2208. Instead, Section 244 of the Insurance Code independently directs insurers to pay damages consisting of attorney's fees and other expenses incurred by the insured plus double interest whenever payment of an insurance claim is unreasonably denied or withheld, without requiring proof of bad faith. Confirming Prudential's unreasonable delay in withholding P8,395,072.26, the Supreme Court denied Prudential's petition (G.R. No. 151890), granted Trans-Asia's petition (G.R. No. 151991) and modified the Court of Appeals decision by ordering Prudential to pay P8,395,072.26 plus 10 percent thereof as attorney's fees, subjecting the aggregate amount to 24 percent annual double interest from September 13, 1996 until full payment and imposing 12 percent annual interest on the total adjudged liability from finality until full satisfaction, with no costs.

### Prohibition Against Double Recovery of Attorney's Fees

In [*Spouses Consing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45955),[^81] sugar planters Spouses Antonio and Soledad Consing purchased fertilizer on credit from Sugar Producers' Cooperative Marketing Association (SPCMA) under written Fertilizer Orders stipulating 1 percent monthly overdue interest, 25 percent of the total amount due as attorney's fees and 10 percent liquidated damages (not less than P250.00). The Regional Trial Court of Negros Occidental, Bacolod City ordered Spouses Consing solidarily to pay P1,243,325.25 with legal interest from November 8, 1977 plus an additional 10 percent of the total unpaid obligation as attorney's fees and costs. The Court of Appeals affirmed.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court confirmed that Article 2208 authorizes recovery of attorney's fees under a written stipulation, so the 25 percent stipulated attorney's fees already incorporated into the adjudged P1,243,325.25 account balance were valid and binding. However, the Supreme Court deleted the separate 10 percent attorney's fees award because there is no legal basis for awarding attorney's fees twice, the lower courts stated no legal or equitable reason for the second award and the unstipulated extra 10 percent fell under no exception in Article 2208. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification ordering Spouses Consing to pay P1,243,325.25 with 1 percent monthly (12 percent annual) interest from April 30, 1983 until finality and 12 percent annual interest on the total obligation after finality until full payment, with no costs.

### Stipulated Attorney's Fees for Government Corporations Represented by the OGCC

In [*Pacific Mills, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/feb1992/gr_87182_1992.html),[^82] government-owned Philippine Cotton Corporation (Philcotton), represented by the Office of the Government Corporate Counsel (OGCC), sued Pacific Mills, Inc. and George U. Lim on promissory notes stipulating 21 percent annual interest, penalty charges and 25 percent attorney's fees. After a joint manifestation confirmed P16,598,725.84 in aggregate principal (distinguished from unliquidated sums under Article 2213 as illustrated by *Bareng v. Court of Appeals*, involving P3,600.00, as cited in [*Pacific Mills*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/feb1992/gr_87182_1992.html)[^83]), the trial court ordered solidary payment of P13,998,725.84 plus 21 percent regular interest, 21 percent additional interest, 8 percent penalty charges, 25 percent attorney's fees and costs. The Court of Appeals reduced attorney's fees from 25 percent to 10 percent of the principal recoverable and fixed penalty charges at 14 percent.

On Rule 45 review, petitioners argued that Philcotton could not recover attorney's fees because it was represented by government lawyers from the OGCC rather than private counsel. The Supreme Court rejected that contention under Republic Act No. 6000 (amending Republic Act No. 2327) and general principle, holding that a government-owned or controlled corporation compelled to sue over default may recover stipulated attorney's fees under Article 2208 because litigation costs are borne directly by the corporation or indirectly by the tax-paying public. Finding the appellate reduction from 25 percent to 10 percent of principal more than reasonable, the Supreme Court modified the Court of Appeals decision to order Pacific Mills and Lim solidarily to pay P13,998,725.84 with 21 percent annual regular interest from the promissory notes' due dates, 14 percent annual penalty charges starting 30 days after due dates and 10 percent of the principal amount recoverable as attorney's fees, plus costs.

### Sustaining Attorney's Fees Under Paragraph (1) for Bad-Faith Double Sale

In [*Spouses Cesa v. Spouses Brucelas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69874),[^84] Spouses Angel and Feliciana Cesa sued Spouses David and Elisa Montano Brucelas and Spouses Raymundo and Nelia Del Rosario after the sellers executed a subsequent June 17, 2002 Deed of Absolute Sale over Lot No. 1799 despite a prior sale to Spouses Cesa who were in possession. Branch 23 of the Regional Trial Court of Trece Martires City nullified the second sale and Transfer Certificate of Title No. T-1019104 and ordered Spouses Brucelas and Spouses Del Rosario to pay PHP 200,000.00 attorney's fees, PHP 50,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 50,000.00 moral damages. The Court of Appeals reversed.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court reinstated Branch 23's findings of deceit and bad faith by both the sellers and the second buyers who failed to exercise due diligence despite Spouses Cesa's possession. Because PHP 50,000.00 exemplary damages were properly awarded and bad faith was established, the Supreme Court held Spouses Cesa entitled to attorney's fees under Article 2208(1) and found the trial court's PHP 200,000.00 award reasonable under the circumstances. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated Branch 23's decision with modification declaring the June 17, 2002 sale void, ordering cancellation of Transfer Certificate of Title No. T-1019104, ordering payment of PHP 200,000.00 attorney's fees, PHP 50,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 50,000.00 moral damages (proceeded against the estate of Elisa Montano Brucelas and Spouses Del Rosario) and imposing 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full satisfaction.

### Classic Third-Person Litigation Under Paragraph (2)

In [*Escaño v. Ortigas, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43947),[^85] Rafael Ortigas, Jr. divested his shares in Falcon Minerals, Inc. under a 1982 Undertaking in which Salvador P. Escaño, Mario M. Silos and Joseph M. Matti obligated themselves to obtain Ortigas's release from his suretyships with Private Development Corporation of the Philippines (PDCP), defend him if sued and reimburse him within seven days if made to pay. Petitioners failed to obtain his release. PDCP then sued Ortigas in Civil Case No. 89-5128 before the Regional Trial Court of Makati, prompting Ortigas to settle with PDCP for P1,300,000.00 and pursue summary judgment against Escaño, Silos and Matti. The trial court held them solidarily liable for P1,300,000.00 with 12 percent interest and attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed.

On Rule 45 review, petitioners argued that the Undertaking contained no stipulation for attorney's fees. The Supreme Court rejected that defense under Article 2208(2) because petitioners' noncompliance with their contractual duty to secure Ortigas's release directly caused him to be impleaded in the third-party collection suit filed by PDCP. The Supreme Court granted the petition in part, modified the trial court orders to declare Escaño, Silos and Matti jointly (not solidarily) liable for P1,300,000.00 with 12 percent annual legal interest counted from judicial demand on March 14, 1994 rather than February 28, 1994, affirmed the assailed rulings in all other respects (including attorney's fees) and assessed costs against petitioners.

### Increasing Attorney's Fees Under Paragraph (2) in a Condominium Refund Action

In [*Phinma Property Holdings Corp. v. Rivera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70021),[^86] condominium buyer Joshua C. Rivera sought cancellation of his Contract to Sell and refund from developer Phinma Property Holdings Corporation after structural defects and unfinished amenities plagued Hacienda Balai Condominium. The Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board (now Human Settlements Adjudication Commission) Arbiter cancelled the contract and ordered Phinma to refund PHP 209,000.00 equity, PHP 14,500.00 move-in fee, PHP 25,000.00 improvement cost and PHP 93,600.64 Pag-IBIG amortizations, pay PHP 20,000.00 attorney's fees and costs and buy back Rivera's loan from Home Development Mutual Fund while dismissing the complaint against the fund. Both the Board of Commissioners and the Court of Appeals affirmed.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court excluded move-in fees and improvement costs from the refund and denied moral and exemplary damages because Rivera failed to prove bad faith or moral suffering and Phinma had already been fined. Applying Article 2208(2), however, the Supreme Court held that Phinma's failure to develop the project on time forced Rivera to retain counsel and litigate for seven years, warranting an upward modification of attorney's fees from PHP 20,000.00 to PHP 80,000.00 in view of his original PHP 60,000.00 complaint prayer and the seven-year duration. The Supreme Court partly granted the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification ordering Phinma to refund PHP 209,000.00 equity and PHP 93,600.64 Pag-IBIG amortizations with 6 percent annual interest from October 8, 2018 until full payment and to pay PHP 80,000.00 attorney's fees with litigation costs, with 6 percent annual legal interest on the total amount due from finality until full payment while leaving intact contract cancellation, loan buyback and dismissal against the fund.

### Clearly Unfounded Suit Exploiting Mistaken Release of Duplicate Notes

In [*Trans-Pacific Industrial Supplies, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/aug1994/gr_109172_1994.html),[^87] borrower Trans-Pacific Industrial Supplies, Inc. received duplicate promissory notes erroneously released by Associated Bank and then sued before Branch 146 of the Regional Trial Court of Makati claiming full payment of its restructured loan despite having written letters admitting P492,100.00 in unpaid accrued interest. Branch 146 declared the loan fully paid, ordered release of mortgages and awarded plaintiffs P50,000.00 moral damages and P30,000.00 attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals nullified the trial court decision, dismissed the complaint and ordered Trans-Pacific on counterclaim to pay Associated Bank P15,000.00 attorney's fees and costs under Article 2208(4) and (11).

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court rejected Trans-Pacific's claim that it sued in the honest belief that it had fully paid. It pointed to Trans-Pacific's undisputed admission of the P492,100.00 unpaid interest balance. It added that Trans-Pacific sued for specific performance and damages after a lawyer advised it that not even the interest could be recovered. It then quoted with approval the Court of Appeals' finding that Trans-Pacific's ill-advised attempt to capitalize on the duplicate notes made Article 2208(4) and (11) applicable and that P15,000.00 was fair and equitable. On that basis the Supreme Court denied the petition for lack of merit and assessed costs against Trans-Pacific.

### Gross and Evident Bad Faith After Broken Credit Extensions

In [*Chartis Philippines Insurance, Inc. v. Cyber City Teleservices, Ltd.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67552),[^88] insurer Chartis Philippines Insurance, Inc. (now AIG Philippines Insurance, Inc.) issued fidelity and professional indemnity policies to call center Cyber City Teleservices, Ltd. (CCTL), paid documentary stamps tax and repeatedly extended CCTL's credit term upon its broker's request. When CCTL broke its promise to pay and instead claimed the policies were void under Section 77 of the Insurance Code, Chartis sued before Branch 139 of the Regional Trial Court of Makati City. On summary judgment, Branch 139 ordered CCTL to pay US$47,304.00 (or peso equivalent) in earned premiums and taxes with 12 percent interest from complaint filing, P100,000.00 attorney's fees under Article 2208 and P60,713.32 costs of suit while dismissing CCTL's counterclaim. The Court of Appeals vacated the summary judgment and dismissed the complaint.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court held that the credit extension exception to Section 77 rendered the policies valid and binding and left the uncontroverted US$47,304.00 assessment undisturbed. Under Article 2208(2) and (5), the Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's award of P100,000.00 attorney's fees and P60,713.32 costs of suit because CCTL sought repeated credit extensions, broke its promise to pay despite several demand letters and constrained Chartis to sue. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated Branch 139's Order with modification ordering CCTL to pay US$47,304.00 (or peso equivalent) plus 12 percent annual interest from January 20, 2006 to June 30, 2013 and 6 percent from July 1, 2013 until full payment, P100,000.00 attorney's fees and P60,713.32 costs of suit.

### Appellate Application of Paragraph (6) in an Action for Legal Support

In [*Ilano v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/feb1994/gr_104376_1994.html),[^89] Merceditas S. Ilano, represented by her mother Leoncia de los Santos, filed an action on August 14, 1972 for compulsory recognition and legal support against Artemio G. Ilano. The trial court dismissed the complaint on April 24, 1981. The Court of Appeals reversed, declared Merceditas the duly acknowledged and recognized illegitimate child of Artemio and ordered Artemio to pay graduated monthly support in arrears (P800.00 from August 16, 1972 to August 15, 1975, P1,000.00 from August 16, 1975 to August 15, 1978, P1,300.00 from August 16, 1978 to August 15, 1981 and P1,500.00 from August 16, 1981 until she reached majority on December 30, 1984) plus P10,000.00 attorney's fees and costs.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court upheld the finding of illegitimate filiation and quoted the Court of Appeals' disposition stating that because the suit was an action for legal support, attorney's fees were appropriate under Article 2208(6) of the Civil Code and just and equitable under the circumstances. Without independent doctrinal elaboration of Article 2208(6), the Supreme Court concurred in the quoted disposition absent proof that it was arrived at arbitrarily. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals' December 17, 1991 decision and February 26, 1992 resolution.

### Equitable Attorney's Fees Benchmark in Inverse Condemnation

In [*Heirs of Cipriano v. National Transmission Corp.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_255113_2025.html),[^90] transmission lines were erected in 1977 (4,785 square meters) and 1994 (1,709 square meters) over petitioners' lots in Daraga, Albay without expropriation proceedings or payment of just compensation. Branch 1 of the Regional Trial Court of Legazpi City fixed just compensation at PHP 700.00 per square meter (PHP 4,545,800.00 for 6,494 square meters) and awarded PHP 50,000.00 attorney's fees and PHP 200,000.00 exemplary damages. The Court of Appeals set aside the judgment and remanded the case for proper determination of just compensation, exemplary damages and attorney's fees.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court invoked Article 2229 and the rule in Article 2208 that attorney's fees may be awarded where just and equitable (quoting *Heirs of Mariano* and *National Power Corporation v. Manalastas*, as cited in [*Heirs of Cipriano*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_255113_2025.html)[^91]) to hold that a government expropriator's prolonged illegal occupation of private land without expropriation proceedings warrants exemplary damages and attorney's fees even while just compensation is remanded. Benchmarking against ordinary inverse condemnation suits (*Tecson*, *Oroville* and *Manalastas*, as cited in [*Heirs of Cipriano*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_255113_2025.html)[^92]) rather than unlawful detainer origins (*Heirs of Mariano*, PHP 75,000.00, as cited in [*Heirs of Cipriano*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_255113_2025.html)[^93]), the Supreme Court fixed PHP 1,000,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 200,000.00 attorney's fees as fair and reasonable. The Supreme Court partially granted the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification reckoning just compensation from actual taking in 1977 and 1994 plus consequential damages earning 6 percent annual interest from finality of the fixing decision, ordered National Transmission Corporation to pay PHP 1,000,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 200,000.00 attorney's fees earning 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full payment, remanded to Branch 1 for determination of just compensation, consequential damages and costs of suit and assessed costs against TransCo.

### Tempering the 10 Percent Labor Ceiling on a Mass Retrenchment Award

In [*Flight Attendants and Stewards Association of the Philippines (FASAP) v. Philippine Airlines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50154),[^94] the Supreme Court resolved Philippine Airlines' motion for reconsideration of its July 22, 2008 decision that had declared the 1998 retrenchment of about 1,400 cabin crew members illegal and ordered reinstatement or separation pay, full backwages and 10 percent attorney's fees. In seeking reconsideration, Philippine Airlines alleged that its liabilities stood at about Php 85,109,075,351.00 against assets of Php 90,642,330,919.00 at retrenchment and warned that the monetary judgment could reach P2.3 billion.

While denying reconsideration on the illegal dismissal, the Supreme Court reaffirmed that a maximum of 10 percent of the total monetary award is justifiable as attorney's fees in wage-recovery suits under Labor Code Article 111, Section 8, Rule VIII, Book III of its Implementing Rules and paragraph 7 of Article 2208. Such an award is proper where lawful wages were not paid accordingly. The Supreme Court then tempered the award in view of the number of flight attendants and the totality of the amounts due to them. Relying on *Concept Placement Resources, Inc. v. Funk* (as cited in [*FASAP*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50154)[^95]), it held that courts may reduce attorney's fees found iniquitous or unconscionable. The flight attendants shared a common cause of action through the union and its counsel. The issues were simple and the strategies and arguments were common to all affected crew members. Considering the length of the litigation, the Supreme Court fixed the attorney's fees at P2,000,000.00, inclusive of all the union's litigation expenses. The Supreme Court denied the motion for reconsideration with finality, affirmed its July 22, 2008 decision with modification reducing attorney's fees and expenses of litigation to P2,000,000.00 and remanded to the Labor Arbiter solely to compute individual monetary awards under five guidelines.

### Third Division Application of Paragraph (8) to a Seafarer POEA-SEC Claim

In [*Nalica III v. Jebsen Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70212),[^96] seafarer Aurelio Dela Cruz Nalica III sought disability benefits for a right shoulder condition after the company-designated physician failed to properly deliver and explain a final medical assessment to him. The Panel of Voluntary Arbitrators held respondents solidarily liable for US$170,512.00 collective bargaining agreement disability benefits plus 10 percent attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals set aside the arbitral decision and awarded only Grade 9 disability benefits under the POEA-SEC.

On Rule 45 review, the Third Division held that Nalica failed to prove an onboard accident for collective bargaining agreement benefits but was entitled by operation of law to USD 60,000.00 total permanent disability benefits under the POEA-SEC. Without discussing [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730),[^97] the Third Division cited Article 2208(8) of the Civil Code alongside Labor Code Article 111 and Section 8, Rule VIII, Book III of the Implementing Rules to award 10 percent attorney's fees because Nalica was forced to litigate to protect his right under the POEA-SEC. The Supreme Court partially granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and ordered Jebsen Maritime, Inc., Hapag-Lloyd Aktiengesellshaft and/or Vivian Catalina Cristobal solidarily to pay USD 60,000.00 (or peso equivalent at payment) and 10 percent of the total monetary award as attorney's fees, both earning 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full payment.

### Enforcing *Bobiles* to Delete Seafarer Attorney's Fees Under Paragraphs (2) and (8)

In [*Arriesgado v. Multinational Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70389),[^98] oiler Ernesto D. Arriesgado suffered a work-related injury during an onboard basketball game and was repatriated, after which the company-designated physician terminated treatment without issuing a final assessment or furnishing medical reports. Labor Arbiter Marvin R. Osias dismissed the complaint. The National Labor Relations Commission reversed and awarded USD 104,866.00 collective bargaining agreement disability benefits plus 10 percent attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals set aside the commission decision and reinstated the dismissal.

On Rule 45 review, the First Division applied [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^99] to delete the 10 percent attorney's fees on three grounds: Labor Code Article 111 applies only to unlawful withholding of wages rather than disability compensation, Civil Code Article 2208(2) presupposes litigation expenses incurred in relation to third persons and Civil Code Article 2208(8) does not cover contractual indemnity claims under the 2010 POEA-SEC. On the substantive claims, the Supreme Court awarded USD 60,000.00 under the 2010 POEA-SEC, USD 2,564.00 sickness allowance less PHP 91,575.65 already received (paid in two tranches of PHP 36,801.08 and PHP 54,774.57) and PHP 50,000.00 nominal damages under Article 2221 for failing to furnish medical records and delaying the second tranche, while denying moral and exemplary damages because respondents acted in a genuine albeit mistaken belief. The Supreme Court partially granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals, reinstated the National Labor Relations Commission decision with modification ordering Multinational Maritime, Inc., MMS. Co., Ltd., Captain Banny B. Briones and MMI's corporate officers and directors solidarily to pay USD 60,000.00 disability benefits, USD 2,564.00 less PHP 91,575.65 differential sickness allowance (USD amounts payable in Philippine currency at prevailing exchange rate) and PHP 50,000.00 nominal damages, deleted attorney's fees for lack of basis, imposed 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full satisfaction and remanded to the National Capital Region Arbitration Branch for computation and conversion.

### Extending *Bobiles* to Land-Based Constructive Dismissal Under Paragraph (2)

In [*ABC-RMO Security & Investigation Agency, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70571),[^100] security guard Louiejie G. Bautista and three co-complainants sued ABC-RMO Security & Investigation Agency, Inc. (ARSIA) and General Manager Jaime J. Juadines for constructive dismissal and money claims. Labor Arbiter Maki T. Datu-Ramos II dismissed constructive dismissal while awarding PHP 110,421.03 across complainants (including Guiritan's PHP 5,370.00 service incentive leave pay, PHP 27,924.00 13th month pay and PHP 3,329.40 attorney's fees and Morallos's PHP 6,003.21 service incentive leave pay). The National Labor Relations Commission found constructive dismissal for Guiritan, Morallos and Balmadres (awarding each PHP 257,387.21 backwages, separation pay of PHP 74,100.00 for Guiritan and Morallos and PHP 59,280.00 for Balmadres, plus illegal deduction refunds of PHP 11,825.00 for Morallos, PHP 8,100.00 for Balmadres and PHP 10,800.00 for Bautista), while denying Bautista's illegal dismissal claim for procedural infirmity. The Court of Appeals granted Bautista's certiorari petition, declared him constructively dismissed and ordered ARSIA to pay full backwages, separation pay, PHP 25,000.00 moral damages, PHP 25,000.00 exemplary damages and 10 percent attorney's fees.

On Rule 65 review, the Supreme Court applied [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^101] and deleted the 10 percent attorney's fees under Civil Code Article 2208(2) because Bautista's suit did not compel him to litigate with third persons. On the merits, the Supreme Court affirmed constructive dismissal, PHP 25,000.00 moral damages and PHP 25,000.00 exemplary damages for ARSIA's bad-faith attempt to mask floating status beyond six months as abandonment. It agreed with the Court of Appeals that Juadines was not personally liable absent malice or bad faith. The Supreme Court dismissed the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals decision with modification deleting attorney's fees, ordered ARSIA to pay Bautista full backwages until finality, separation pay of one month pay per year of service, PHP 25,000.00 moral damages and PHP 25,000.00 exemplary damages with 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full satisfaction and remanded to the arbitration branch for computation and execution.

### Sustaining 10 Percent Fees Where Constructive Dismissal Concurs With Unlawful Withholding of Wages

In [*Gabrillo v. Tri-J Marketing, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70583),[^102] tire-recapping team leader Niño Rio Gabrillo, employed for over 20 years, was repeatedly pressured under threat of criminal charges to resign in exchange for financial assistance. The Labor Arbiter dismissed his illegal dismissal complaint. The National Labor Relations Commission reversed and awarded PHP 611,039.00. The Court of Appeals set aside the commission decision and reinstated the dismissal.

On Rule 45 review, the First Division reversed the Court of Appeals and found bad-faith constructive dismissal warranting full backwages, separation pay in lieu of reinstatement, PHP 30,000.00 moral damages and PHP 30,000.00 exemplary damages. Invoking Civil Code Article 2208 in relation to Labor Code Article 111 (citing compelled litigation to protect interest, just and equitable grounds and unlawful withholding of wages), the Supreme Court held that because Gabrillo was compelled to sue to protect his right to continued employment and recover illegally withheld monetary benefits, 10 percent attorney's fees on the total monetary award were proper. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution, declared Gabrillo illegally dismissed, ordered Tri-J Marketing, Inc. to pay full backwages from February 11, 2019 until finality, separation pay of one month salary per year of service until finality, PHP 30,000.00 moral damages, PHP 30,000.00 exemplary damages and 10 percent attorney's fees with 6 percent annual legal interest from finality until full payment and remanded to the Labor Arbitration Branch for computation.

### Deleting Arbitral Attorney's Fees and Arbitration Costs Absent Bad Faith

In [*Roxaco-Asia Hospitality Corp. v. Gulf Canary Construction and Development, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70194),[^103] hotel owner Roxaco-Asia Hospitality Corporation terminated its construction contract with Gulf Canary Construction and Development, Inc. and claimed against surety Asia United Insurance, Inc. The Construction Industry Arbitration Commission (CIAC) Tribunal upheld termination and ordered Gulf Canary (with Asia United solidarily liable under its performance bond) to pay PHP 14,305,098.96 liquidated damages, PHP 29,701,532.37 downpayment recoupment, PHP 16,964,878.38 rectification cost, attorney's fees and arbitration costs, stating that Roxaco was compelled to obtain counsel to protect its interest. The Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed Roxaco's complaint.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated the CIAC Final Award on the merits, yet deleted both attorney's fees and arbitration costs. Reaffirming under Article 2208 that no premium should be placed on the right to litigate and quoting *President of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints v. BTL Construction* (as cited in [*Roxaco-Asia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70194)[^104]), the Supreme Court held that even when a claimant is compelled to litigate or incur expenses, attorney's fees cannot be awarded absent a sufficient showing of bad faith beyond an erroneous conviction of the righteousness of a party's cause. Because the CIAC Tribunal made no finding that Gulf Canary and Asia United defended the arbitration in bad faith, merely being wrong on the merits could not sustain attorney's fees or arbitration costs. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals and affirmed the CIAC Final Award with modification that Roxaco is not entitled to attorney's fees and costs of arbitration, imposing 6 percent annual legal interest on the monetary award from finality until full payment.

### Sustaining Arbitral Attorney's Fees Against a Government Instrumentality Under Paragraph (5)

In [*Tourism Infrastructure and Enterprise Zone Authority v. Global-V Builders Co.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64609),[^105] contractor Global-V Builders Co. sought arbitration before the CIAC against Tourism Infrastructure and Enterprise Zone Authority (TIEZA) for unpaid infrastructure billings across five Boracay and Banaue projects, despite TIEZA invoking a Commission on Audit disallowance of P12,161,423.11 on one sidewalk project and contesting CIAC jurisdiction. The CIAC Tribunal awarded Global-V P10,178,440.17 (incorporating P350,000.00 in proven counsel's fees supported by a letter agreement) plus P322,897.58 arbitration costs with 6 percent interest from finality, finding TIEZA acted in gross and evident bad faith under Article 2208(5). The Court of Appeals affirmed.

On Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court upheld the CIAC's jurisdiction and sustained both the P350,000.00 attorney's fees and the P322,897.58 arbitration costs under Article 2208(5) because TIEZA acted in gross and evident bad faith in refusing to pay Global-V's plainly valid, just and demandable claims. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals' Amended Decision and Resolution upholding the CIAC Final Award and clarified that 6 percent annual legal interest on the P10,178,440.17 total monetary award runs from finality of the Supreme Court decision until full payment.

### Election Contests and the Clearly Unfounded Standard Under Paragraph (4)

In [*Malaluan v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666),[^106] mayoralty protestant Luis Malaluan initially won his election protest before the trial court and assumed office on a P500,000.00 bond through execution pending appeal. On appeal, the Commission on Elections (COMELEC) reversed, proclaimed protestee Joseph Evangelista winner and ordered Malaluan to pay P257,110.00 in actual damages (derived from P557,110.00 comprising attorney's fees, P15,154.00 xerox expenses and P169,456.00 unearned salary for 14 months at P12,104.00 per month, less a P300,000.00 contingent success fee) under Article 2208(4), deeming the protest clearly unfounded.

Sitting En Banc on certiorari, the Supreme Court held that although the mayoralty term had expired, the damages award remained justiciable. Absent breach of contract, quasi-contract, tort, crime or specific statutory authority, damages cannot be awarded in election contests except attorney's fees under Article 2208. The Supreme Court found no breach of contract or quasi-contract, tortious act or crime attributable to Malaluan. Evangelista also pointed to no specific provision of law authorizing a money claim for protest expenses against the losing party. The Supreme Court declared COMELEC's reasoning fatally flawed because a differing appellate appreciation of contested ballots or perceived trial-court error does not prove malicious intention or make an election protest clearly unfounded. It also held that the trial court acted judiciously in granting execution pending appeal. Malaluan was not a usurper but a de facto officer who served under color of election, so the award of salaries and emoluments lacked legal sanction. Any damage Evangelista suffered from the execution pending appeal was damnum absque injuria. The Supreme Court granted the petition, upheld COMELEC's proclamation as moot and academic and declared null and void the portion awarding actual damages and attorney's fees to Evangelista.

### Public Officers Entitled to Government Counsel and Negation of Paragraph (4)

In [*Angara v. Gorospe*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1957/apr1957/gr_l-9230_1957.html),[^107] Dr. Andres A. Angara filed quo warranto proceedings in the Court of First Instance of Baguio against Acting City Health Officer Dra. Josefina A. Gorospe and Baguio city officials and initially obtained a preliminary mandatory injunction on a P3,000 bond ordering payment of his monthly salary. After the Supreme Court annulled that injunction in an earlier certiorari case (G.R. No. L-8408) over a vigorous dissent by Justice Marceliano Montemayor, the trial court dismissed the quo warranto petition, dismissed Gorospe's P5,000 counterclaim for attorney's fees and ordered payment of Angara's accrued salaries.

Sitting En Banc, the Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of Gorospe's P5,000 attorney's fees claim on two majority grounds. First, as an officer of the City of Baguio, Gorospe was entitled to the legal services of the City Attorney. Because the record failed to show any reason why that government lawyer could not defend her, she could not hire private counsel and shift those fees onto Angara. Second, because Angara initially prevailed in obtaining a preliminary mandatory injunction in the trial court and drew a vigorous Supreme Court dissent in G.R. No. L-8408, his quo warranto action was not a clearly unfounded civil action under Article 2208(4). The Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of Gorospe's P5,000 attorney's fees counterclaim, reversed the order granting Angara accrued salaries and made no pronouncement as to costs.

### Non-Retroactivity of Article 2208(11) to Actions Filed Before the New Civil Code

In [*Receiver for North Negros Sugar Co., Inc. v. Ybañez*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/aug1968/gr_l-22183_1968.html),[^108] siblings Pedro and Rosario Ybañez filed a quasi-delict suit on May 15, 1940 in the Court of First Instance of Negros Occidental for the 1937 death of their brother Cesar V. Ybañez. The trial court dismissed the complaint on July 23, 1958. The Court of Appeals reversed on October 14, 1963 and ordered North Negros Sugar Co., Inc. to pay P9,600.00 actual damages, P5,000.00 moral damages and P5,000.00 attorney's fees on the ground of protracted litigation.

Sitting En Banc on review, the Supreme Court deleted both the P5,000.00 moral damages under Article 2206 and Article 2257 and the P5,000.00 attorney's fees. Quoting *Bureau of Lands v. Samia* (as cited in [*Receiver for North Negros Sugar Co.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/aug1968/gr_l-22183_1968.html)[^109]), the Supreme Court held that Article 2208(11) of the new Civil Code cannot be applied retroactively to an action instituted on May 15, 1940 before the Code took effect. It added that petitioner's resistance to the claim following criminal acquittal and trial-court dismissal showed no bad faith. The Supreme Court modified the Court of Appeals decision by eliminating the P5,000.00 moral damages and the P5,000.00 attorney's fees, with no pronouncement as to costs.

## Related Provisions

- [Article 2199](art-2199.md) (Actual or compensatory damages): Attorney's fees under Article 2208 are allowed in the concept of actual damages and therefore require factual and legal basis rather than speculation, as applied in [*University of the Philippines v. Dizon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55112),[^110] [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650)[^111] and [*Malaluan v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666).[^112]
- [Article 2206](art-2206.md) (Civil indemnity and moral damages for death): Interacts with Article 2208(1) and the reasonableness clause when death indemnity increases on appeal in [*University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70367),[^113] and with transitional rules on actions filed before the new Civil Code in [*Receiver for North Negros Sugar Co., Inc. v. Ybañez*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/aug1968/gr_l-22183_1968.html).[^114]
- [Article 2209](art-2209.md) and [Article 2213](art-2213.md) (Damages and interest in monetary obligations): Applied alongside stipulated attorney's fees under Article 2208 on commercial promissory notes in [*Pacific Mills, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/feb1992/gr_87182_1992.html).[^115]
- [Article 2217](art-2217.md) and [Article 2219](art-2219.md) (Moral damages): Distinguished from extraordinary attorney's fees as actual damages in [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832),[^116] [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650)[^117] and [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163).[^118]
- [Article 2221](art-2221.md) (Nominal damages): Awarded for procedural due process violations in a seafarer suit even where attorney's fees were deleted under Article 2208(2) and (8) in [*Arriesgado v. Multinational Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70389).[^119]
- [Article 2227](art-2227.md) (Reduction of liquidated damages): Parallels Article 2208's reasonableness mandate in tempering unconscionable stipulated percentages on a large principal debt in [*Virata v. Ng Wee*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63192).[^120]
- [Article 2229](art-2229.md), [Article 2231](art-2231.md) and [Article 2232](art-2232.md) (Exemplary or corrective damages): Supply the predicate for Article 2208(1) when properly sustained, as discussed in [*Heirs of Cipriano v. National Transmission Corp.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/aug2025/gr_255113_2025.html),[^121] [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163),[^122] [*Petron Corporation v. National College of Business and Arts*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41209)[^123] and [*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650).[^124]
- **Labor Code, Article 111 and Republic Act No. 9406 (amending Executive Order No. 292, Section 16-D)**: Govern 10 percent extraordinary attorney's fees in unlawful withholding of wages and Public Attorney's Office trust-fund deposits, harmonized with Article 2208 in [*Traders Royal Bank Employees Union-Independent v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34610),[^125] [*Kaisahan at Kapatiran ng mga Manggagawa at Kawani sa MWC-East Zone Union v. Manila Water Co., Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32584),[^126] [*Alva v. High Capacity Security Force, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63711)[^127] and [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730).[^128]
- **Rules of Court, Rule 142, Sections 1, 6, 8, 9, 10 and 11**: Separate fixed statutory judicial costs taxed by the clerk of court from Article 2208 damages in [*Go v. Saint Jude Catholic School Manila, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69952),[^129] [*Damasen v. Hernando*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/apr1981/gr_49995_1981.html)[^130] and [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163).[^131]
- **Insurance Code, Section 244**: Independently authorizes 10 percent attorney's fees and double interest for unreasonable delay in paying insurance claims without bad-faith proof in [*Prudential Guarantee and Assurance Inc. v. Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41756).[^132]

## Key Cases

- [*Angara v. Gorospe*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1957/apr1957/gr_l-9230_1957.html)[^133]: En Banc denied a Baguio city official's P5,000 attorney's fees counterclaim because she failed to show why the City Attorney could not defend her and because an initial lower-court injunctive win and a Supreme Court dissent negated a clearly unfounded civil action under Article 2208(4).
- [*Buenaventura v. Sto. Domingo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1958/mar1958/gr_l-10651_1958.html)[^134]: En Banc affirmed dismissal of a malicious prosecution damages suit, holding that recovery under Article 2208(3) requires proof that the accuser acted deliberately and with knowledge that the criminal charge was false and groundless.
- [*Rizal Surety and Insurance Co. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/may1967/gr_l-23729_1967.html)[^135]: En Banc reversed a P300.00 fee award to an alternative defendant, holding that Article 2208(2) covers litigation expenses incurred in suing third persons and non-litigation protective expenses, while a defendant claiming an unnecessary suit must satisfy Article 2208(4)'s gross bad-faith standard.
- [*Receiver for North Negros Sugar Co., Inc. v. Ybañez*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/aug1968/gr_l-22183_1968.html)[^136]: En Banc eliminated P5,000.00 attorney's fees, holding that Article 2208(11) of the Civil Code does not apply retroactively to a civil action filed on May 15, 1940 before the Code took effect.
- [*Damasen v. Hernando*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/apr1981/gr_49995_1981.html)[^137]: Annulled a post-judgment order inserting P2,500.00 attorney's fees into a Bill of Costs, holding that even where exemplary damages were awarded under Article 2208(1), unadjudicated counsel's fees cannot be taxed as Rule 142 judicial costs.
- [*Scott Consultants & Resource Development Corp., Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_112916_1995.html)[^138]: Deleted P50,000.00 attorney's fees, establishing that attorney's fees under Article 2208 are the exception rather than the rule and require explicit factual, legal and equitable justification in the body of the decision rather than solely in the dispositive portion.
- [*Malaluan v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666)[^139]: En Banc nullified a P257,110.00 COMELEC damages and attorney's fees award, holding that election protests allow no damages except attorney's fees under Article 2208 and that differing ballot appreciation does not make a protest clearly unfounded under paragraph (4).
- [*Traders Royal Bank Employees Union-Independent v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34610)[^140]: Defined ordinary attorney's fees (client-lawyer compensation based on employment) versus extraordinary attorney's fees under Article 2208 (court-ordered damages payable to the client) and fixed P10,000.00 ordinary fees on quantum meruit.
- [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832)[^141]: Deleted P500,000.00 attorney's fees awarded to RBS while leaving unappealed VIVA fees intact, holding that even when compelled to litigate under Article 2208(2), attorney's fees require sufficient showing of bad faith beyond an erroneous conviction of the righteousness of a party's cause.
- [*Trade & Investment Development Corporation of the Philippines v. Roblett Industrial Construction Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41596)[^142]: Reduced stipulated 10 percent attorney's fees to 10 percent of the P11,775,611.25 principal alone to prevent unconscionability while holding a non-privy surety solidarily liable for P100,000.00 reasonable attorney's fees under Article 2208 where just and equitable.
- [*Petron Corporation v. National College of Business and Arts*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41209)[^143]: Revoked P150,000.00 attorney's fees and P100,000.00 exemplary damages against an intervenor relying on final execution deeds, holding that even an untenable defense does not satisfy Article 2208(5) unless it amounts to gross and evident bad faith.
- [*Kaisahan at Kapatiran ng mga Manggagawa at Kawani sa MWC-East Zone Union v. Manila Water Co., Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32584)[^144]: Reinstated 10 percent extraordinary attorney's fees under Article 2208(7) and Labor Code Article 111 upon a plain showing that lawful wages went unpaid without needing employer bad faith, separating extraordinary fees from union-counsel fee deductions.
- [*University of the Philippines v. Dizon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55112)[^145]: Deleted as void P150,000.00 plus P1,500.00 per appearance attorney's fees alongside actual and moral damages, holding that fallo-only awards without body-text findings violate Article 2208 and the Constitution and constitute a patent nullity.
- [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163)[^146]: Deleted P50,000.00 attorney's fees even while sustaining P50,000.00 exemplary damages, holding that when the trial court's decision body has nil discussion of attorney's fees, the Court of Appeals should disallow the award rather than supply Article 2208(1) on appeal.
- [*Spouses Mercader v. Spouses Bardilas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62135)[^147]: Deleted P20,000.00 attorney's fees granted merely pursuant to Article 2208(11), holding that what is just and equitable is a matter of demonstration rather than feeling and requires express factual and legal findings.
- [*Spouses Timado v. Rural Bank of San Jose, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62143)[^148]: Fixed attorney's fees at P100,000.00 under Article 2208(4) despite deleting exemplary damages, because the trial court set out five concrete bad-faith findings in the body of its decision showing vexatious and unfounded suits.
- [*Virata v. Ng Wee*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63192)[^149]: Tempered stipulated attorney's fees from 25 percent to 5 percent of the total amount due on a P213,290,410.36 investment fraud judgment pursuant to Article 2208's mandate that attorney's fees must be reasonable in all cases.
- [*Alva v. High Capacity Security Force, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63711)[^150]: Integrated 10 percent extraordinary attorney's fees under Labor Code Article 111 and Article 2208 for an illegally dismissed employee assisted by the Public Attorney's Office, holding that Republic Act No. 9406 directs such awards into a National Treasury trust fund and distinguishing *Lambo v. NLRC* as a case decided before that law.
- [*KLM Royal Dutch Airlines v. Tiongco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67711)[^151]: Sustained 20 percent attorney's fees despite the trial court's silent text because the Court of Appeals conducted an independent assessment explaining entitlement under Article 2208(1) on validly affirmed exemplary damages.
- [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^152]: Deleted 10 percent seafarer attorney's fees while affirming USD 60,000.00 disability benefits, harmonizing doctrine by confining Labor Code Article 111 to unlawful withholding of wages (repudiating *Heirs of Aniban*), Civil Code Article 2208(2) to third-person litigation and Civil Code Article 2208(8) to statutory workmen's compensation laws rather than contractual POEA-SEC claims.
- [*Go v. Saint Jude Catholic School Manila, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69952)[^153]: En Banc denied PHP 348,014.00 in additional costs including PHP 264,000.00 counsel appearance fees after a final PHP 100,000.00 Article 2208 award, holding that attorney's fees cannot be recovered simultaneously as damages and as Rule 142 judicial costs beyond the PHP 20.00 flat rate taxed initially by the clerk of court.
- [*University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70367)[^154]: En Banc upheld entitlement to attorney's fees because exemplary damages of PHP 1,000,000.00 were imposed, but converted the trial court's 20 percent fee rate into a fixed PHP 150,000.00 under Article 2208's reasonableness mandate after sharply increasing the principal damages.
- [*Arriesgado v. Multinational Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70389)[^155]: Applied [*Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^156] to delete 10 percent NLRC attorney's fees in a seafarer POEA-SEC disability claim under Labor Code Article 111 and Civil Code Article 2208(2) and (8), while awarding USD 60,000.00 disability benefits, differential sickness allowance and PHP 50,000.00 nominal damages.
- [*ABC-RMO Security & Investigation Agency, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70571)[^157]: Applied [*Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^158] in a land-based constructive dismissal suit to delete 10 percent attorney's fees under Article 2208(2) because the action did not compel the employee to litigate with third persons, while affirming backwages, separation pay and PHP 25,000.00 each in moral and exemplary damages.

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1:** A seafarer suffered an onboard injury during a vessel basketball game, was medically repatriated and sued his manning agency and foreign principal for total and permanent disability benefits under the 2010 POEA-SEC after the company-designated physician terminated treatment without issuing a final assessment. The National Labor Relations Commission awarded disability benefits plus 10 percent attorney's fees on the ground that the seafarer was compelled to litigate and that the suit was an action for disability indemnity. The manning agency had shouldered medical treatment and prorated sickness allowance under a genuine albeit mistaken belief that the injury was not work-related. Should the 10 percent award of attorney's fees be sustained under Labor Code Article 111 or Civil Code Article 2208(2) and (8)?

**Answer:** No, the award of attorney's fees must be deleted for lack of legal basis. Under the harmonizing doctrine established in [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730)[^159] and enforced in [*Arriesgado v. Multinational Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70389),[^160] none of the three provisions supports attorney's fees on these facts. First, Labor Code Article 111 applies strictly to cases involving unlawful withholding and recovery of wages, not standalone claims for disability compensation. Second, Civil Code Article 2208(2) embodies the wrongful act doctrine and requires that the defendant's act or omission compelled the plaintiff to litigate or incur expenses in relation to third persons rather than in a direct suit against the employer, lest attorney's fees become the general rule and place a premium on the right to litigate. Third, Civil Code Article 2208(8) covers only statutory actions for indemnity under workmen's compensation and employer's liability laws (now Title II, Book IV of the Labor Code), whereas a seafarer's POEA-SEC disability claim rests on contract. Absent employer bad faith or another specific factual, legal and equitable basis under Article 2208(11), attorney's fees cannot be awarded.

**Question 2:** In a joint venture dispute over condominium units, the Regional Trial Court ordered the developer to pay the landowners a lump-sum monetary share and added PHP 20,000,000.00 for attorney's fees and litigation expenses solely in the dispositive portion of its decision without discussing attorney's fees in the body of the decision. The landowners did not prove bad faith on the developer's part in resisting their claim. On appeal, the landowners argue that the Court of Appeals may affirm the PHP 20,000,000.00 fee award because they prevailed and were forced to hire counsel to protect their rights. Decide.

**Answer:** The trial court's award of attorney's fees must be deleted. Under Article 2208 of the Civil Code, attorney's fees generally cannot be recovered because no premium should be placed on the right to litigate. They are not granted merely because a party prevails. As held in [*Scott Consultants & Resource Development Corp., Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_112916_1995.html),[^161] [*University of the Philippines v. Dizon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55112)[^162] and [*Burgundy Realty Corp. v. Bella*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70154),[^163] an award of attorney's fees as actual damages demands factual, legal and equitable justification explicitly stated in the body of the decision rather than appearing for the first time in the dispositive portion. Moreover, under [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832)[^164] and [*Burgundy Realty Corp. v. Bella*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70154),[^165] even when a claimant is compelled to litigate or incur expenses, attorney's fees may not be awarded absent a sufficient showing of bad faith beyond an erroneous conviction of the righteousness of a party's cause. Because the trial court gave no factual or legal basis in its decision body and bad faith was not proved, the PHP 20,000,000.00 fee award cannot stand.

**Question 3:** A plaintiff won a final Regional Trial Court damages judgment awarding PHP 21,128.00 actual damages, PHP 100,000.00 exemplary damages, PHP 100,000.00 attorney's fees and cost of suit. During execution, the plaintiff filed a bill of costs claiming an additional PHP 348,014.00 as costs of suit, including PHP 264,000.00 representing counsel's appearance fees for attending 41 court hearings. The plaintiff contends that Article 2208 attorney's fees are damages separate from costs of suit, so actual hearing appearance fees remain recoverable as costs under Rule 142. Rule on the plaintiff's claim and state who must initially tax the costs of suit.

**Answer:** The plaintiff's claim for PHP 264,000.00 in counsel appearance fees as judicial costs must be denied. The clerk of court must initially tax the allowable costs under Rule 142. As the Supreme Court En Banc held in [*Go v. Saint Jude Catholic School Manila, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69952),[^166] while Article 2208 of the Civil Code allows recovery of reasonable attorney's fees as actual or compensatory damages, nothing in the law permits a prevailing litigant to recover attorney's fees simultaneously as damages under Article 2208 and as judicial costs under Rule 142 beyond the fixed sums exclusively listed in Rule 142, Sections 9 to 11. In the Regional Trial Court, Rule 142, Section 10 allows only a flat rate of PHP 20.00 for all counsel appearances down to final judgment and PHP 15.00 for the complaint or answer, which Rule 142, Section 6 states has no relation to fees charged by counsel against a client. Finally, under Rule 142, Section 8 as enforced in [*Go v. Saint Jude Catholic School Manila, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69952)[^167] and [*Damasen v. Hernando*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/apr1981/gr_49995_1981.html),[^168] taxation of costs in a Regional Trial Court is a ministerial duty performed in the first instance by the clerk of court on five days' written notice rather than directly by the judge.

# Quotes

> "In its extraordinary concept, an attorney’s fee is an indemnity for damages ordered by the court to be paid by the losing party in a litigation. The basis of this is any of the cases provided by law where such award can be made, such as those authorized in Article 2208, Civil Code, and is payable not to the lawyer but to the client, unless they have agreed that the award shall pertain to the lawyer as additional compensation or as part thereof."[^169]

> "While Article 2208 of the Civil Code provides for the recovery of attorney's fees as damages, nothing in the law allows their simultaneous recovery as costs, other than those listed in Rule 142, Sections 9 to 11. Indeed, it would be absurd to award a reasonable amount of attorney's fees as damages, only to later award the actual appearance fees of the prevailing party's counsel as costs. It is for this reason that Section 6 specifically states that it "shall have no relation to the fees to be charged by an attorney as against his[/her] client." It would also be illogical to consider the number of counsel appearances in determining costs where the Rules only provide a flat rate of PHP 20.00 to cover all appearances "down to and including final judgment" in the RTC. While PHP 20.00 today is admittedly not the sizeable amount that it was when the old Rules were in effect, unless amended, that amount remains the only amount of lawyer's fees that can properly be awarded as costs."[^170]

> "The same downward modification is in order as regards the award of attorney's fees. Although Ng Wee finds justification for the entitlement to the award under Article 2208 of the New Civil Code, the same provision mandates that "in all cases, the attorney's fees and expenses of litigation must be reasonable." Just as We have reduced the rate for liquidated damages, the Court likewise tempers the stipulated rate of attorney's fees to five percent (5%) of the total amount due on Ng Wee's investment."[^171]

> "It is settled that the award of attorney's fees is the exception rather than the rule and counsel's fees are not to be awarded every time a party wins a suit. The power of the court to award attorney's fees under Article 2208 of the Civil Code demands factual, legal, and equitable justification; its basis cannot be left to speculation or conjecture. Where granted, the court must explicitly state in the body of the decision, and not only in the dispositive portion thereof, the legal reason for the award of attorney's fees."[^172]

> "Like the actual and moral damages, the P150,000.00, plus P1,500.00 per appearance, granted as attorney’s fees were factually unwarranted and devoid of legal basis. The general rule is that a successful litigant cannot recover attorney’s fees as part of the damages to be assessed against the losing party because of the policy that no premium should be placed on the right to litigate. Prior to the effectivity of the present Civil Code, indeed, such fees could be recovered only when there was a stipulation to that effect. It was only under the present Civil Code that the right to collect attorney’s fees in the cases mentioned in Article 2208 of the Civil Code came to be recognized. Nonetheless, with attorney’s fees being allowed in the concept of actual damages, their amounts must be factually and legally justified in the body of the decision and not stated for the first time in the decretal portion. Stating the amounts only in the dispositive portion of the judgment is not enough; a rendition of the factual and legal justifications for them must also be laid out in the body of the decision."[^173]

> "First of all, the litigation expenses recoverable therein are those incurred in suing third persons, upon the premise that such expenses were unnecessary. The expenses mentioned in the second situation in said par. 2 are therefore non-litigation; otherwise, there would have been no justification for the distinction."[^174]

> "Even when a claimant is compelled to litigate with third persons or to incur expenses to protect his rights, still attorney’s fees may not be awarded where no sufficient showing of bad faith could be reflected in a party’s persistence in a case other than an erroneous conviction of the righteousness of his cause."[^175]

> "It is true that the present action is not criminal but civil in nature, but the provisions of the Civil Code in taking reference to malicious prosecutions must necessarily imply that the person to be held liable to pay moral damages should have acted deliberately and with knowledge that his accusation of the person subject to such malicious prosecution, was false and groundless. the same thing is true as regards the demand for attorney's fees and expenses of litigation authorized under Article 2208, No. 3 of the Civil Code. In this connection it may be stated that the act of a complainant in submitting his case to the authorities for prosecution does not make him liable for malicious prosecution, for it is the Government or representative of the State that takes charge of the prosecution of the offense."[^176]

> "Article 2208(5) contemplates a situation where one refuses unjustifiably and in evident bad faith to satisfy another’s plainly valid, just and demandable claim, compelling the latter needlessly to seek redress from the courts. In such a case, the law allows recovery of money the plaintiff had to spend for a lawyer’s assistance in suing the defendant – expenses the plaintiff would not have incurred if not for the defendant’s refusal to comply with the most basic rules of fair dealing. It does not mean, however, that the losing party should be made to pay attorney’s fees merely because the court finds his legal position to be erroneous and upholds that of the other party, for that would be an intolerable transgression of the policy that no one should be penalized for exercising the right to have contending claims settled by a court of law. In fact, even a clearly untenable defense does not justify an award of attorney’s fees unless it amounts to gross and evident bad faith."[^177]

> "Considering that the decision of the CA does not express any justification other than stating that attorney's fees were being awarded to the respondents "pursuant to paragraph 11 of Article 2208 of the New Civil Code" the award by the CA must be set aside; otherwise, attorney's fees would be turned into a premium on the right to litigate, which is prohibited. Moreover, attorney's fees, being in the nature of actual damages, should be based on the facts on record and the Court must delineate the legal reason for such award."[^178]

> "Over the years, the Court has vacillated on the award of attorney's fees on the basis of Article 111 of the Labor Code and Article 2208 of the Civil Code in worker disability indemnification cases. We clarify that attorney's fees under Article 111 of the Labor Code are recoverable only where there is unlawful withholding of wages, not in cases involving only indemnification claims for disability or death. Anent attorney's fees under Article 2208(2) of the Civil Code, it is not sufficient that the plaintiff be compelled to litigate or incur expense to protect their interest. The litigation or incurrence of expense must be in relation to third persons. A contrary rule would make entitlement to attorney's fees the general rule instead of the exception and would negate the policy against placing a premium on the right to litigate. Finally, attorney's fees under Article 2208(8) of the Civil Code are recoverable only in actions for indemnity under workmen's compensation and employer's liability laws, not under contract."[^179]

> "In PCL Shipping, we found the award of attorney’s fees due and appropriate since the respondent therein incurred legal expenses after he was forced to file an action for recovery of his lawful wages and other benefits to protect his rights. From this perspective and the above precedents, we conclude that the CA erred in ruling that a finding of the employer’s malice or bad faith in withholding wages must precede an award of attorney’s fees under Article 111 of the Labor Code. To reiterate, a plain showing that the lawful wages were not paid without justification is sufficient."[^180]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Step-by-Step Analysis Before Claiming or Awarding Attorney's Fees**
> 1. **Identify the concept first:** Determine whether the fee is ordinary compensation between client and counsel (governed by their contract or quantum meruit, as in [*Traders Royal Bank Employees Union-Independent v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34610)) or extraordinary indemnity for damages paid by the losing party to the prevailing party under Article 2208.
> 2. **Check for a special statute:** Confirm whether an independent statutory fee-shifting provision applies without Article 2208's bad-faith limits, such as Section 244 of the Insurance Code for unreasonable delay in paying insurance proceeds ([*Prudential Guarantee and Assurance Inc. v. Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41756)) or Article 111 of the Labor Code for unlawful withholding of wages ([*Kaisahan at Kapatiran ng mga Manggagawa at Kawani sa MWC-East Zone Union v. Manila Water Co., Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32584)).
> 3. **Check for a written stipulation:** If a contract stipulates attorney's fees, treat it as a penal clause enforceable against signatories under Article 2208's opening clause ([*Trade & Investment Development Corporation of the Philippines v. Roblett Industrial Construction Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41596)), verify that no double recovery occurs ([*Spouses Consing v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45955)) and test the amount for reasonableness under the final paragraph ([*Virata v. Ng Wee*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63192) and [*Philippine Fisheries Development Authority v. Pascual*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70016)).
> 4. **Pinpoint a specific paragraph from (1) to (11):** Absent stipulation, plead attorney's fees specifically rather than relying on a general equitable prayer ([*Trans-Asia Shipping Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33650)) and prove every element of the invoked exception, including bad faith where required under paragraphs (2), (3), (4) and (5).
> 5. **Verify body-of-decision reasoning:** Confirm that the decision body explicitly states the factual, legal and equitable justification rather than placing the award solely in the dispositive portion ([*Scott Consultants & Resource Development Corp., Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_112916_1995.html) and [*University of the Philippines v. Dizon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55112)).
> 6. **Audit reasonableness on appeal:** Whenever principal damages increase sharply or a debtor settles pending appeal, evaluate whether a percentage fee has become unreasonable and should be converted to a fixed sum ([*University of Southeastern Philippines v. Sarate*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70367) and [*Flight Attendants and Stewards Association of the Philippines (FASAP) v. Philippine Airlines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50154)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 1: Treating Compelled Direct Litigation as Automatic Proof Under Paragraph (2)**
> Litigants routinely argue that hiring counsel to file or defend a direct lawsuit triggers Article 2208(2). Under [*Rizal Surety and Insurance Co. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/may1967/gr_l-23729_1967.html), [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730), [*Arriesgado v. Multinational Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70389) and [*ABC-RMO Security & Investigation Agency, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70571), paragraph (2) presupposes litigation or expense in relation to third persons. Even where third-person litigation or protective expenses exist, [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832) and [*Roxaco-Asia Hospitality Corp. v. Gulf Canary Construction and Development, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70194) still bar attorney's fees without proof of bad faith beyond an erroneous belief in one's cause.

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 2: Confusing Seafarer POEA-SEC Disability Claims With Wage Recovery or Workmen's Compensation Laws**
> Do not automatically award 10 percent attorney's fees in a seafarer's contractual disability suit. Under [*Pacific Ocean Manning, Inc. v. Bobiles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69730) and [*Arriesgado v. Multinational Maritime, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70389), Labor Code Article 111 applies only to unlawful withholding of wages (not standalone disability or death claims) and Civil Code Article 2208(8) covers statutory workmen's compensation and employer's liability laws rather than contractual POEA-SEC claims. Contrast this with wage-recovery actions under Labor Code Article 111 in [*Kaisahan at Kapatiran ng mga Manggagawa at Kawani sa MWC-East Zone Union v. Manila Water Co., Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/32584) and [*Alva v. High Capacity Security Force, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63711), where no bad faith is needed once lawful wages are unpaid.

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 3: Attempting to Recover Counsel's Hearing Fees as Rule 142 Judicial Costs**
> Prevailing parties cannot supplement a judgment or bypass an omitted fee award by submitting counsel's hearing attendance fees in a post-judgment Bill of Costs. Under [*Go v. Saint Jude Catholic School Manila, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69952) and [*Damasen v. Hernando*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/apr1981/gr_49995_1981.html), Rule 142 judicial costs are strictly capped at the statutory schedule (PHP 20.00 flat rate for all counsel appearances in the Regional Trial Court) and must be taxed in the first instance by the clerk of court.

> [!TIP]
> **Arguments That Succeeded**
> - Proving five concrete acts of bad faith stated expressly in the trial court's decision body sustained P100,000.00 attorney's fees under Article 2208(4) even after exemplary damages were deleted ([*Spouses Timado v. Rural Bank of San Jose, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62143)).
> - Showing that a defendant's breach of a written undertaking caused the plaintiff to be impleaded in a creditor bank's third-party collection suit satisfied Article 2208(2) ([*Escaño v. Ortigas, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43947)).
> - Demonstrating gross and evident bad faith through broken credit extensions ([*Chartis Philippines Insurance, Inc. v. Cyber City Teleservices, Ltd.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67552)) or unjustified refusal of valid infrastructure billings ([*Tourism Infrastructure and Enterprise Zone Authority v. Global-V Builders Co.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64609)) sustained attorney's fees under Article 2208(5).
> - Invoking Republic Act No. 9406 secured 10 percent extraordinary attorney's fees in an illegal dismissal wage-recovery suit despite free Public Attorney's Office representation ([*Alva v. High Capacity Security Force, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63711)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Arguments That Failed**
> - Relying on a silent dispositive award or a single boilerplate sentence that the claimant was forced to hire counsel led to deletion in [*Scott Consultants & Resource Development Corp., Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_112916_1995.html), [*University of the Philippines v. Dizon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55112), [*Philippine National Construction Corp. v. APAC Marketing Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56034), [*Mendoza v. Spouses Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57163), [*Spouses Mercader v. Spouses Bardilas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62135) and [*Burgundy Realty Corp. v. Bella*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70154).
> - Equating an unsuccessful defense or a criminal acquittal with bad faith failed under Article 2208(3) in [*Buenaventura v. Sto. Domingo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1958/mar1958/gr_l-10651_1958.html), under Article 2208(4) in [*Malaluan v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33666) and [*Angara v. Gorospe*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1957/apr1957/gr_l-9230_1957.html) and under Article 2208(5) in [*Petron Corporation v. National College of Business and Arts*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41209).
> - Counsel suing an employer directly after clients executed valid quitclaims failed for lack of real-party-in-interest standing because extraordinary attorney's fees belong to the clients ([*Ortiz v. San Miguel Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46105)).
