> ART. 21. Any person who willfully causes loss or injury to another in a manner that is contrary to morals, good customs or public policy shall compensate the latter for the damage.[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 21 of the Civil Code governs liability for acts *contra bonos mores* by requiring any person who willfully inflicts loss or injury on another in a manner contrary to morals, good customs or public policy to compensate the victim for the damage. Crafted by the Code Commission to fill the countless gaps in positive legislation, Article 21 expands the concept of civil wrongs beyond criminal offenses and negligent quasi-delicts so that intentional moral wrongs do not go unredressed.

- **Remedy for moral wrongs outside positive statutes.** Article 21 was adopted to vouchsafe an adequate legal remedy for the untold number of moral wrongs that human foresight cannot specifically enumerate in statute books.[^2] [^3]
- **Three essential elements.** Liability under Article 21 requires the concurrence of three requisites: (1) an act that is legal or not proscribed by positive law, (2) committed in a manner contrary to morals, good customs, public order or public policy and (3) done willfully with intent to injure.[^4] [^5]
- **Direct anchor for moral damages.** Under Article 2219(10) of the Civil Code, acts and actions referred to in Article 21 expressly authorize the recovery of moral damages.[^6] [^7]

This commentary examines the legislative purpose and elements of Article 21, its boundary with Articles 19, 20 and 2176, its application to breach of promise to marry, moral seduction, bigamy and sexual coercion, its role in commercial bad faith, contractual pre-termination, passenger humiliation and employment harassment, the defenses of good faith, psychological incapacity and *damnum absque injuria* and the procedural rules on independent civil actions, labor jurisdiction and conflict of laws.

## Purpose, Requisites and Scope of Acts *Contra Bonos Mores*

In [*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html) and [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html), the Supreme Court explained that the Code Commission incorporated Article 21 to remedy the countless gaps in the statutes that would otherwise leave victims of grievous moral wrongs helpless despite suffering material and moral injury.[^8] [^9] In the general scheme of Philippine law described in [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html), intentional and malicious felonies are governed by the Revised Penal Code while negligent acts or omissions without pre-existing contractual relations are covered by Article 2176 of the Civil Code.[^10] Article 21 fills the vacuum between those two poles by providing civil redress for willful, injurious acts that are not punishable as crimes or proscribed by specific statutes but offend morals, good customs or public policy.[^11]

As synthesized in [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html), [*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407) and [*Couples for Christ School of the Morning Star v. Malonda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70275), an action for damages under Article 21 requires three elements: (1) there is an act that is legal, (2) the act is contrary to morals, good customs, public order or public policy and (3) the act is done with intent to injure.[^12] [^13] [^14] Moreover, as the Supreme Court held in [*Guevarra v. Banach*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68051), the human relations provisions of the Civil Code presuppose that the party seeking to recover damages acted in good faith.[^15]

## Boundary With Articles 19, 20 and 2176

Articles 19, 20 and 21 operate together to enforce the standards of human relations, while Article 2176 governs quasi-delicts.[^16] [^17] Article 19 sets the cardinal rule of conduct requiring every person to act with justice, give everyone his due and observe honesty and good faith, whereas Articles 20 and 21 provide the actionable remedies when that rule is violated.[^18] [^19] In [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36425), the Supreme Court observed that while Article 19 declares a principle of law, Article 21 gives flesh to its provisions by rendering violations actionable in the courts.[^20]

In [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134), [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826) and [*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692), the Supreme Court delineated the boundaries among Articles 20, 21 and 2176:

- **Article 20 versus Article 21.** Article 20 concerns violations of existing law and allows recovery whether the unlawful act was committed willfully or negligently.[^21] [^22] Article 21 concerns injuries caused by acts that are not necessarily proscribed by positive law and requires that the act be willful, meaning that there was an intention to do the act and a desire to achieve the injurious outcome in violation of morals, good customs or public policy.[^23] [^24]
- **Article 21 versus Article 2176.** In [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html) and [*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692), the Supreme Court held that Article 2176 is limited to fault or negligence where the intention to commit the injurious outcome is irrelevant and the act is not a breach of an existing statute or pre-existing contract.[^25] [^26] Consequently, where a claimant sues a medical clinic solely for alleged negligence in issuing a pre-deployment fit-to-work certificate without alleging a statutory violation, a pre-existing contract or a willful intent to injure, the claim is governed by Article 2176 rather than Articles 19, 20 and 21.[^27]

## Breach of Promise to Marry, Moral Seduction, Bigamy and Sexual Coercion

One of the classic domains of Article 21 is the redress of willful deception and coercion in intimate and family relations where the act falls outside criminal punishment or contractual enforcement.

- **Breach of promise to marry and moral seduction.** Because Congress deliberately omitted the Spanish Civil Code provisions on breach of promise to marry to prevent the abuse of "Heart Balm" suits, a mere breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong.[^28] [^29] In [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html), however, the Supreme Court held that where a man's fraudulent and deceptive promise of marriage is the proximate cause of a woman accepting his love and surrendering her virtue to him in sexual congress without any real intent on his part to marry her, the resulting moral seduction and willful injury to her honor justify moral damages under Article 21.[^30] The Supreme Court distinguished actionable moral seduction from non-actionable carnal knowledge born of mutual lust and rejected the *in pari delicto* defense under Article 1412 because the parties' fault was not equivalent.[^31] Conversely, in [*Guevarra v. Banach*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68051), where a foreign suitor lied about his identity and concealed that he was still married to his third wife while giving his fiancée P500,000.00 as a gift to help her family avoid eviction, the Supreme Court held that his lies justified her calling off the engagement and that his own bad faith barred him from claiming damages under the human relations provisions. It added that Article 22 did not apply because the P500,000.00 was a gift.[^32]
- **Fraudulent inducement into a bigamous marriage.** In [*Manuel v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42205), the Supreme Court noted that although bigamy is not expressly listed among the specific crimes in Article 2219, an offender who through a continuous series of fraudulent assurances that he is single induces an innocent woman to contract a bigamous marriage commits a willful act against public policy and good morals under Articles 19, 20 and 21, entitling the deceived spouse to moral damages under Article 2219(10).[^33]
- **Sexual coercion by a married man.** In [*Quimiguing v. Icao*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1970/jul1970/gr_26795_1970.html), the Supreme Court held that independently of an unborn conceived child's provisional personality and right to support under Article 40, a married man who forces a woman not his wife to yield to his lust through force and intimidation commits a clear violation of her rights that gives her a personal cause of action for damages under Article 21 supported by Article 2219(3) and (10).[^34]

## Commercial Bad Faith, Contractual Pre-Termination, Public Humiliation and Employment Harassment

Even when parties stand in a contractual, commercial or administrative relationship, willful and malevolent conduct that tramples on elementary norms of fairness, human dignity or public policy triggers liability under Article 21.

- **Humiliation and bad-faith ejection of a passenger by a common carrier.** In [*Air France v. Carrascoso*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1966/sep1966/gr_l-21438_1966.html), an airline's station manager in Bangkok forcibly ejected a Filipino passenger from his paid first-class seat amid a public commotion and threatened to throw him off the airplane to give the seat to a "white man" without proving any better right.[^35] The Supreme Court held that because a contract of air carriage is attended with a public duty to treat passengers with courtesy and respect, the act that breaks the contract may also be a tort, rendering the carrier liable for its manager's willful and malevolent act under Article 21 and Article 2219(10) for moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees.[^36]
- **Bad-faith breach of contract and unilateral pre-termination or padlocking.** In [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134), a paper mill that issued a partial-payment check drawn on a closed account and executed an agreement on the same day diverting its finished goods to a third party without the raw-material supplier's consent was held solidarily liable with its president for the debt, moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Articles 19, 20, 21, 1159, 2219(10) and 2220.[^37] In [*Sunbanun v. Go*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53551), a lessor who forcibly drove away her lessee's permitted lodgers and padlocked the rooms three months before the lease expired without notifying the lessee in Hong Kong was held liable for actual damages, moral damages under Articles 2219(10) and 2220 in relation to Article 21, exemplary damages under Article 2232 and attorney's fees under Article 2208.[^38] Similarly, in [*Sevilla v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/apr1988/gr_l-41182_1988.html), a travel company and its corporate secretary that revoked an agency coupled with an interest, disconnected the branch telephone lines and padlocked the branch office after hours without notice to the agent were held solidarily liable as joint tortfeasors for moral, exemplary and nominal or temperate damages under Article 21 in relation to Article 2219(10).[^39]
- **Cut-throat unfair competition and agency deception.** In [*Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc. v. Spouses Bernardo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62599), a beverage manufacturer that deceived its long-time wholesaler into submitting its customer list on a false promise of contract renewal and then trailed the wholesaler's trucks and undercut its prices to take over its customers was held liable for temperate damages for loss of goodwill under Article 2224, moral damages under Article 2219(10) in relation to Articles 21 and 28, exemplary damages and attorney's fees.[^40] In [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826), a lender bank acting as an insurance agent that exceeded its authority under Article 1897 by offering Mortgage Redemption Insurance and deducting the premium from a business loan without disclosing that coverage applied only to consumer loans was held liable under Articles 19, 20 and 21 for premium reimbursement, moral damages and attorney's fees.[^41]
- **Fraudulent self-help, utility extortion and false shoplifting accusations.** In [*Sandejas v. Spouses Ignacio, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46434), two sisters who filled in their brother's signed blank check for three million pesos and encashed it through an impostor at a grossly negligent bank were held solidarily liable with the bank for restitution, moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Articles 20, 21 and 2219(10) because taking the law into one's own hands violates public policy.[^42] In [*Lomarda v. Fudalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66363), a barangay power association chairperson and an electric cooperative clerk who withheld a required certification, demanded P1,750.00 when only P20.00 was due and publicly humiliated a farmhouse owner by shouting that his connection was an illegal tapping before cutting off his electricity were held solidarily liable as joint tortfeasors under Article 19 in relation to Article 21 and the Supreme Court barred them from invoking the clean hands doctrine because their own bad faith prevented formal compliance.[^43] In [*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html), a supermarket and its officer that detained a customer who inadvertently forgot to pay for a P3.85 file, publicly branded him a thief and confiscated his P5.00 bill as a "fine" for guards were held solidarily liable for moral damages, attorney's fees and return of the P5.00 fine under Articles 19, 21 and 26 in relation to Article 2219, although the customer's contributory negligence under Article 2214 and the supermarket's initial probable cause under Article 429 reduced the awards and eliminated exemplary damages.[^44]
- **Public humiliation of a minor child and abusive employee dismissal.** In [*Spouses Dorao v. Spouses BBB and CCC*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68985), parents who repeatedly went to a school and a parents' meeting to publicly call their son's thirteen-year-old girlfriend a flirt and a woman of loose morals, driving the child to attempt suicide and drop out of school, were held solidarily liable for moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Articles 21, 26 and 2219(10) because parental discipline cannot justify cruel or degrading public humiliation of a child.[^45] In [*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html), an employer and its general manager who branded the employee who reported company anomalies a "crook" and a "swindler", disparaged Filipinos, filed six baseless criminal complaints despite police reports clearing him and sent an unsolicited letter to a prospective employer blocking his hiring were held liable for actual, moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Article 19 in relation to Articles 21, 26, 2176 and 2219(10).[^46]

## Defenses: Good Faith, Psychological Incapacity and *Damnum Absque Injuria*

Because willfulness, malice or intent to injure lies at the core of Article 21, no liability attaches where the defendant acted without free volition or exercised a legitimate right in good faith without intent to humiliate or injure.

- **Incompatibility between psychological incapacity and willful injury under Article 21.** In [*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809), the Supreme Court held that because Article 21 requires an individual to willfully cause loss or injury in complete freedom, it is logically contradictory to declare a spouse psychologically incapacitated under Article 36 of the Family Code (meaning truly incognitive of basic marital obligations due to an innate inability beyond his control) while simultaneously treating the same acts as willful grounds for moral and exemplary damages under Article 21 and Article 2217, absent specific proof that he knew of his disability and maliciously concealed it.[^47]
- **Discreet request for an uninvited guest to leave a private party.** In [*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407), the Supreme Court absolved a hotel and its executive secretary of liability under Articles 19 and 21 where the secretary approached an uninvited guest at an intimate birthday party and asked him in a close, discreet voice to finish his food and leave, holding that in the absence of ill motive or intent to humiliate, mere bad judgment or alleged lack of consideration does not constitute an act *contra bonos mores* and any resulting embarrassment is the guest's alone to bear.[^48]
- **Academic noise from legitimate school operations.** In [*Couples for Christ School of the Morning Star v. Malonda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70275), the Supreme Court dismissed a subdivision neighborhood's damage suit under Articles 19, 20, 21, 26 and 2219 against a licensed school, ruling that noise from regular school activities is not a nuisance *per se* and that where the school had no intent to injure or vex its neighbors and undertook noise-abatement measures verified by environmental tests to fall within residential decibel limits, the plaintiffs failed to prove malice or bad faith.[^49]
- **Extrajudicial repossession under a valid lease stipulation and good-faith litigation.** In [*CJH Development Corp. v. Aniceto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66462), the Supreme Court held that a lessor who demolished a restaurant structure and stored the lessee's personal properties after lease expiration, after multiple notices to vacate and after the denial of a preliminary injunction pursuant to express lease stipulations acted in good faith and incurred no liability for actual damages under Articles 19, 20 and 21 (*damnum absque injuria*).[^50] Similarly, in [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html), filing a bouncing check complaint in good faith with probable cause and without malice under an honest mistake of identity between a father and son sharing the same name and address was held to be *damnum absque injuria* and not actionable under Articles 19, 20 or 21.[^51]

## Procedural Rules: Independent Civil Actions, Labor Jurisdiction and Conflict of Laws

Actions predicated on Article 21 frequently raise threshold questions regarding their independence from criminal or cadastral proceedings, the jurisdictional boundary between regular courts and Labor Arbiters and the choice of law in transnational torts.

- **Independence from criminal prosecutions and cadastral proceedings.** In [*Hao v. Lagahid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70070), a woman falsely claimed to be a decedent's widow and fraudulently obtained new owner's duplicate copies of 15 land titles that were actually in the possession of the decedent's brother.[^52] The Supreme Court held that the brother filed the perjury complaints first and did not reserve the civil action, so his claim for damages arising from the crime was deemed instituted with the criminal action. It held that his claim for damages based on her fraud was a separate independent civil action under Article 33 that proceeded independently of the criminal action. That claim was not a compulsory counterclaim in the summary land-title proceeding because he was not a party there and the cadastral court lost jurisdiction once the titles were shown not to be lost. The trial court had found a violation of Article 21.[^53]
- **Regular civil court jurisdiction over oppressive non-payment of resigned officer's claims.** In [*Calderon, Sr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/oct1980/gr_52235_1980.html), a resigned corporate executive vice-president who did not seek reinstatement sued the controlling stockholders and officers in the Court of First Instance for actual and moral damages and promised shares of stock, alleging that their refusal to pay his salaries, allowances and bonuses was oppressive, willful, fraudulent and in bad faith in violation of Articles 1701 and 21 of the Civil Code.[^54] The Supreme Court upheld the jurisdiction of the regular civil court over the Labor Arbiter because the underlying cause of action was intrinsically a civil dispute over a tortious and oppressive refusal to pay rather than an existing employer-employee controversy.[^55]
- **Conflict of laws and the "State of the most significant relationship" rule.** In [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36425), a resident Filipina flight attendant sued a resident foreign airline in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City for damages under Articles 19 and 21, alleging that the airline deceived her in Manila into flying to Jeddah and handed her over to a Saudi court where she was wrongfully convicted and sentenced to imprisonment and lashes.[^56] The Supreme Court held that the Regional Trial Court had subject-matter jurisdiction and that under the modern conflict-of-laws "State of the most significant relationship" rule, Philippine tort law under Articles 19 and 21 governed the dispute because the Philippines was the situs where the alleged deceit and overall injury to her person, reputation and human rights lodged.[^57]

## Case Discussions

### Non-Liability for Academic Noise From Legitimate School Operations

Residents of Saint Joseph Subdivision in Barangay Villa Kananga, Butuan City sued Couples for Christ School of the Morning Star before the Regional Trial Court of Butuan City for damages under Articles 19, 20, 21, 26 and 2219 over daytime and evening noise from school drums, bugles, megaphones and multipurpose center activities, even after the school executed an Affidavit of Undertaking and erected higher fences, planted trees and limited weekday hours so that a February 5, 2020 City Environment and Natural Resources Office test confirmed its noise fell within residential limits.[^58] The Regional Trial Court of Butuan City, Branch 3 dismissed the complaint and the school's counterclaim in Civil Case No. 7890, while the Court of Appeals reversed, ordered the school to abate the noise and directed it to pay PHP 500,000.00 nominal damages and PHP 100,000.00 attorney's fees and the Supreme Court granted the school's petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and reinstated the trial court's dismissal. The Supreme Court recalled that noise is not a nuisance per se and becomes actionable only when it unreasonably affects the health or comfort of ordinary people. It found that the residents' witnesses showed at most minor inconveniences. It also found that the school had renovated its chapel and held special gatherings off campus. It had raised its perimeter fence and planted trees as noise buffers.[^59] The case establishes that liability for acts *contra bonos mores* under Article 21 requires a legal act that is contrary to morals, good customs, public order or public policy and done with intent to injure, so that academic noise from legitimate school operations without malice or bad faith is not actionable.[^60]

### Independent Civil Action Under Articles 21 and 33 for Fraudulent Land Title Petitions

After Samson Eng Guan Hao died on August 24, 2007, Jennifer Lagahid executed an Affidavit of Self-Adjudication and two Affidavits of Loss falsely claiming to be Samson's lawful wife and alleging that the owner's duplicate copies of 15 Transfer Certificates of Title were lost, enabling her to obtain new duplicate titles in Miscellaneous Case No. 2008-086 until Samson's brother and business partner Angelito O. Hao, who held the original duplicate titles, had the order voided on a petition for relief from judgment and filed four perjury charges and a separate civil action for damages in Civil Case No. 2009-302.[^61] The Regional Trial Court of Cagayan de Oro City, Branch 21 found that Jennifer violated Article 21 and ordered her to pay Angelito PHP 50,000.00 actual damages, PHP 100,000.00 attorney's fees, PHP 2,000.00 appearance fee, PHP 50,000.00 litigation expenses, PHP 200,000.00 moral damages, PHP 100,000.00 exemplary damages and costs, which the Court of Appeals reversed by dismissing the complaint on grounds of *res judicata* and *litis pendentia* and the Supreme Court granted Angelito's petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and reinstated the trial court's decision ordering Jennifer to pay PHP 50,000.00 actual damages, PHP 100,000.00 attorney's fees, PHP 2,000.00 appearance fee, PHP 50,000.00 litigation expenses, PHP 200,000.00 moral damages, PHP 100,000.00 exemplary damages and costs with 6% annual legal interest from finality.[^62] The Supreme Court held that damages arising from the crime itself were deemed instituted with the perjury complaints because Angelito filed them first without reserving the civil action. It held that his action for damages based on fraud was an independent civil action under Article 33 that needed no reservation. It was not an omitted compulsory counterclaim in a proceeding to which he was not a party and where the court lost jurisdiction once the titles proved not to be lost. The trial court had found that her misrepresentations violated Article 21 and the Supreme Court reinstated its award.[^63]

### Public Humiliation and Slut-Shaming of a Minor Student by Boyfriend's Parents

Disapproving of their son Paul's special friendship with thirteen-year-old honor student AAA, Spouses Melchor and Yolanda Dorao repeatedly went to the children's high school in La Union and to a November 30, 2004 parents' meeting where they publicly called AAA a flirt ("malanding babae"), sexually aggressive ("makati ang laman") and a woman of loose morals ("puta") and accused her of dragging their son into a restroom, causing AAA to suffer depression, attempt suicide by drug overdose, lose her academic honors and transfer schools.[^64] In the damage suit where Spouses BBB and CCC prayed for P100,000.00 moral damages and P50,000.00 exemplary damages, the Regional Trial Court of La Union, Branch 34 ordered Spouses Dorao jointly and severally to pay PHP 30,000.00 moral damages, PHP 20,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 30,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses, which the Court of Appeals affirmed and the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution with modification imposing 6% annual legal interest on the PHP 30,000.00 moral damages, PHP 20,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 30,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses from finality until full satisfaction.[^65] The decision holds that parental duty cannot justify cruel or degrading public humiliation that debases a minor child's human dignity, rendering the wrongdoers solidarily liable for moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Articles 21, 26 and 2219(10) of the Civil Code.[^66]

### Deceptive Mortgage Redemption Insurance Deduction on an Ineligible Business Loan

Maria Josefina G. Miranda and her co-borrowers obtained three loans totaling P2,400,000.00 (P850,000.00, P1,150,000.00 and P400,000.00) under a P3,000,000.00 credit line from Land Bank of the Philippines, which released P2,390,699.00 after deducting P5,700.82 (also referred to in the complaint as P5,700.00) as a Mortgage Redemption Insurance premium even though coverage applied only to consumer loans and not Miranda's business loan, leading Land Bank to foreclose the mortgage for P3,186,731.10 and purchase the property for P5,115,904.97 after co-borrower Robert Glenn Fox died.[^67] The Regional Trial Court of Mariveles, Bataan denied annulment of the foreclosure because no insurance application was perfected, but ordered Land Bank to pay Php150,000.00 moral damages, reimburse Php5,700.82 and pay Php100,000.00 attorney's fees and costs, which the Court of Appeals affirmed and the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution on the consolidated petitions with modification imposing 6% annual legal interest from finality until full payment.[^68] The case demonstrates that Article 21 requires a willful act causing injury in violation of the standards of Article 19 and a bank that exceeds its authority as an insurance agent under Article 1897 by deducting an insurance premium without disclosing that the borrower's business loan is ineligible commits an actionable deception supporting moral damages under Article 2219 in relation to Articles 19, 20 and 21.[^69]

### Claimant's Bad Faith as a Bar to Recovery After a Broken Engagement

German citizen Jan Banach courted Jhonna Guevarra by introducing himself under the false name Roger Brawner and claiming to be divorced when he was still married to his third wife and after they agreed to marry, he sent her P500,000.00 to help her family avoid eviction and buy a lot for their conjugal home, only for Guevarra to break up with him upon discovering his lies and true marital status.[^70] In Banach's suit under Articles 20, 21 and 22, the Regional Trial Court held Guevarra and her parents liable for actual damages, moral damages and attorney's fees, which the Court of Appeals modified by deleting moral damages and attorney's fees due to Banach's fraud while still ordering the return of the P500,000.00 under unjust enrichment and the Supreme Court granted Guevarra's petition and deleted the P500,000.00 actual damages award.[^71] The Supreme Court recalled that a mere breach of promise to marry is not actionable and that *Wassmer v. Velez* awarded damages under Article 21 for walking out of a prepared wedding contrary to good customs. It held that the human relations provisions presuppose good faith on the claimant's part. Banach lied about his name and marital status and those lies justified calling off the engagement. He therefore could not recover under those provisions. Article 22 on unjust enrichment did not apply because the P500,000.00 was a gift. The Supreme Court also said that litigation over a broken heart and a broken promise must be discouraged because the choice of whom to marry belongs to each person.[^72]

### Intentional Failure of Guardian Wife to Bring Infirm Patriarch to Family Birthday Celebration

After confirming for a year that she would bring her physically and mentally infirm 90-year-old husband Pascasio Banaria to a 90th birthday celebration organized by his children and contributing P5,000.00 toward the expenses, second wife and legal guardian Adelaida Navarro-Banaria took Pascasio to Tarlac the day before the event and failed to inform the family until after the celebration had concluded.[^73] The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City awarded the children US$3,619.00 travel expenses, P61,200.00 for food and refreshments, P3,000.00 for the birthday cake, P3,275.00 for balloon arrangements, P60,000.00 moral damages per plaintiff, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P60,000.00 attorney's fees, which the Court of Appeals modified by deleting the US$3,619.00 award and granting Php300,000.00 lump-sum moral damages, Php30,000.00 exemplary damages and Php50,000.00 attorney's fees and the Supreme Court denied Adelaida's petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. The Supreme Court found that she should have told the children at once when Pascasio decided not to attend and called her excuse about a grabbed phone feeble and unrealistic. It sustained the P61,200.00, P3,000.00 and P3,275.00 actual damages and the P300,000.00 lump-sum moral damages because the children suffered serious anxiety and humiliation before the guests.[^74] The decision holds that a spouse and guardian who fails to observe good faith in the exercise of her authority by intentionally breaking her commitment to bring her husband to a family event without timely notice violates Article 19 and must compensate the injured family members for actual, moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees pursuant to Article 21.[^75]

### Good-Faith Extrajudicial Demolition and Storage Under an Express Lease Stipulation

After the extended term of Corazon D. Aniceto's lease over El Rancho Cafe and Restaurant at Camp John Hay, Baguio City expired on May 17, 2007 and the trial court denied her application for a preliminary injunction, lessor Camp John Hay Development Corporation gave repeated notices to vacate and then invoked Article VI, Section 1 and Article X, Section 2 of the Lease Contract by demolishing the restaurant structure from April 29 to May 1, 2008 and inventorying and storing Aniceto's personal properties at its Roosevelt Building.[^76] The Regional Trial Court of Baguio City ordered CJH Development and two of its lawyers solidarily to pay Aniceto P2,183,625.00 actual damages less undamaged properties returned, P1,000,000.00 moral damages, P500,000.00 exemplary damages and P200,000.00 attorney's fees, while the Court of Appeals reversed, absolved the lawyers, deducted P 2,800,000.00 for permanent improvements, deleted moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees and ordered CJH Development to pay Php2,183,625.00 less undamaged personal properties, if any, kept at its Roosevelt Building and the Supreme Court denied Aniceto's petition, granted CJH Development's petition, deleted the P2,183,625.00 actual damages award and ordered that remaining personal properties stored with CJH Development, if any, be turned over to Aniceto.[^77] The case shows that extrajudicial repossession and demolition of leased premises upon lease expiration pursuant to an express contractual authorization and prior notice is a valid exercise of a right in good faith that creates no liability for actual damages under Articles 19, 20 and 21, rendering any resulting loss *damnum absque injuria*.[^78]

### Bad-Faith Withholding of Electric Cooperative Certification, Extortion and Public Humiliation

After Engr. Elmer T. Fudalan paid a P48.12 membership fee to BOHECO I Electric Cooperative, Inc. and had a BOHECO I authorized electrician tap his farmhouse line in Baclayon, Bohol to a check meter while Barangay Power Association Chairperson Crispina Raso was unavailable, Raso and BOHECO I clerk Ismael G. Lomarda withheld the required certification, repeatedly told Fudalan "Sabut sabuton lang ni nato" ("let us just settle this"), demanded a P1,750.00 penalty or promissory note when his actual electric consumption was only P20.00 and publicly shouted "This is an illegal tapping" before policemen and passersby while cutting off his electricity.[^79] The Regional Trial Court of Tagbilaran City ordered Lomarda and Raso solidarily to pay Fudalan P451.65 actual damages, P200,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees and P20,000.00 litigation expenses under Article 21 and the Court of Appeals affirmed, after which the Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed with modification ordering Lomarda and Raso jointly and severally to pay Fudalan P451.65 actual damages, P50,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P25,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses.[^80] The decision holds that even where enforcing utility requirements is a legal act, conducting it contrary to morals and good customs with intent to extort money and publicly humiliate the consumer renders the officials solidarily liable as joint tortfeasors under Article 19 in relation to Article 21 and they cannot invoke the clean hands doctrine when their own bad faith made formal compliance impossible.[^81]

### Inapplicability of Article 21 to Pure Negligence Claims Governed by Article 2176

Relying on a January 11, 2008 Medical Report issued by accredited clinic St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. certifying overseas worker applicant Jonathan V. Raguindin as "fit for employment", recruitment agency LWV Construction Corporation deployed Raguindin to Saudi Arabia at an alleged expense of P84,373.41 and after Raguindin purportedly tested positive for the Hepatitis C virus in Saudi Arabia on March 24, 2008 and April 28, 2008 and was repatriated, LWV sued St. Martin Polyclinic for damages.[^82] The Metropolitan Trial Court of Mandaluyong City ordered the clinic to pay P84,373.41 actual damages, P20,000.00 attorney's fees and costs under Articles 19, 20 and 21, which the Regional Trial Court affirmed and the Court of Appeals modified by replacing actual damages with P50,000.00 temperate damages and the Supreme Court granted the clinic's petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and dismissed LWV's complaint for lack of merit.[^83] The case clarifies that Article 21 applies only to willful acts done *contra bonos mores* where the actor intends the act and desires the injurious outcome, so a claim alleging mere negligence without a pre-existing contractual relation or statutory breach is governed by Article 2176 rather than Articles 19, 20 and 21.[^84]

### Deceptive Acquisition of Distributor's Customer List and Cut-Throat Unfair Competition

Before the renewal of its 13-year exclusive dealership agreement with Quezon City softdrink wholesalers Spouses Jose and Lilibeth Bernardo (doing business as Jolly Beverage Enterprises, with monthly quotas of 7,000 to 8,000 cases), Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc. required the spouses to submit their customer list on the promise of a long-term renewal, then used the list and trailed their trucks to sell directly to their customers at lower prices (such as P40.00 per box of cans in groceries and P76.00 per case versus the wholesalers' P112.00) and through "Coke Alok" free-bottle promos, driving the spouses out of business with P449,154 in unpaid deliveries.[^85] On a complaint praying for P1,000,000.00 actual damages for loss of goodwill, P200,000 moral damages, P100,000 exemplary damages and P100,000 attorney's fees, the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City awarded P500,000 temperate damages offset against the P449,154 unpaid balance plus P50,000 moral damages, P20,000 exemplary damages and P100,000 attorney's fees, which the Court of Appeals affirmed in toto and the Supreme Court denied Coca-Cola's petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification imposing 6% annual legal interest from finality and upheld the total compensation of the spouses' P449,154 obligation and accrued legal interest against the P500,000 temperate damages.[^86] The ruling demonstrates that a manufacturer that deceives its distributor into surrendering its customer list and deploys oppressive, cut-throat marketing tactics to destroy the distributor's business violates Articles 19, 20, 21 and 28, justifying temperate damages under Article 2224, moral damages under Article 2219(10), exemplary damages and attorney's fees.[^87]

### Bad-Faith Breach of Contract by Issuing a Closed-Account Check and Diverting Goods

After Dan T. Lim delivered scrap papers worth P7,220,968.31 to Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. through its President and Chief Executive Officer Candida A. Santos under an agreement to pay cash or deliver finished products of equivalent value, Arco issued a partial-payment check for P1,487,766.68 drawn against a closed account and on the same day executed a memorandum of agreement binding itself to deliver 600 tons of finished Test Liner at P18.50 per kilogram (using 600 metric tons of Lim's raw materials at P6.50 per kilogram) to Eric Sy's Megapack Container Corporation without Lim's conformity.[^88] The Regional Trial Court of Valenzuela City dismissed Lim's collection suit on the theory of novation, while the Court of Appeals reversed and ordered Arco and Santos solidarily to pay Lim P7,220,968.31 with 12% annual interest from demand, P50,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees and the Supreme Court denied the petition in part, affirmed the Court of Appeals and ordered Arco and Santos solidarily to pay Lim P7,220,968.31 with 6% annual interest from the May 5, 2007 extrajudicial demand until full satisfaction plus P50,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees.[^89] The decision distinguishes Article 20 (willful or negligent violations of law) from Article 21 (willful acts not proscribed by law that violate morals, good customs or public policy) and holds that breaching a contract in bad faith by issuing a check on a closed account and attempting to shift the debt to a third party violates Articles 19, 20, 21 and 1159 and supports moral and exemplary damages under Articles 2219(10), 2220 and 2232.[^90]

### Forcible Ejection of Lessee's Permitted Lodgers Prior to Lease Expiration

Aurora B. Go leased the entire ground floor of Doris U. Sunbanun's residential house in Cebu City for a one-year term from July 7, 1995 to July 7, 1996 under a contract expressly allowing the lessee to use the premises as a dwelling or lodging house, paid a P16,000 deposit and earned P15,000 per month from lodgers, but in March 1996 Sunbanun drove away Go's lodgers without notifying Go in Hong Kong and padlocked the vacated rooms by April 15, 1996.[^91] After Sunbanun moved for judgment on the pleadings at pre-trial, the trial court ordered her to pay Go P45,000.00 actual damages for three months of lost lodging income and P8,000.00 attorney's fees, which the Court of Appeals modified by adding P50,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and costs and the Supreme Court denied Sunbanun's petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution.[^92] The case shows that a lessor who forcibly ejects her lessee's permitted lodgers and pre-terminates the lease in bad faith without valid reason or notice is liable for moral damages under Articles 2219(10) and 2220 in relation to Article 21, exemplary damages under Article 2232 and attorney's fees under Article 2208.[^93]

### Fraudulent Encashment of a Signed Blank Check as Self-Help

Believing that their brother Arturo Ignacio, Jr. had preterminated and kept a P3,000,000.00 money market placement belonging to Rosita A.I. Cusi, sisters Alice A.I. Sandejas and Rosita took a signed blank check that Arturo had left payable to lessor Dr. Manuel Borja for the condominium lease of nephew Benjamin A.I. Espiritu, filled in the amount of P3,000,000.00 dated March 17, 1995 and had Alice's driver pose as Dr. Borja to open a joint savings account at Security Bank and Trust Company without identification or endorsement before withdrawing P1 million and transferring P2 million to investment accounts.[^94] The Regional Trial Court of Pasig City ordered Security Bank, two of its officers, Alice and Rosita solidarily to pay Spouses Ignacio P3,000,000.00 with legal interest from March 17, 1995, P500,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages, P300,000.00 attorney's fees and costs while awarding Benjamin Espiritu P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees on his counterclaim and dismissing the counterclaim of niece Patricia A.I. Sandejas, which the Court of Appeals modified by deleting the awards to Benjamin Espiritu and reducing the awards for Spouses Ignacio to P3,000,000.00 with legal interest, P200,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees and costs and the Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with costs.[^95] The ruling holds that fraudulently encashing a check as an act of self-help violates the law under Article 20 as well as the public policy under Article 21 that no person should put the law into his own hands, justifying solidary liability with the grossly negligent bank for moral damages under Article 2219(10), exemplary damages under Article 2229 and attorney's fees under Article 2208.[^96]

### Moral Damages Under Article 2219(10) and Article 21 in Bigamy Prosecutions

Although married to Rubylus Gaña in 1975, 39-year-old Eduardo P. Manuel courted 21-year-old student Tina B. Gandalera in 1996, repeatedly assured her along with his parents that he was single, declared himself "single" in their April 22, 1996 marriage contract and lived with her as her husband before abandoning her without financial support in January 2001, leading Tina to discover his prior marriage from the National Statistics Office.[^97] The Regional Trial Court of Baguio City, Branch 3 convicted Manuel of bigamy, sentenced him to imprisonment and ordered him to indemnify Tina P200,000.00 as moral damages plus costs, which the Court of Appeals affirmed with modification of the minimum prison term and the Supreme Court denied Manuel's petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision with costs.[^98] The decision establishes that even though bigamy is not expressly listed in Article 2219, an accused who deceives an innocent woman into contracting a bigamous marriage through continuous fraud commits a willful act contrary to morals and public policy under Articles 19, 20 and 21 and is liable to pay the defrauded woman moral damages under Article 2219(10).[^99]

### Incompatibility Between Psychological Incapacity Under Article 36 and Willful Injury Under Article 21

In declaring the July 4, 1979 marriage of Noel A. Buenaventura and Isabel Lucia Singh Buenaventura null and void *ab initio* on the ground of Noel's psychological incapacity under Article 36 of the Family Code, the trial court ordered Noel to pay Isabel 2.5 million pesos moral damages (on a 7 million peso counterclaim), 1 million pesos exemplary damages, P100,000.00 attorney's fees, P50,000.00 litigation expenses, 50% of his P3,675,335.79 retirement benefits (or P1,837,667.89) with 12% interest, half of his corporate shares and P15,000.00 monthly child support (which the Court of Appeals increased to P20,000 *pendente lite* before affirming the judgment in toto).[^100] On consolidated petitions, the Supreme Court modified the Court of Appeals decision and resolution in G.R. No. 127449 by deleting the awards of moral damages, exemplary damages, attorney's fees, litigation expenses and costs while sustaining the equal division of retirement benefits and shares on the basis of co-ownership under Article 147 of the Family Code and dismissed G.R. No. 127358 on child support as moot because the couple's son had reached the age of majority.[^101] The case establishes that because Article 21 requires an act to be done willfully and in complete freedom, a court cannot award moral and exemplary damages under Article 21 based on the very same acts that it found to be the product of an innate, non-willful psychological incapacity under Article 36 of the Family Code.[^102]

### Discreet Request for an Uninvited Guest to Leave an Intimate Party

After actor Roberto Reyes (known as "Amay Bisaya") joined guest Dr. Violeta Filart at an intimate penthouse birthday party for Nikko Hotel Manila Garden's general manager Masakazu Tsuruoka without being on the 60-person guest list, 44-year-old hotel executive secretary Ruby Lim, who had served the hotel for 20 years, verified with Filart's sister that Reyes had not been invited and approached Reyes closely after he took food from the buffet to discreetly ask him to finish his meal and leave, prompting Reyes to scream and sue for One Million Pesos actual damages, One Million Pesos moral and/or exemplary damages and Two Hundred Thousand Pesos attorney's fees.[^103] The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 104 dismissed the complaint, while the Court of Appeals reversed and ordered Hotel Nikko, Lim and Filart solidarily to pay Reyes P200,000 exemplary damages, P200,000 moral damages and P10,000 attorney's fees and the Supreme Court granted the petition of Lim and Hotel Nikko, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and affirmed the trial court's dismissal without costs.[^104] The ruling confirms that Article 21 requires a legal act done contrary to morals, good customs, public order or public policy with intent to injure, so discreetly asking an uninvited guest in good faith and without ill motive to leave a private party does not violate Articles 19 and 21.[^105]

### Philippine Jurisdiction and Choice of Law Over a Transnational Tort Under Articles 19 and 21

After Filipina flight attendant Milagros P. Morada survived an attempted rape by two Saudi co-crew members during a layover in Jakarta, Indonesia, her employer Saudi Arabian Airlines pressured her in Jeddah to drop charges and later assured her in Manila that a return trip to Jeddah was only for a routinary investigation, where airline officers escorted her to a Saudi court that convicted her of Islamic law offenses and sentenced her to five months imprisonment and 286 lashes before the Prince of Makkah dismissed the case.[^106] When Morada filed an amended complaint for damages under Articles 19 and 21 before the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 89 (whose jurisdiction under Republic Act No. 7691 covered demands exceeding P100,000.00 or P200,000.00 in Metro Manila), the trial court and the Court of Appeals denied the airline's motions to dismiss and for preliminary injunction and the Supreme Court dismissed the airline's petition for certiorari and remanded Civil Case No. Q-93-18394 to Branch 89 for further proceedings.[^107] The decision holds that Article 21 gives flesh to Article 19 by providing judicially enforceable remedies in the municipal forum and that under the conflict-of-laws "State of the most significant relationship" rule, Philippine law governs a resident Filipina's tort action against a resident foreign carrier where the deceit and overall injury lodged in the Philippines.[^108]

### Moral Seduction Through a Fraudulent Promise of Marriage

Iranian medical exchange student Gashem Shookat Baksh, then 29 years old, courted 21-year-old (22 when the complaint was filed) virgin luncheonette waitress Marilou T. Gonzales in Dagupan City, traveled to her hometown in Bugallon, Pangasinan to secure her parents' consent to marry her in October 1987, induced her to resign her job, live with him in his apartment and surrender her virginity and then maltreated her, gave her medicine to abort her fetus and repudiated the marriage agreement by falsely claiming he was already married to a woman in Bacolod City.[^109] On Marilou's complaint praying for at least P45,000.00 damages and P600.00 actual expenses (against Baksh's counterclaim for P5,000.00 expenses and P25,000.00 moral damages), the Regional Trial Court of Lingayen, Pangasinan, Branch 38 ordered Baksh to pay Marilou P20,000.00 moral damages, P3,000.00 attorney's fees, P2,000.00 litigation expenses and costs under Article 21, which the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court affirmed in full by denying Baksh's petition with costs.[^110] The landmark ruling establishes that while a breach of promise to marry *per se* is not actionable and mutual passion bars recovery, a man who uses a fraudulent and deceitful promise of marriage as a scheme to induce a woman to surrender her virtue is liable for moral damages under Article 21 for moral seduction.[^111]

### Elements of Article 21 and Honest Mistake of Identity as *Damnum Absque Injuria*

After a P2,575.00 check drawn against the account of "E.L. Woodworks" bounced in part payment of mild steel plates delivered to Guaranteed Industries, Inc. at 3267 V. Mapa Street, Sta. Mesa, Manila and government records listed "Eugenio S. Baltao" as president of Guaranteed and "Eugenio Baltao" as proprietor of E.L. Woodworks at the same Baltao Building address as his namesake son Eugenio Baltao III, unpaid supplier Albenson Enterprises Corporation, Jesse Yap and Benjamin Mendiona filed a Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 complaint that resulted in an Information against Eugenio S. Baltao after he failed to clarify that the check belonged to his son.[^112] In Eugenio S. Baltao's civil suit for damages, the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City ordered Albenson, Yap and Mendiona to pay P133,350.00 actual or compensatory damages, P1,000,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and P100,000.00 attorney's fees, which the Court of Appeals modified by reducing moral damages to P500,000.00 and attorney's fees to P50,000.00 and the Supreme Court granted the petition and reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision with costs against Baltao.[^113] The decision formulates the three elements of Article 21 (a legal act, contrary to morals, good custom, public order or public policy and done with intent to injure), emphasizes that Articles 19 and 21 require an intentional act unlike Article 20 and holds that filing a criminal complaint in good faith with probable cause under an honest mistake of identity results in *damnum absque injuria*.[^114]

### Abusive Dismissal, Baseless Criminal Prosecutions and Blacklisting Letter

One day after purchasing agent Restituto M. Tobias discovered and reported fraudulent transactions at Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corporation in November 1972, Executive Vice-President Herbert C. Hendry placed him on forced leave, called him a "crook" and a "swindler", remarked that "You Filipinos cannot be trusted", threatened to file one hundred cases against him, filed six criminal complaints for estafa and violation of Article 290 of the Revised Penal Code that were all dismissed by the fiscal despite two police crime laboratory reports and a lie detector test clearing Tobias and sent an unsolicited letter to prospective employer Republic Telephone Company stating that Tobias had been dismissed for dishonesty.[^115] On Tobias's complaint praying for P100,000.00 actual damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages, P800,000.00 moral damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees, the Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch IX ordered Globe Mackay and Hendry to pay P80,000.00 actual damages, P200,000.00 moral damages, P20,000.00 exemplary damages, P30,000.00 attorney's fees and costs, which the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court affirmed by denying the petition.[^116] The case establishes that an employer's right to dismiss an employee must be distinguished from the abusive manner of its exercise and when a dismissal is accompanied by baseless accusations, malicious criminal complaints and an unsolicited blacklisting letter, *damnum absque injuria* does not apply and the employer is liable for damages under Article 19 in relation to Articles 21, 26, 2176 and 2219(10).[^117]

### Unilateral Revocation of Agency Coupled With an Interest and Padlocking of Branch Office

Under an October 19, 1960 arrangement in which travel agent Lina O. Sevilla bound herself solidarily with Tourist World Service, Inc. to pay monthly rentals to lessor Segundina Noguera and managed Tourist World's Ermita branch office for a 4% airline fare commission (while 3% went to Tourist World), Tourist World's board abolished her manager position after hearing she was connected with a rival firm, terminated the lease without notice to her, disconnected the office telephone lines and had corporate secretary Eliseo S. Canilao padlock the premises after office hours on June 4, 1962.[^118] After the trial court and the Court of Appeals dismissed Sevilla's complaint and Noguera's counterclaim, the Supreme Court reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution, absolved Noguera for lack of connivance and ordered Tourist World Service, Inc. and Eliseo Canilao jointly and severally to indemnify Lina Sevilla P25,000.00 (printed in the dispositive paragraph as 25,00.00) moral damages, P10,000.00 exemplary damages and P5,000.00 nominal and/or temperate damages with costs.[^119] The ruling holds that an agency coupled with an interest cannot be revoked at will and that a principal and its officer who take the law into their own hands by disconnecting telephones and padlocking the branch office without notice are solidarily liable for moral, exemplary and nominal or temperate damages under Article 21 in relation to Article 2219(10).[^120]

### Civil Court Jurisdiction Over Resigned Executive's Action for Oppressive Non-Payment Under Articles 1701 and 21

After Jose D. Calderon, Sr. acquired full ownership of Luzon Brokerage Corporation and allegedly induced Executive Vice-President Atty. Antonio C. Amor to remain by promising him the same compensation and 1,200 shares of stock before Amor resigned on December 31, 1977, Amor filed Civil Case No. 28845 before the Court of First Instance of Rizal, Branch XXI against Calderon, Sr. and four individuals to recover P229,355.38 in unpaid salaries, allowances and reimbursable expenses, P 1.7 M in moral damages (totaling at least P2 M) and 1,200 shares of stock for their oppressive, willful and fraudulent refusal to pay.[^121] The Court of First Instance denied the defendants' motion to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction and the Court of Appeals dismissed their certiorari petition, after which the Supreme Court denied certiorari, upheld the Court of Appeals judgment and lifted its restraining order without pronouncement as to costs.[^122] The decision establishes that where a resigned corporate officer does not seek reinstatement and sues the controlling individuals for their oppressive and fraudulent refusal to pay his monies and assign promised shares in violation of Articles 1701 and 21 of the Civil Code, the dispute is intrinsically civil and falls within the exclusive jurisdiction of regular courts rather than Labor Arbiters.[^123]

### False Shoplifting Accusation, Public Humiliation and Unauthorized P5.00 Fine

While shopping with his wife and two daughters at Grand Union Supermarket's South Supermarket in Makati and paying P77.00 for groceries, Procter and Gamble Philippines executive Jose J. Espino, Jr. forgot to pay for a P3.85 cylindrical "rat tail" file placed in his front shirt breast pocket and despite his immediate apology and offer to pay at the exit, a uniformed guard marched him past incoming customers to a rear cubicle for an incident report labeled "Shoplifting" and escorted him to a public desk by the first checkout counter where Nelia Santos-Fandino remarked "Ano, nakaw na naman ito" and confiscated his P5.00 bill as a fine to reward guards before making him line up with a P50.00 bill to pay for the file.[^124] The Court of First Instance of Pasig, Rizal dismissed Espino's complaint, while the Court of Appeals reversed and ordered the supermarket and Fandino solidarily to pay P75,000.00 moral damages, P25,000.00 exemplary damages and P5,000.00 attorney's fees and the Supreme Court modified the Court of Appeals judgment by ordering petitioners jointly and severally to pay Espino P5,000.00 moral damages and P2,000.00 attorney's fees and to return the P5.00 fine, while deleting the P25,000.00 exemplary damages award.[^125] The case holds that falsely branding a customer a thief in public and imposing an unauthorized fine humiliate and degrade his dignity in violation of Articles 19, 21 and 26 in relation to Article 2219, though the customer's contributory forgetfulness under Article 2214 and the store's initial probable cause under Article 429 mitigate moral damages and bar exemplary damages.[^126]

### Independent Cause of Action Under Article 21 for Woman Forced Into Carnal Intercourse by Married Man

Assisted by her parents, Carmen Quimiguing sued her married neighbor Felix Icao in Civil Case No. 1590 before the Court of First Instance of Zamboanga del Norte for support at P120.00 per month, damages and attorney's fees, alleging that Icao succeeded in having carnal intercourse with her several times by force and intimidation and without her consent, rendering her pregnant despite drugs he supplied and forcing her to stop studying.[^127] After the trial judge dismissed the complaint for lack of cause of action because it did not allege that the child had been born and refused to allow an amendment alleging that she had later given birth to a baby girl, the Supreme Court reversed and set aside the appealed orders and remanded the case to the court of origin for further proceedings with costs against Icao. It found the dismissal doubly erroneous because the conceived child had a provisional personality under Article 40 and the mother had a cause of action of her own on the allegations of the complaint.[^128] The ruling establishes that in addition to a conceived unborn child's right to support under Article 40, a married man who forces a woman not his wife to yield to his lust commits a clear violation of her rights that gives the victim a cause of action of her own for damages under Article 21 supported by Article 2219(3) and (10).[^129]

### Forcible Ejection of First-Class Airline Passenger as Both Breach of Contract and Tort Under Article 21

Civil engineer Rafael Carrascoso, travelling as part of a group of 48 Filipino pilgrims on a confirmed first-class round-trip Manila-Rome ticket issued by Air France through Philippine Air Lines, rode in first class from Manila to Bangkok, where Air France's Bangkok manager forcibly ejected him from his first-class seat amid a heated commotion and threatened to throw him out of the airplane to give his seat to a "white man" who had no proven better right.[^130] The Court of First Instance of Manila sentenced Air France to pay Carrascoso P25,000.00 moral damages, P10,000.00 exemplary damages, P393.20 fare difference with legal interest from filing of the complaint, P3,000.00 attorney's fees and costs, which the Court of Appeals affirmed while slightly reducing the ticket refund to P383.10 and the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals judgment with costs against Air France.[^131] The landmark decision holds that a contract of air carriage is imbued with a public duty to protect passengers from misconduct and indignities by carrier employees, so that a manager's willful and malevolent expulsion of a first-class passenger in bad faith is both a breach of contract and a tort actionable under Article 21 and Article 2219(10).[^132]

## Key Cases

- [*Couples for Christ School of the Morning Star v. Malonda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70275) (2025) -- Where subdivision residents sued a licensed school for damages over school noise and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal by ordering noise abatement, PHP 500,000.00 nominal damages and PHP 100,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that Article 21 requires a legal act contrary to morals, good customs or public policy done with intent to injure and reinstated the trial court's dismissal because academic noise from legitimate school operations that the school abated within environmental limits involved no malice or bad faith.[^148]
- [*Hao v. Lagahid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70070) (2025) -- Where a woman falsely claimed to be a decedent's widow and fraudulently obtained new owner's duplicate copies of 15 land titles actually held by the decedent's brother and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's award of PHP 50,000.00 actual damages, PHP 200,000.00 moral damages, PHP 100,000.00 exemplary damages, PHP 100,000.00 attorney's fees, PHP 2,000.00 appearance fee and PHP 50,000.00 litigation expenses on *res judicata* and *litis pendentia* grounds, the Supreme Court held that the damage suit for fraud was an independent civil action under Article 33 and reinstated the trial court's judgment finding a violation of Article 21 with 6% annual interest from finality.[^149]
- [*Spouses Dorao v. Spouses BBB and CCC*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68985) (2023) -- Where a boy's parents repeatedly went to a school and a parents' meeting to publicly call their son's thirteen-year-old girlfriend a flirt and a woman of loose morals, driving her to attempt suicide and transfer schools and the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded PHP 30,000.00 moral damages, PHP 20,000.00 exemplary damages and PHP 30,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses, the Supreme Court held that publicly humiliating a minor child under the guise of discipline violates public policy under Articles 21, 26 and 2219(10) and affirmed the awards with 6% annual legal interest from finality.[^150]
- [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826) (2023) -- Where a lender bank deducted P5,700.82 for Mortgage Redemption Insurance from a P2,400,000.00 business loan without disclosing that coverage applied only to consumer loans and the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded Php150,000.00 moral damages, Php5,700.82 reimbursement and Php100,000.00 attorney's fees while upholding foreclosure, the Supreme Court held that the bank's non-disclosure of its agency limits under Article 1897 violated Articles 19, 20 and 21 and affirmed the awards with 6% annual legal interest from finality.[^151]
- [*Guevarra v. Banach*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68051) (2021) -- Where a German suitor courted a Filipina under a false name while concealing his existing marriage and gave her P500,000.00 to help her family avoid eviction and buy a lot and the Court of Appeals deleted the trial court's moral damages and attorney's fees awards after she broke off the engagement but still ordered return of the P500,000.00, the Supreme Court held that the human relations provisions presuppose good faith on the claimant's part and deleted the P500,000.00 award because the suitor's own fraud and deceit barred recovery under the human relations provisions and the money was a gift outside Article 22.[^152]
- [*Navarro-Banaria v. Banaria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66456) (2020) -- Where a second wife and guardian confirmed her infirm husband's attendance at his 90th birthday celebration organized by his children and then took him to Tarlac without notifying them and the Court of Appeals modified the trial court's judgment by deleting US$3,619.00 travel expenses and awarding P61,200.00, P3,000.00 and P3,275.00 actual damages, Php300,000.00 moral damages, Php30,000.00 exemplary damages and Php50,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that her intentional bad-faith breach of commitment violated Article 19 in relation to Article 21 and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^153]
- [*CJH Development Corp. v. Aniceto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66462) (2020) -- Where a lessor demolished a restaurant structure after lease expiration and stored the lessee's personal properties pursuant to express lease clauses and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's P1,000,000.00 moral, P500,000.00 exemplary and P200,000.00 attorney's fees awards while retaining P2,183,625.00 actual damages less undamaged properties returned, the Supreme Court held that extrajudicial repossession under a valid contract clause in good faith creates no liability under Articles 19, 20 and 21, deleted the P2,183,625.00 actual damages award and ordered remaining stored properties, if any, turned over to the lessee.[^154]
- [*Lomarda v. Fudalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66363) (2020) -- Where a barangay power association chairperson and an electric cooperative clerk withheld a required certification, demanded P1,750.00 when only P20.00 was due and publicly shouted that a farmhouse owner had an illegal tapping before cutting off his power and the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded P451.65 actual damages, P200,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees and P20,000.00 litigation expenses, the Supreme Court held them solidarily liable as joint tortfeasors under Article 19 in relation to Article 21 and affirmed with modification reducing moral damages to P50,000.00, exemplary damages to P50,000.00 and attorney's fees and litigation expenses to P25,000.00 alongside P451.65 actual damages.[^155]
- [*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692) (2017) -- Where a recruitment agency sued a medical clinic after a worker certified "fit for employment" later tested positive for Hepatitis C in Saudi Arabia and the Court of Appeals modified the lower courts' P84,373.41 actual damages and P20,000.00 attorney's fees award under Articles 19, 20 and 21 to P50,000.00 temperate damages, the Supreme Court held that Article 21 applies only to willful acts *contra bonos mores* whereas pure negligence without a pre-existing contract or statutory breach falls under Article 2176 and reversed and dismissed the complaint for lack of proof of negligence.[^156]
- [*Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc. v. Spouses Bernardo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62599) (2016) -- Where a beverage manufacturer obtained its wholesaler's customer list on a false promise of contract renewal and then trailed the wholesaler's trucks and undercut its prices to take over its customers and the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded P500,000 temperate damages offset against P449,154 unpaid deliveries plus P50,000 moral damages, P20,000 exemplary damages and P100,000 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that oppressive cut-throat competition violates Articles 19, 20, 21 and 28 and affirmed the awards with 6% annual interest from finality and full offset of the P449,154 debt and interest.[^157]
- [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134) (2014) -- Where a paper mill issued a partial-payment check for P1,487,766.68 drawn on a closed account for P7,220,968.31 worth of delivered scrap paper and on the same day contracted to deliver its finished products to a third party without the supplier's consent and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal by awarding P7,220,968.31 with interest plus P50,000.00 each for moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees against the corporation and its president, the Supreme Court held that the bad-faith breach violated Articles 19, 20, 21 and 1159 and affirmed the solidary awards with 6% annual interest from extrajudicial demand.[^158]
- [*Sunbanun v. Go*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53551) (2010) -- Where a lessor forcibly ejected her lessee's permitted lodgers and padlocked the rooms three months before lease expiration without notifying the lessee in Hong Kong and the Court of Appeals modified the trial court's judgment on the pleadings by adding P50,000.00 moral damages and P50,000.00 exemplary damages to P45,000.00 actual damages and P8,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that pre-terminating the lease and evicting the lodgers in bad faith warranted moral damages under Articles 2219(10) and 2220 in relation to Article 21 and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^159]
- [*Sandejas v. Spouses Ignacio, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46434) (2007) -- Where two sisters filled in their brother's signed blank check for P3,000,000.00 and encashed it through an impostor at a bank that waived identification and endorsement and the Court of Appeals modified the trial court's judgment by holding the sisters, the bank and its officers solidarily liable for P3,000,000.00 with legal interest, P200,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees while deleting counterclaim damages, the Supreme Court held that fraudulent self-help violates Article 20 and public policy under Article 21 and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^160]
- [*Manuel v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42205) (2005) -- Where a married man courted a 21-year-old student, assured her along with his parents that he was single, declared himself "single" on their marriage contract and later abandoned her and the trial court and Court of Appeals convicted him of bigamy and awarded P200,000.00 moral damages, the Supreme Court held that his continuous fraud and deceit subverted the family and good morals under Articles 19, 20 and 21, authorizing moral damages under Article 2219(10) and affirmed the conviction and P200,000.00 moral damages award.[^161]
- [*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809) (2005) -- Where the trial court and Court of Appeals declared a marriage void *ab initio* for the husband's psychological incapacity under Article 36 of the Family Code and ordered him to pay 2.5 million pesos moral damages, 1 million pesos exemplary damages, P100,000.00 attorney's fees and P50,000.00 litigation expenses under Article 21, the Supreme Court held that acts resulting from an innate psychological incapacity cannot simultaneously be deemed willful under Article 21 and modified the judgment by deleting the moral and exemplary damages, attorney's fees, litigation expenses and costs.[^162]
- [*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407) (2005) -- Where a hotel executive secretary discreetly asked an uninvited actor to finish his food and leave an intimate penthouse birthday party and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal by awarding P200,000 moral damages, P200,000 exemplary damages and P10,000 attorney's fees under Articles 19, 20 and 21, the Supreme Court held that exercising a legitimate right to exclude an uninvited guest in a polite manner without intent to humiliate does not violate Articles 19 and 21 and reinstated the trial court's dismissal.[^163]
- [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36425) (1998) -- Where a Filipina flight attendant sued a resident foreign airline in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City under Articles 19 and 21 for deceiving her in Manila into flying to Jeddah and turning her over to a Saudi court that sentenced her to five months imprisonment and 286 lashes and the trial court and Court of Appeals denied the airline's motion to dismiss, the Supreme Court held that Philippine courts had jurisdiction and that Philippine tort law under Articles 19 and 21 governed under the "State of the most significant relationship" rule and remanded the case for trial.[^164]
- [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html) (1993) -- Where an Iranian medical student courted a 21-year-old virgin waitress, obtained her parents' marriage consent, induced her to live with him and surrender her virginity through a deceitful promise to marry and then maltreated her and repudiated the marriage and the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded P20,000.00 moral damages, P3,000.00 attorney's fees and P2,000.00 litigation expenses under Article 21, the Supreme Court held that moral seduction through a fraudulent promise of marriage is actionable under Article 21 and affirmed the awards.[^165]
- [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html) (1993) -- Where an unpaid supplier filed a Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 complaint against a son sharing the same name and address as his father after a P2,575.00 check bounced and the Court of Appeals modified the trial court's awards to P133,350.00 actual damages, P500,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court defined the three elements of Article 21, held that filing a criminal complaint in good faith with probable cause and without intent to injure produces *damnum absque injuria* and reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision.[^166]
- [*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html) (1989) -- Where an employer and its general manager called the employee who reported company anomalies a "crook" and a "swindler", filed six baseless criminal complaints against him despite police laboratory reports and a lie detector test clearing him and sent an unsolicited letter blocking his hiring at another company and the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded P80,000.00 actual damages, P200,000.00 moral damages, P20,000.00 exemplary damages and P30,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that abusive dismissal and post-dismissal harassment violate Articles 19, 21, 26 and 2176 and affirmed the awards.[^167]
- [*Sevilla v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/apr1988/gr_l-41182_1988.html) (1988) -- Where a travel company and its corporate secretary unilaterally revoked an agency coupled with an interest, disconnected the branch telephone lines and padlocked the branch office after hours without notice to the agent and the trial court and Court of Appeals dismissed the agent's complaint, the Supreme Court held the company and its officer solidarily liable for unwarranted revocation and brazen self-help under Article 21 in relation to Article 2219(10) and reversed the dismissal to award P25,000.00 moral damages, P10,000.00 exemplary damages and P5,000.00 nominal and/or temperate damages.[^168]
- [*Calderon, Sr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/oct1980/gr_52235_1980.html) (1980) -- Where a resigned executive vice-president sued the controlling individuals in the Court of First Instance for P229,355.38 unpaid salaries and allowances, P 1.7 M moral damages and 1,200 promised shares of stock on the ground that their refusal to pay was oppressive, willful and fraudulent under Articles 1701 and 21 and the trial court and Court of Appeals denied dismissal for lack of jurisdiction, the Supreme Court held that the action was intrinsically a civil dispute within regular court jurisdiction rather than Labor Arbiter jurisdiction and upheld the Court of Appeals.[^169]
- [*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html) (1979) -- Where a supermarket guard and officer detained an executive who inadvertently forgot to pay for a P3.85 file sticking out of his shirt pocket, publicly branded him a shoplifter and confiscated his P5.00 bill as a fine for guards and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal by awarding P75,000.00 moral damages, P25,000.00 exemplary damages and P5,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that publicly humiliating the shopper and imposing a fine violated Articles 19, 21 and 26 in relation to Article 2219 and modified the judgment to award P5,000.00 moral damages, P2,000.00 attorney's fees and return of the P5.00 fine while deleting exemplary damages due to probable cause under Article 429 and contributory negligence under Article 2214.[^170]
- [*Quimiguing v. Icao*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1970/jul1970/gr_26795_1970.html) (1970) -- Where an unmarried woman sued her married neighbor for support at P120.00 per month and damages after he forced her into carnal intercourse several times through force and intimidation and impregnated her and the trial court dismissed the complaint for lack of cause of action because the child was not yet born and denied leave to amend after birth, the Supreme Court held that the unborn child had a right to support under Article 40 and the mother had an independent cause of action for moral damages under Article 21 and Article 2219(3) and (10) and reversed and remanded the case.[^171]
- [*Air France v. Carrascoso*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1966/sep1966/gr_l-21438_1966.html) (1966) -- Where an air carrier's manager in Bangkok forcibly ejected a Filipino passenger from his paid first-class seat amid a public commotion and threatened to throw him off the plane to accommodate a "white man" without proving any better right and the Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court's award of P25,000.00 moral damages, P10,000.00 exemplary damages and P3,000.00 attorney's fees while adjusting the fare refund from P393.20 to P383.10, the Supreme Court held that the manager's willful and malevolent act was both a breach of contract and a tort under Article 21 and Article 2219(10) and affirmed the judgment.[^172]

## Suggested Answers

### Problem 1: Moral Seduction Versus Mutual Passion and Bad-Faith Suitor

A 29-year-old foreign medical exchange student courted a 21-year-old virgin waitress, traveled to her parents' rural home to ask for her hand in marriage and promised to wed her at the end of the semester. Relying on his promise and her parents' approval, she resigned her job, moved into his apartment and surrendered her virginity to him. Weeks later, he maltreated her and repudiated the marriage agreement. When she sued him for moral damages under Article 21 of the Civil Code, he moved to dismiss on the grounds that breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong in the Philippines and that she was equally at fault under Article 1412 for consenting to premarital cohabitation. Should the action for moral damages under Article 21 prosper?

**Suggested Answer:** Yes, the woman's action for moral damages under Article 21 of the Civil Code should prosper.[^173] In [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html), the Supreme Court held that while a breach of promise to marry *per se* is not an actionable wrong, where a man's fraudulent and deceptive promise of marriage is the proximate cause of a woman accepting his love and giving herself to him in sexual congress without any real intention on his part to fulfill the promise, the resulting moral seduction and willful injury to her honor justify moral damages under Article 21 in relation to Article 2219(10).[^174] Recovery is awarded not because of the broken promise to marry itself, but because of the fraud and deceit behind it and the *in pari delicto* rule under Article 1412 does not apply where the fault of the innocent victim is not equivalent to the calculated deception of the seducer.[^175]

### Problem 2: Psychological Incapacity Under Article 36 and Moral Damages Under Article 21

In a petition for declaration of nullity of marriage under Article 36 of the Family Code, the trial court found on the basis of psychological testimony that the husband suffered from a severe personality disorder rendering him truly incognitive of the essential marital obligations of love, respect and fidelity and declared the marriage null and void *ab initio*. Relying on the very same post-marriage acts of insensitivity and abandonment that established the husband's psychological incapacity, the trial court ordered him to pay the wife moral and exemplary damages under Article 21 and Article 2217 of the Civil Code. Is the award of moral and exemplary damages under Article 21 proper?

**Suggested Answer:** No, the award of moral and exemplary damages under Article 21 is improper and must be deleted.[^176] In [*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809), the Supreme Court held that Article 21 explicitly requires that the individual willfully cause loss or injury to another, meaning that the act must be done willfully and in complete freedom.[^177] Because psychological incapacity under Article 36 of the Family Code is an innate mental inability to comprehend and discharge basic marital covenants that is beyond the control of the incapacitated spouse, it is logically contradictory to characterize the same set of acts as both the product of psychological incapacity and a willful, malicious wrong under Article 21, absent specific evidence that the spouse knew of his disability before the marriage and maliciously concealed it.[^178]

### Problem 3: Public Humiliation and Unauthorized Fine Imposed on a Forgetful Shopper

While paying for a cart of groceries at a supermarket, an engineer inadvertently forgot to pay for a small tool worth P3.85 that he had placed in his front shirt pocket with the handle exposed. When a security guard stopped him at the exit, he immediately apologized and turned back to pay the cashier, but the guard compelled him to march past incoming customers to a rear cubicle to sign an incident report later stamped "Shoplifting" and then led him to a public desk near the checkout counters where a store supervisor loudly remarked "Ano, nakaw na naman ito" and confiscated his P5.00 bill as a "fine" to reward guards. In the shopper's suit under Articles 19 and 21, the supermarket argued that it acted within its property rights under Article 429 to apprehend shoplifters. Resolve the supermarket's liability for moral and exemplary damages.

**Suggested Answer:** The supermarket and its supervisor are solidarily liable for moral damages, attorney's fees and the return of the P5.00 fine under Articles 19, 21 and 26 in relation to Article 2219 of the Civil Code, but they are not liable for exemplary damages.[^179] In [*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html), the Supreme Court held that falsely branding a customer a thief in public, uttering defamatory remarks within the hearing of other shoppers and imposing an unauthorized P5.00 fine humiliate and degrade the customer's dignity in a manner contrary to morals, good customs and public policy under Article 21.[^180] However, because the customer's own forgetfulness in failing to check out the item constituted contributory negligence under Article 2214 and the supermarket initially acted in good faith upon probable cause to protect its property under Article 429, the moral damages award must be moderated and exemplary damages under Article 2229 must be eliminated.[^181]

# Quotes

> "This article, adopted to remedy the "countless gaps in the statutes, which leave so many victims of moral wrongs helpless, even though they have actually suffered material and moral injury" [Id.] should "vouchsafe adequate legal remedy for that untold number of moral wrongs which it is impossible for human foresight to provide for specifically in the statutes" [Id. it p. 40; See also PNB v. CA, G.R. No. L-27155, May 18,1978, 83 SCRA 237, 247]."[^182]

> "Article 21 refers to acts contra bonus mores and has the following elements: (1) There is an act which is legal; (2) but which is contrary to morals, good custom, public order, or public policy; and (3) it is done with intent to injure."[^183]

> "Article 21, on the other hand, concerns injuries that may be caused by acts which are not necessarily proscribed by law. This article requires that the act be willful, that is, that there was an intention to do the act and a desire to achieve the outcome. In cases under Article 21, the legal issues revolve around whether such outcome should be considered a legal injury on the part of the plaintiff or whether the commission of the act was done in violation of the standards of care required in Article 19."[^184]

> "In the light of the above laudable purpose of Article 21, We are of the opinion, and so hold, that where a man's promise to marry is in fact the proximate cause of the acceptance of his love by a woman and his representation to fulfill that promise thereafter becomes the proximate cause of the giving of herself unto him in a sexual congress, proof that he had, in reality, no intention of marrying her and that the promise was only a subtle scheme or deceptive device to entice or inveigle her to accept him and to obtain her consent to the sexual act, could justify the award of damages pursuant to Article 21 not because of such promise to marry but because of the fraud and deceit behind it and the willful injury to her honor and reputation which followed thereafter. It is essential, however, that such injury should have been committed in a manner contrary to morals, good customs or public policy."[^185]

> "The Court of Appeals and the trial court considered the acts of the petitioner after the marriage as proof of his psychological incapacity, and therefore a product of his incapacity or inability to comply with the essential obligations of marriage. Nevertheless, said courts considered these acts as willful and hence as grounds for granting moral damages. It is contradictory to characterize acts as a product of psychological incapacity, and hence beyond the control of the party because of an innate inability, while at the same time considering the same set of acts as willful. By declaring the petitioner as psychologically incapacitated, the possibility of awarding moral damages on the same set of facts was negated. The award of moral damages should be predicated, not on the mere act of entering into the marriage, but on specific evidence that it was done deliberately and with malice by a party who had knowledge of his or her disability and yet willfully concealed the same. No such evidence appears to have been adduced in this case."[^186]

> "Although Article 19 merely declares a principle of law, Article 21 gives flesh to its provisions. Thus, we agree with private respondent’s assertion that violations of Articles 19 and 21 are actionable, with judicially enforceable remedies in the municipal forum."[^187]

# Tips

## Part 1: Diagnostic Checklist

> [!TIP]
> **Step-by-step framework for evaluating an Article 21 claim or defense:**
> 1. **Test all three elements of an act *contra bonos mores*.** Verify that (a) the act is not itself a direct violation of a specific statute (or else pair it with Article 20), (b) the manner in which the act was performed offends morals, good customs, public order or public policy and (c) the actor acted willfully with intent to injure ([*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407) and [*Couples for Christ School of the Morning Star v. Malonda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70275)).
> 2. **Check whether the wrong was willful or merely negligent.** If the claimant alleges only carelessness or lack of due diligence without a pre-existing contract or statutory breach and without intent to achieve the injurious result, frame the action under Article 2176 rather than Article 21 ([*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692)).
> 3. **Confirm the plaintiff's own good faith and clean hands.** A claimant whose own fraud and deceit triggered the breakup or dispute is barred from recovering under Article 21 ([*Guevarra v. Banach*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68051)). Conversely, a defendant cannot invoke the clean hands doctrine against a plaintiff whose technical noncompliance was caused by the defendant's own extortion and bad faith ([*Lomarda v. Fudalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66363)).
> 4. **Verify whether free volition was negated or contributory fault applies.** If the defendant's acts have already been declared the product of psychological incapacity under Article 36 of the Family Code, those same acts cannot be treated as willful under Article 21 ([*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809)). If the plaintiff's own forgetfulness triggered the incident while the defendant initially had probable cause under Article 429, invoke Article 2214 to reduce moral damages and delete exemplary damages ([*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html)).

## Part 2: Common Traps and Misconceptions

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 1: Assuming that any broken promise of marriage or premarital sexual relationship is actionable under Article 21.** A broken engagement by itself is not actionable, and sexual relations born of mutual passion or adult voluntariness do not support moral damages. Article 21 applies only when there is extrinsic moral seduction (deceitful promise as the proximate cause of surrender of virtue, as in [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html)) or when a wedding is called off in a manner palpably contrary to good customs against a partner who acted in good faith, as the Supreme Court recalled in [*Guevarra v. Banach*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68051) from *Wassmer v. Velez*.
>
> **Trap 2: Confusing the existence of a contractual or managerial right with immunity for the manner of its exercise.** An employer's right to investigate and dismiss an employee ([*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html)), a principal's right to close a branch ([*Sevilla v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/apr1988/gr_l-41182_1988.html)), a manufacturer's right to compete ([*Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc. v. Spouses Bernardo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62599)) or a utility official's right to require a clearance ([*Lomarda v. Fudalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66363)) does not shield the actor from Article 21 damages when the right is exercised through public humiliation, extortion, self-help padlocking or cut-throat deception.
>
> **Trap 3: Assuming a prior criminal complaint or summary cadastral proceeding bars a separate Article 21 fraud suit.** When the claim rests on fraud, Article 33 allows an independent civil action that requires no reservation in a prior perjury prosecution. Damages arising from the crime itself are deemed instituted with the criminal complaint absent a reservation. The fraud claim need not be pleaded as a compulsory counterclaim in a non-adversarial land-title proceeding where the court lacked jurisdiction ([*Hao v. Lagahid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70070)).
>
> **Trap 4: Believing foreign elements automatically oust Philippine courts of jurisdiction over an Article 21 tort.** Under the "State of the most significant relationship" rule applied in [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36425), Philippine courts retain jurisdiction and apply Articles 19 and 21 where a resident Filipina was deceived in the Philippines and suffered overall injury to her person and reputation centered in the Philippines.

## Part 3: What Worked and What Did Not in Real Cases

- **Suing for moral seduction, bigamous fraud or sexual coercion under Article 21 and Article 2219(10) (Worked).** In [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html), [*Manuel v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42205) and [*Quimiguing v. Icao*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1970/jul1970/gr_26795_1970.html), women who were deceived by a fake promise of marriage, induced into a bigamous marriage by false assurances of single status or forced into carnal relations by a married man successfully recovered moral damages under Article 21 in relation to Article 2219(10) even where criminal seduction did not lie or bigamy was not listed by name in Article 2219.
- **Offsetting temperate damages for loss of business goodwill against unpaid delivery balances (Worked).** In [*Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc. v. Spouses Bernardo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62599), wholesalers whose business was crippled after the manufacturer tricked them into surrendering their customer list and undercut their prices obtained P500,000 in temperate damages under Article 2224 (even though their complaint prayed for actual damages and "other reliefs") and had that award completely offset their P449,154 unpaid delivery debt and accrued interest, alongside moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Articles 21 and 28.
- **Invoking parental discipline or property protection to justify public humiliation or unauthorized fines (Did Not Work).** In [*Spouses Dorao v. Spouses BBB and CCC*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68985) and [*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html), defendants who claimed they were merely advising their son's minor girlfriend or protecting supermarket merchandise were held liable under Articles 21 and 26 because publicly slut-shaming a thirteen-year-old student at school or publicly calling a forgetful customer a thief and confiscating a P5.00 fine degraded human dignity.
- **Claiming moral damages under Article 21 for the same conduct that proved Article 36 psychological incapacity (Did Not Work).** In [*Buenaventura v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42809), a spouse who obtained a declaration of nullity of marriage under Article 36 lost her multi-million-peso moral and exemplary damages awards on appeal because conduct caused by an innate psychological incapacity cannot simultaneously be willful under Article 21.
- **Suing for everyday academic noise or a discreet request to leave an uninvited party based on conjecture (Did Not Work).** In [*Couples for Christ School of the Morning Star v. Malonda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70275) and [*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407), plaintiffs who sued over normal school noise that had been abated within environmental limits or over a hotel secretary's close, polite request for an uninvited guest to leave an intimate party had their complaints dismissed for failure to prove malice or intent to injure.
