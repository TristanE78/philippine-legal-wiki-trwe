> ART. 17. The forms and solemnities of contracts, wills, and other public instruments shall be governed by the laws of the country in which they are executed.
>
> When the acts referred to are executed before the diplomatic or consular officials of the Republic of the Philippines in a foreign country, the solemnities established by Philippine laws shall be observed in their execution.
>
> Prohibitive laws concerning persons, their acts or property, and those which have for their object public order, public policy and good customs shall not be rendered ineffective by laws or judgments promulgated, or by determinations or conventions agreed upon in a foreign country. (11a)

[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 17 establishes three distinct conflict-of-laws rules governing formal validity, consular execution and mandatory Philippine public policy. Paragraph 1 submits the forms and solemnities of contracts, wills and other public instruments to the law of the country where they are executed (*lex loci celebrationis*). Paragraph 2 prescribes Philippine solemnities when those instruments are executed before diplomatic or consular officials of the Republic of the Philippines in a foreign country. Paragraph 3 bars foreign laws, foreign judgments and foreign determinations or conventions from rendering ineffective Philippine prohibitive laws concerning persons, their acts or property or Philippine laws having public order, public policy and good customs as their object.[^civil-code]

**In brief**

1. Identify first whether the dispute concerns extrinsic form under paragraph 1, execution before a Philippine diplomatic or consular official abroad under paragraph 2 or substantive public policy under paragraph 3.
2. Apply the law of the place of execution (*lex loci celebrationis*) only to extrinsic forms and solemnities, keeping subject-matter jurisdiction, personal status and intrinsic validity under their separate governing rules.
3. Refuse to give effect to foreign laws, judgments or contractual stipulations that contravene Philippine prohibitive laws, constitutional mandates or established public policy, unless a specific statutory provision such as Article 16(2) of the Civil Code or Article 26(2) of the Family Code governs.
4. Require the party invoking foreign law or a foreign judgment to plead and prove it as a fact because Philippine courts do not take judicial notice of foreign enactments or decrees.

The sections below examine *lex loci celebrationis* and extrinsic validity under paragraph 1, the scope of consular execution under paragraph 2, the public policy reservation and its statutory limits under paragraph 3, proof of foreign law and the legislative transition from the 1889 Spanish Civil Code, followed by Case Discussions, Related Provisions, Key Cases and Suggested Answers.

## Lex Loci Celebrationis and Extrinsic Validity (Paragraph 1)

Under paragraph 1 of Article 17, the law of the country of execution governs only the extrinsic forms and solemnities of contracts, wills and other public instruments. In [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Rebesencio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60343),[^2] the Supreme Court emphasized that Philippine law is definite under Article 17, paragraph 1 regarding what governs the formal or extrinsic validity of contracts (*lex loci celebrationis*). By contrast, Philippine statutes provide no single rule for the substantive or intrinsic content of contracts, where three modes have emerged in conflicts jurisprudence: *lex loci contractus*, *lex loci solutionis* and *lex loci intentionis*.[^3]

In overseas employment disputes, *lex loci celebrationis* defaults to Philippine substantive labor law when the parties omit an express choice of foreign law in a Philippine-executed contract. In [*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749),[^4] a Filipino safety officer executed his POEA-processed employment contract at his residence in Batangas to work in Madagascar for a Canadian company. Because the contract failed to specify on its face which foreign law governed and was executed in the Philippines, the Supreme Court applied *lex loci celebrationis* so that Philippine labor laws governed the employment relation.[^5]

For wills executed in the Philippines by foreign nationals, Article 17 paragraph 1 operates alongside Articles 816 and 817 of the Civil Code to make Philippine formalities govern by default. In [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229),[^6] an Iriga City trial court *motu proprio* dismissed a petition to probate an American resident's locally executed will on the ground that American national law required prior probate in the United States. Reversing the dismissal, the Supreme Court held that probate tests extrinsic validity rather than intrinsic succession under Article 16(2). Under Article 17, the law of the country of execution governs the forms and solemnities of wills. An alien's will executed in the Philippines may therefore be probated under Philippine formalities by default unless the alien's national law is pleaded and proved. The local probate court keeps subject-matter jurisdiction over the probate and local real property.[^7]

Extrinsic validity under Article 17 also does not strip Philippine courts of subject-matter jurisdiction over marriages celebrated abroad. In [*De Munari v. Asprec*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/apr2025/gr_262831_2025.html),[^8] a trial court dismissed an Italian citizen's complaint to declare his Italian marriage to a Filipino citizen void for bigamy, invoking Article 17 because the ceremony occurred in Italy. Setting aside that dismissal, the Supreme Court explained that Article 17 expresses *lex loci celebrationis* for forms and solemnities, which Family Code Article 26(1) reflects for marriages. Intrinsic validity and bigamy are distinct matters governed by Family Code Article 35(4) and Civil Code Article 15 for Filipino citizens. Family Courts, or Regional Trial Courts where no Family Court sits, retain exclusive original jurisdiction under Republic Act No. 8369 over complaints for declaration of nullity of marriage.[^9]

Where a contract affects real property, *lex loci celebrationis* under Article 17 also stands distinct from *lex rei sitae* and Philippine consensual contract rules. In [*Republic v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_108292_1993.html),[^10] the Supreme Court noted that while *lex loci celebrationis* generally governs contract forms and solemnities under Article 17, *lex rei sitae* generally governs formalities for the acquisition, encumbrance and alienation of real and personal property. Under Philippine substantive law in Articles 1356 and 1358(a) of the Civil Code, contracts are obligatory in whatever form if their essential requisites exist. Reducing a transfer of real rights to a public document is merely for greater efficacy or convenience.[^11]

## Execution Before Philippine Diplomatic or Consular Officials (Paragraph 2)

Paragraph 2 of Article 17 requires the solemnities established by Philippine laws to be observed whenever contracts, wills or other public instruments are executed before diplomatic or consular officials of the Republic of the Philippines in a foreign country.[^civil-code] None of the decisions cited here affirmatively upholds or invalidates a will, contract or public instrument executed before a Philippine diplomatic or consular official under paragraph 2.

The sole ruling touching consular authentication is [*Republic v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_108292_1993.html).[^12] In that case, the Presidential Commission on Good Government contended that its 1990 compromise agreement with Roberto S. Benedicto was defective because it had not been authenticated before consular officials abroad and lacked witnesses. Rejecting that challenge alongside Article 17, the Supreme Court held that under Articles 1356 and 1358(a) contracts remain obligatory regardless of form and that lack of authentication before consular officers abroad or lack of witnesses did not invalidate the compromise.[^13]

## Prohibitive Laws and Public Policy Reservation (Paragraph 3)

Paragraph 3 of Article 17 protects Philippine prohibitive laws and rules of public order, public policy and good customs against displacement by foreign laws, foreign judgments or foreign stipulations.[^civil-code] Supreme Court decisions apply this reservation across overseas employment, procedural policy against claim-splitting and foreign divorces, while enforcing Congress's statutory boundaries in succession and mixed marriages.

### Overseas Employment Contracts and Labor Protection

Philippine labor and social legislation follow Filipino workers overseas as an expression of public policy under Article 17, paragraph 3. In [*Royal Crown Internationale v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/oct1989/gr_78085_1989.html),[^14] a recruitment agency argued that Clause 13 of a Saudi deployment contract designating Saudi Arabian law governed the validity of an employee's termination. Calling that contention patently erroneous, the Supreme Court held that all Filipino workers employed locally or overseas enjoy Philippine labor and social legislation regardless of contrary contract stipulations. That rule implements Article 3 of the Labor Code and Sections 18, Article II and 3, Article XIII of the 1987 Constitution and is rendered imperative by Article 17 of the Civil Code.[^15]

The Supreme Court applied the same Article 17 mandate even when a Filipino worker was hired abroad rather than recruited in Manila. In [*Philippine National Bank v. Cabansag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43423),[^16] a Filipino tourist applied directly with PNB's Singapore branch, secured Manila head-office approval, obtained a Singapore Employment Pass and later secured POEA certification. Quoting [*Royal Crown Internationale*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/oct1989/gr_78085_1989.html),[^17] the Supreme Court held that a local immigration work pass does not waive Philippine labor laws and that PNB could not rely on Singaporean law, which it had not proved, to defeat liability for illegal dismissal.[^18] In [*Sim v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44910),[^19] the Supreme Court likewise quoted [*Cabansag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43423)[^20] on Article 17 to reject a Labor Arbiter's ruling that Philippine labor law lacked extraterritorial jurisdiction over an Italian-law bank assignment, confirming Labor Arbiter jurisdiction under Labor Code Article 217 and Republic Act No. 8042 Section 10 even though it denied relief because the worker failed to move for NLRC reconsideration and substantial evidence supported her dismissal.[^21]

Building on those precedents, [*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749)[^22] synthesized four cumulative requisites before a foreign law may govern an overseas employment contract:

1. The overseas employment contract must expressly stipulate that a specific foreign law shall govern.
2. The foreign law invoked must be proved before Philippine courts under Philippine rules on evidence.
3. The stipulated foreign law must not be contrary to Philippine law, morals, good customs, public order or public policy.
4. The overseas employment contract must be processed through the POEA.[^23]

The Supreme Court anchored the third requisite directly on Article 17 of the Civil Code and Article 1306 on contractual stipulations.[^24] Although the employer in [*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749)[^25] submitted an authenticated copy of Ontario's Employment Standards Act, the foreign statute failed the third requisite because it allowed dismissal without just cause and dispensed with prior notice through severance pay, violating Philippine constitutional security of tenure and due process.[^26] In [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Rebesencio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60343),[^27] the Supreme Court, citing only paragraph 1 of Article 17, similarly refused to enforce Saudi choice-of-law clauses or decline jurisdiction under *forum non conveniens* where the employer forced pregnant Filipino flight attendants to resign under its Unified Contract, holding that contractual autonomy yields to Article II, Section 14 of the 1987 Constitution and the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women.[^28]

Article 17, paragraph 3 also reinforces tortious and statutory accountability for recruitment agencies and foreign employers toward distressed overseas workers. In [*Becmen Service Exporter and Promotion, Inc. v. Cuaresma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49057),[^29] a Filipino nurse died violently in Saudi Arabia, yet her employer and recruitment agencies insisted she committed suicide and refused to investigate or aid her parents. Relying on Civil Code Articles 19, 21, 24 and 2219(10), Republic Act No. 8042 and Article 17, the Supreme Court held the foreign principal, recruitment agencies and their corporate directors and officers solidarily liable for moral and exemplary damages because abandoning a distressed worker violates good customs and public policy.[^30] In [*Jerzon Manpower and Trading, Inc. v. Nato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67875),[^31] an overseas machine operator in Taiwan developed end-stage kidney disease, was ignored, abruptly repatriated and forcibly ejected from the recruiter's office. Quoting [*Becmen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49057)[^32] and [*Cabansag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43423),[^33] the Supreme Court held the recruiters, foreign principal and corporate officer solidarily liable for unexpired pay, moral and exemplary damages and PhilHealth-based financial assistance under Republic Act No. 7875.[^34]

### Prohibition Against Splitting a Single Cause of Action

Article 17, paragraph 3 prevents foreign law from authorizing procedural remedies prohibited by Philippine public policy. In [*Bank of America, NT and SA v. American Realty Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37085),[^35] a foreign bank filed four civil collection suits in English and Hong Kong courts against Panamanian borrowers before extrajudicially foreclosing a third-party mortgagor's Bulacan lands. The bank argued that English law allowed a mortgagee to pursue collection suits without waiving its mortgage security. Invoking Article 17, paragraph 3 verbatim, the Supreme Court held that even if English law were proved, Philippine law refused to apply a foreign rule that violated Philippine public policy against splitting a single cause of action under Rule 2, Section 4 of the 1997 Rules of Civil Procedure and worked undeniable injustice to the Philippine mortgagor.[^36]

### Foreign Divorces and the Family Code Article 26(2) Exception

Outside mixed marriages, Article 17, paragraph 3 reinforces Article 15 in barring recognition of absolute divorces between Filipino citizens. In [*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282),[^37] the Supreme Court explained that Philippine law does not recognize divorce between Filipinos and that Article 17 provides that the policy against absolute divorces cannot be subverted by judgments promulgated in a foreign country. The second paragraph of Article 26 of the Family Code provides the direct statutory exception to this rule so that a Filipino married to an alien spouse who obtains a valid foreign divorce is capacitated to remarry. Only the Filipino spouse holds that substantive right under Article 26(2), though an alien spouse retains legal interest under Rule 39, Section 48(b) to petition for judicial recognition of the foreign judgment as presumptive evidence of a right.[^38]

Synthesizing this interplay in [*Morisono v. Morisono*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64412),[^39] the Supreme Court stated four prevailing rules on divorce. First, Philippine laws do not provide for absolute divorce. Second, consistent with Articles 15 and 17 of the Civil Code, the marital bond between two Filipino citizens cannot be dissolved even by a foreign absolute divorce. Third, an absolute divorce obtained abroad by two aliens may be recognized if consistent with their respective national laws. Fourth, in mixed marriages involving a Filipino and a foreigner, the Filipino is allowed to remarry when a valid absolute divorce is obtained abroad capacitating the alien spouse to remarry. Quoting [*Corpuz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282)[^40] on Article 26(2) and applying *Republic v. Manalo*, as cited in [*Morisono*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64412), the Supreme Court held that Article 26(2) covers mixed-marriage divorces even when jointly initiated by the spouses through a Japanese "Divorce by Agreement".[^41]

### Boundary With Succession Under Article 16(2)

Article 17, paragraph 3 does not override specific conflict-of-laws rules enacted elsewhere in the Civil Code. In [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html),[^42] two illegitimate daughters of a Texas citizen and domiciliary opposed the distribution of his Philippine estate, arguing that Article 17, paragraph 3 required enforcing Philippine compulsory legitimes as a matter of public policy. Rejecting that argument, the Supreme Court held that Article 16, paragraph 2 and Article 1039 form a specific provision making the decedent's national law govern the order of succession, the amount of successional rights, the intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions and capacity to succeed. Because specific provisions prevail over general ones, Article 17, paragraph 3 did not extend Philippine legitimes to a foreign national's succession, even assuming the testator meant his Philippine will to follow Philippine law.[^43]

## Proof of Foreign Law and Processual Presumption

Across all three paragraphs of Article 17, Philippine courts do not take judicial notice of foreign laws or foreign judgments. A party relying on foreign formalities, foreign divorce law or foreign employment rules must plead and prove them as facts under Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25 and Rule 39, Section 48(b) of the Rules of Court.

When foreign law is unproved, Philippine law governs either by default or by processual presumption:

- In [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229),[^44] Philippine formalities governed the probate of an American citizen's Philippine-executed will by default unless foreign national law was pleaded and proved.
- In [*Royal Crown Internationale v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/oct1989/gr_78085_1989.html),[^45] the employer neither pleaded nor proved Saudi Arabian law, leaving Philippine labor statutes applicable alongside Article 17.
- In [*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749),[^46] the Supreme Court restated that failure to prove foreign law under Rule 132 activates processual presumption, presuming foreign law to be the same as Philippine law, though even an authenticated Ontario statute failed Requisites 1 and 3.
- In [*Jerzon Manpower and Trading, Inc. v. Nato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67875),[^47] the worker's failure to prove Taiwan health insurance laws constrained the Supreme Court to apply Philippine health coverage under Republic Act No. 7875 in light of Article 17.
- In [*Bank of America, NT and SA v. American Realty Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37085),[^48] the Supreme Court noted processual presumption under Rule 132, Section 24 and added that even assuming English law had been proved, Article 17, paragraph 3 barred its application for contravening Philippine public policy.
- In [*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282),[^49] both the foreign divorce decree and the alien spouse's national law had to be proved in judicial recognition proceedings. Administrative registration by a local civil registrar without a court order was void.
- In [*Morisono v. Morisono*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64412),[^50] the Supreme Court remanded the case because the Filipino spouse still had to prove the Japanese divorce and prevailing Japanese divorce law.

## Legislative Genesis From the 1889 Civil Code

Article 17 of Republic Act No. 386 is derived from Article 11 of the 1889 Spanish Civil Code.[^civil-code] In [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html),[^51] the Supreme Court examined the textual change made by Congress to determine the boundary between Article 16 and Article 17. When incorporating Article 11 of the old Civil Code as Article 17 of the New Civil Code, Congress deleted the saving phrase *"notwithstanding the provisions of this and the next preceding article"*, reproduced the second paragraph of old Article 10 without substantial change as Article 16 and added a new rule in Article 1039 governing capacity to succeed. That deletion confirmed Congress's intent that Article 16, paragraph 2 should operate as a specific provision unaffected by the general public-policy reservation of Article 17, paragraph 3.[^52]

## Case Discussions

### [*De Munari v. Asprec*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/apr2025/gr_262831_2025.html): Lex Loci Celebrationis Does Not Strip Nullity Jurisdiction

Gianni de Munari, an Italian citizen residing in the Philippines, married Filipino citizen Thelma Asprec in Italy on December 10, 2011. She registered the marriage at the Philippine Consulate General in Milan on December 29, 2011. On July 17, 2022, Gianni sued before Branch 47, Regional Trial Court of Puerto Princesa City to declare their marriage void for bigamy, alleging she had two prior subsisting marriages from September 2, 1979 and August 12, 1991 with still-living spouses.[^53] On August 15, 2022, the trial court set aside its earlier August 2, 2022 order and dismissed the complaint for lack of jurisdiction under Civil Code Article 17 because the marriage occurred in Italy. On August 24, 2022, it denied reconsideration while stating Gianni's national law governed and PSA cancellation belonged under Rule 108. It added that the outcome would differ if Thelma were the petitioner.[^54]

The Supreme Court granted Gianni's direct Rule 45 petition, reversed the August 15, 2022 and August 24, 2022 Orders of Branch 47, Regional Trial Court of Puerto Princesa City and remanded the case for further proceedings with utmost dispatch.[^55] It held that Family Courts, or Regional Trial Courts where none exist, have exclusive jurisdiction under Sections 3 and 5 of Republic Act No. 8369 over nullity complaints. Article 17 states *lex loci celebrationis* for the forms and solemnities of acts, which Family Code Article 26(1) mirrors for the extrinsic validity of foreign marriages, whereas bigamy concerns intrinsic validity under Family Code Article 35(4) and binds Filipinos anywhere under Civil Code Article 15.[^56] Gianni also possessed legal standing as an alleged victim of a bigamous union affecting his civil status. The Supreme Court cited Section 2(a) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, which does not distinguish between Filipino and foreign petitioners. It remanded because it is not a trier of facts. The case shows that Article 17 is a choice-of-law rule on extrinsic form and never a ground to dismiss a nullity suit for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.[^57]

### [*Jerzon Manpower and Trading, Inc. v. Nato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67875): Philippine Standards for a Distressed Worker's Claims

On May 22, 2008, Jerzon Manpower and Trading, Inc., on behalf of United Taiwan Corp., hired Emmanuel B. Nato as a machine operator in Taiwan for one year, seven months and seven days at NT$17,280.00 monthly, deploying him on June 8, 2008. After a year of hot factory exposure, his complaints of stomach pain were ignored and he developed end-stage chronic kidney disease, leading to his abrupt repatriation, denial of medical or airport aid and forcible ejection from Jerzon's office before his death on February 10, 2013.[^58] On September 14, 2012, the Labor Arbiter awarded three months' unpaid salaries plus P1,000,000.00 financial assistance. On May 2, 2013, the National Labor Relations Commission found a valid dismissal for inability to work and reduced financial assistance to P100,000.00, adding P30,000.00 nominal damages on January 30, 2014. On October 26, 2016, the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 134814 reversed the commission and reinstated the Labor Arbiter's decision, denying reconsideration on January 13, 2017.[^59]

The Supreme Court held that Rule 65 was the wrong remedy but relaxed the rule because the P1,000,000.00 award was an oppressive exercise of judicial authority. It partially granted the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals Decision and Resolution with modification. It ordered Jerzon Manpower and Trading, Inc., United Taiwan Corporation and Clifford Uy Tuazon jointly and solidarily to pay Emmanuel B. Nato's heirs the Philippine peso equivalent of NT$102,528.00 for his unexpired contract, P200,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages, P500,000.00 financial assistance (reduced from P1,000,000.00), 10% attorney's fees on the total award and 6% annual legal interest from finality until full payment. It also denied Nato's claimed P85,000.00 processing fee because no evidence on record substantiated payment.[^60] Nato failed to prove Taiwan health insurance laws, so the Supreme Court applied the processual presumption and the Philippine PhilHealth standards under Republic Act No. 7875 alongside Republic Act No. 8042. The PhilHealth dialysis package pays P4,000.00 per session for up to 45 sessions a year. The Supreme Court struck down the three-month cap on unexpired pay under *Sameer Overseas* and *Serrano*, so the pay covers July 18, 2009 to January 15, 2010. Jerzon did not prove that Nato resigned voluntarily. The Supreme Court quoted [*Becmen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49057) on Article 17 and on Civil Code Articles 19, 21, 24 and 2219(10). The case shows that Philippine labor and social standards govern a distressed worker's claims when foreign benefit laws are unproved.[^61]

### [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229): Extrinsic Validity of an Alien's Locally Executed Will

Luz Gaspe Lipson, a Filipina later naturalized as an American citizen and temporarily residing in Iriga City, executed a will in Iriga City on February 23, 2011 covering real property there and naming Roel P. Gaspi executor. After she died of lymphoma on October 17, 2015 at age 70, Gaspi petitioned for probate and letters testamentary on October 3, 2016.[^62] Branch 36 of the Regional Trial Court of Iriga City *motu proprio* dismissed the petition without prejudice on October 6, 2016 for lack of jurisdiction, ruling that American law required prior probate in the United States. It denied reconsideration on November 16, 2016. It distinguished *Palaganas* because that will was executed abroad.[^63]

The Supreme Court granted the Rule 45 petition, reversed and set aside the assailed Regional Trial Court orders in Spec. Proc. No. IR-2919 and remanded the case for further proceedings to check formal compliance, testamentary capacity and voluntary execution.[^64] Under Article 17, paragraph 1, forms and solemnities of wills pertain to extrinsic validity governed by *lex loci celebrationis*. Read with Articles 816 and 817 of the Civil Code and Rule 73, Section 1 of the Rules of Court, an alien's will made in the Philippines follows Philippine formalities by default unless foreign law is pleaded and proved and need not undergo prior foreign probate. The Supreme Court cited *Johannes v. Harvey* for the rule that a foreigner's will made in the Philippines may be probated here when it covers local estate. No one had objected to jurisdiction, so the trial court gravely abused its discretion. The case shows that Article 17 applies Philippine formal law by default to an alien's local will without stripping Philippine probate jurisdiction.[^65]

### [*Morisono v. Morisono*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64412): Four Divorce Rules Under Articles 15 and 17

Filipino citizen Luzviminda Dela Cruz married Japanese national Ryoji Morisono in Quezon City on December 8, 2009 and lived with him in Japan for one year and three months. After marital quarrels, both spouses filed a "Divorce by Agreement" in Nagoya City approved on January 17, 2012 and recorded on July 1, 2012, leading Luzviminda to petition Branch 105, Regional Trial Court of Quezon City in SP. PROC. NO. Q-12-71830 for recognition. She also sought to cancel her ex-husband's surname in her passport and to remarry. The court received her evidence ex parte.[^66] On July 18, 2016, the trial court denied the petition under Civil Code Article 15 in relation to Family Code Article 26(2) solely because she had initiated the divorce.[^67]

The Supreme Court partly granted the Rule 45 petition, reversed and set aside the July 18, 2016 trial court Decision and remanded the case for further proceedings.[^68] Citing *Republic v. Manalo*, it summarized four divorce rules. The second rule states that consistent with Civil Code Articles 15 and 17 the marital bond between two Filipinos cannot be dissolved even by a foreign absolute divorce. It also quoted [*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282)[^69] on the substantive right that Article 26(2) gives the Filipino spouse. That passage says no declaration is needed for the alien spouse. The Supreme Court held that *Manalo* made the trial court's sole ground nugatory. It took the elements of Article 26(2) from *Orbecido*. It ruled that Family Code Article 26(2) provides a direct exception even for a joint mixed-marriage divorce, though Luzviminda still had to prove the Japanese decree and Japanese law on remand. The case restates the divorce rules that rest on Articles 15 and 17 and applies Family Code Article 26(2) to a joint divorce in a mixed marriage.[^70]

### [*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749): Four Requisites and Article 17 Public Policy Limit

In May 2008, Canadian firm SNC-Lavalin, through local agency IPAMS and its president Angelito C. Hernandez, offered licensed general surgeon Alberto B. Arriola a 19-month Safety Officer post in Madagascar at CA$32.00 per hour for 40 hours weekly. Arriola signed the POEA-processed contract in Batangas, started work on June 9, 2008, received a pre-termination notice on September 9, 2009 effective September 11, 2009 for diminishing workload and was repatriated on September 15, 2009 with CA$2,636.80 paid under Canadian law, prompting an illegal dismissal claim for P1,062,936.00.[^71] On May 31, 2010, the Labor Arbiter dismissed the complaint under Ontario's Employment Standards Act. On November 30, 2010, the National Labor Relations Commission reversed and awarded CA$81,920.00 (later adjusted in execution to CA$26,880.00 for three months and three weeks), denying reconsideration on February 2, 2011. On January 24, 2013, the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 118869 modified the award to CA$19,200.00 based on a 40-hour workweek.[^72]

The Supreme Court denied the Rule 45 petition and affirmed the January 24, 2013 Court of Appeals Decision *in toto*.[^73] It announced four cumulative requisites for applying foreign law to an overseas employment contract, grounding the third requisite on Civil Code Articles 17 and 1306. The Supreme Court found requisites 2 and 4 satisfied because the Ontario statute was authenticated and POEA processed the contract. Requisites 1 and 3 failed. The contract lacked an express stipulation naming Canadian law, leaving Philippine law applicable under *lex loci celebrationis*. Ontario's Employment Standards Act also violated Philippine security of tenure and due process by allowing termination without just cause or prior notice, while petitioners failed to prove any authorized cause for dismissal. The Financial Post article offered to show a business downturn was hearsay. The Supreme Court cited *Pakistan International Airlines v. Ople*, *EDI-Staffbuilders*, *Sameer Overseas* and *PCL Shipping*. It left the CA$2,636.80 already paid to be accounted for in execution. The case shows how Article 17 operates both as the default rule of *lex loci celebrationis* and as the public-policy barrier invalidating oppressive foreign employment statutes.[^74]

### [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Rebesencio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60343): Extrinsic Form vs. Substantive Choice of Law and Public Policy

Four Filipino permanent flight attendants hired between 1990 and 1995 under POEA-approved Cabin Attendant contracts applied for maternity leaves in 2006. Saudi Arabian Airlines (Saudia) disapproved their leaves under a Unified Contract that respondents dated September 23, 2006 and that voided employment upon pregnancy, threatened termination with benefit forfeiture and pressured them into submitting handwritten resignation letters before they sued on November 8, 2007.[^75] The Executive Labor Arbiter dismissed the complaint on December 12, 2008, but on November 19, 2009 the National Labor Relations Commission vacated that dismissal and awarded SR614,001.24 in backwages and separation pay plus 10% attorney's fees (denying reconsideration on February 11, 2010). On June 16, 2011, the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP. No. 113006 modified the computation to run up to finality plus 10% attorney's fees and denied reconsideration on September 13, 2011.[^76]

The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals Decision and Resolution with modifications: it cleared petitioner Brenda J. Betia of solidary liability and held Saudia liable for P100,000.00 moral damages and P200,000.00 exemplary damages per respondent, alongside full backwages until finality, separation pay at one month per year of service until finality (at least six months counted as one whole year), 10% attorney's fees and 6% annual interest from finality until full satisfaction, remanding to the Labor Arbiter for computation.[^77] Distinguishing Article 17, paragraph 1 (*lex loci celebrationis* for contract forms) from substantive choice-of-law rules (*lex loci contractus*, *lex loci solutionis* and *lex loci intentionis*), the Supreme Court rejected *forum non conveniens* because it must be pleaded at the earliest opportunity and backed by a prior suit elsewhere. It held that contractual choice of Saudi law could not defeat constitutional gender equality under Article II, Section 14 and CEDAW. The Supreme Court cited only paragraph 1 of Article 17. It found constructive dismissal because the resignation letters were on letterheads that Saudia supplied. It struck down the quitclaims under *Paramio*. Betia was cleared because no bad faith was shown. The case separates Article 17(1) formal rules from public-policy limits on contractual choice of law.[^78]

### [*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282): Article 17 Divorce Bar and Alien Standing Under Rule 39

Gerbert R. Corpuz, a former Filipino naturalized as a Canadian citizen on November 29, 2000, married Filipina Daisylyn T. Sto. Tomas in Pasig City on January 18, 2005 and later obtained an Ontario divorce decree on December 8, 2005 (effective January 8, 2006). After the Pasig civil registry annotated the decree, the National Statistics Office advised him that judicial recognition was required, leading him to petition Branch 11, Regional Trial Court of Laoag City, which denied his petition on October 30, 2008 and February 17, 2009 on the ground that only the Filipino spouse could invoke Family Code Article 26(2). Daisylyn did not oppose the petition.[^79]

The Supreme Court granted the Rule 45 petition, reversed the Regional Trial Court decision and order and remanded the case for further proceedings without costs, furnishing the Civil Registrar General a copy.[^80] It explained that Article 17 bars subverting Philippine policy against absolute divorces through foreign judgments and that Family Code Article 26(2) creates a direct statutory exception solely for the Filipino spouse. Even so, an alien spouse retains legal interest under Rule 39, Section 48(b) to petition for judicial recognition by proving the foreign decree and foreign national law as facts, whereas unjudicially registered decrees in the civil registry are void. The Supreme Court treated a foreign divorce decree as presumptive evidence of a right under Rule 39, Section 48(b). Gerbert did not attach the Canadian law, so it remanded for proof. It also held that cancelling the registry annotation requires a Rule 108 proceeding under Civil Code Article 412. Recognition may be made within that proceeding. The case defines Article 17's relationship with Family Code Article 26(2) and civil-registry proof requirements.[^81]

### [*Becmen Service Exporter and Promotion, Inc. v. Cuaresma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49057): Public Policy Damages for Distressed Workers

Deployed on January 6, 1997 by Becmen Service Exporter and Promotion, Inc. as an assistant nurse in Al-Birk Hospital in Saudi Arabia at US$247.00 monthly, Jasmin Cuaresma was found dead in her dormitory on June 21, 1998. Saudi reports attributed death to poisoning, whereas Cabanatuan and NBI autopsy reports showed violent physical trauma and no poison. Her parents received P130,000.00 from OWWA and sued Becmen and its Saudi principal Rajab on November 22, 1999. White Falcon Services, Inc. was later impleaded as substitute recruiter.[^82] The Labor Arbiter dismissed the complaint on February 28, 2001, the National Labor Relations Commission reversed on November 22, 2002 (reducing actual damages on October 9, 2003 from US$113,000.00 to US$80,000.00) and the Court of Appeals in its May 14, 2008 Amended Decision in CA-G.R. SP Nos. 80619 and 81030 further reduced the award to US$4,686.73 (19 months' unpaid salary) with legal interest.[^83]

The Supreme Court set aside the May 14, 2008 Amended Decision of the Court of Appeals and found Rajab & Silsilah Company, White Falcon Services, Inc., Becmen Service Exporter and Promotion, Inc. and their corporate directors and officers jointly and solidarily liable to indemnify Jasmin's parents P2,500,000.00 moral damages, P2,500,000.00 exemplary damages, 10% attorney's fees and costs of suit.[^84] The Supreme Court held that the death was not work-connected or compensable. Her contract had no insurance or death benefits and the reference to iqama insurance was a typographical error. It therefore set aside the US$4,686.73 award. The Saudi police report was inconclusive. The autopsy found a rib fracture and lacerations and the NBI toxicology report was negative. White Falcon's assumption of liability did not free Becmen. Finding that Jasmin died of murderous aggression rather than suicide, the Supreme Court held that abandoning her investigation and insisting on suicide violated Republic Act No. 8042 and Civil Code Articles 19, 21, 24 and 2219(10), rendered imperative by Article 17 of the Civil Code. The case shows that the Supreme Court tied Philippine public-policy remedies for distressed overseas workers to Article 17.[^85]

### [*Sim v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44910): Jurisdiction Under Article 17 vs. Procedural Denial

Corazon C. Sim alleged she was hired by Equitable PCI-Bank in 1990 for its Frankfurt Representative Office and promoted to Manager before being dismissed in September 1999 for alleged mismanagement and fund misappropriation. On September 3, 2001, the Labor Arbiter dismissed her complaint for want of jurisdiction (reasoning Philippine labor law had no extraterritorial reach over an allegedly Italian-law contract) and lack of merit. The bank denied any employer-employee relationship. The National Labor Relations Commission affirmed.[^86] Without moving for reconsideration before the commission, Sim filed a Rule 65 petition with the Court of Appeals, which dismissed it on October 29, 2002 and denied reconsideration on February 26, 2003.[^87]

The Supreme Court denied the Rule 45 petition with costs against petitioner.[^88] It corrected the Labor Arbiter by holding under Labor Code Article 217 and Republic Act No. 8042 Section 10 that Labor Arbiters hold original and exclusive jurisdiction over overseas worker disputes and quoted [*Cabansag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43423)[^89] on Article 17. Nevertheless, it sustained dismissal because Sim bypassed a mandatory NLRC motion for reconsideration and substantial evidence supported her dismissal for loss of trust and confidence. The Supreme Court held that the case involved mixed questions of fact and law, so no exception to the motion-for-reconsideration rule applied. Sim admitted withdrawing 3,000,000 lire, which she said was for the Radio Pilipinas sa Roma program. The case shows that Philippine labor jurisdiction over overseas workers does not excuse fatal procedural defaults or defeat substantiated just causes.[^90]

### [*Philippine National Bank v. Cabansag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43423): Direct-Hired Overseas Worker Covered by Article 17

While a tourist in Singapore in late 1998, Florence O. Cabansag applied with PNB's Singapore branch, obtained Manila head-office approval and a Singapore Employment Pass, received a December 7, 1998 appointment at SG$4,500.00 monthly and obtained a POEA Overseas Employment Certificate on March 8, 1999 before being pressured to resign and dismissed in April 1999.[^91] On January 18, 2000, the Labor Arbiter found illegal dismissal and awarded reinstatement, SGD 40,500.00 backwages, bonuses, allowances and leave credits (SGD 2,250.00, SGD 120.00, SGD 1,557.67, SGD 1,150.60, SGD 319.85 and SGD 4,500.00), SGD 1,978.00 actual damages, P200,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages and SGD 5,039.81 attorney's fees. On June 29, 2001, the National Labor Relations Commission affirmed while reducing moral damages to P100,000.00 and exemplary damages to P50,000.00. The Court of Appeals in CA-GR SP No. 68403 affirmed on July 16, 2002 and January 29, 2003.[^92]

The Supreme Court denied PNB's Rule 45 petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals Decision and Resolution with costs against petitioner.[^93] Rejecting PNB's claim that Singaporean law applied, it held that a Singapore work pass did not waive Philippine labor protections, that Manila approval and POEA certification brought Cabansag within migrant-worker protection and quoted [*Royal Crown Internationale*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/oct1989/gr_78085_1989.html)[^94] on Article 17 of the Civil Code. PNB did not prove Singapore law. Cabansag could file where she resides or at the employer's principal office. She filed in Quezon City. PNB paid one month in lieu of notice but gave no notice or hearing and showed no just cause. Moral and exemplary damages were justified. The case shows that a local hire with Manila approval and POEA certification keeps Philippine labor protections.[^95]

### [*Bank of America, NT and SA v. American Realty Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37085): Foreign Law Yielding to Rule Against Claim-Splitting

Bank of America, NT and SA (BANTSA) and Bank of America International Limited loaned multi-million-dollar sums to three Panamanian corporations, secured by two real estate mortgages executed in 1983 and 1984 by third-party mortgagor American Realty Corporation (ARC) over Bulacan lands. After filing four civil collection actions in England and Hong Kong in 1992 against the borrowers without impleading ARC, BANTSA applied on December 16, 1992 to foreclose ARC's Bulacan mortgages extrajudicially. The January 22, 1993 auction sold the lands to ICCS for P24,000,000.00. ICCS resold them to Stateland on March 18, 1994 for P39,000,000.00.[^96] On May 12, 1993, Branch 159 of the Pasig Regional Trial Court held the foreign collection suits waived foreclosure and awarded ARC P99,000,000.00 actual damages, P5,000,000.00 exemplary damages and costs, which the Court of Appeals affirmed in CA G.R. CV No. 51094 on September 30, 1997 and May 22, 1998.[^97]

The Supreme Court denied BANTSA's Rule 45 petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision with modification reducing exemplary damages to P50,000.00, ordering BANTSA to pay P99,000,000.00 actual or compensatory damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and costs of suit.[^98] Rejecting BANTSA's reliance on English law allowing concurrent collection and foreclosure, the Supreme Court quoted Article 17, paragraph 3 verbatim and held that even if English law were proved under Rule 132, Section 24, it could not apply because it contravened Philippine public policy against splitting a single cause of action under Rule 2, Section 4 and worked undeniable injustice. The Supreme Court held that merely filing the collection suit waived foreclosure. The remedies are alternative and the cause of action is single. A suit in a foreign court counts. *Bachrach* remains good law and *Caltex* is an exception. The case demonstrates Article 17(3)'s force against foreign commercial laws that impair mandatory Philippine procedural policies.[^99]

### [*Republic v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_108292_1993.html): Consular Authentication and Contract Formality

Following prior settlements in the United States and Switzerland, the Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG) executed a November 3, 1990 compromise agreement with Roberto S. Benedicto ceding Annex A and B properties, lifting Annex C sequestrations and extending immunity. On October 2, 1992, the Sandiganbayan approved the compromise, leading PCGG and belatedly intervening sugar planters and millers (on November 13, 1992) to seek nullification in consolidated petitions. The Sandiganbayan denied the motion to intervene.[^100]

The Supreme Court dismissed the petitions in G.R. Nos. 108292, 108368, 108548-49 and 108550, lifted the March 1993 restraining orders and ordered strict compliance with the compromise agreement.[^101] Among other rulings, it rejected PCGG's contention that the compromise was defective for lacking authentication before consular officials abroad and witnesses. It called that contention puerile. It also rejected the lack of participation of the Solicitor General. The PCGG could not repudiate the agreement while keeping the property it received. The intervenors could protect their rights in a separate proceeding. Explaining that *lex loci celebrationis* under Article 17 generally governs contract forms while *lex rei sitae* governs property-transfer formalities, it applied Articles 1356 and 1358(a) to hold that contracts are obligatory in whatever form and public-document requirements for immovables serve only convenience. The case distinguishes Article 17 form rules from Philippine consensual validity where consular authentication is absent.[^102]

### [*Royal Crown Internationale v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/oct1989/gr_78085_1989.html): Foundational Application of Article 17 to Foreign Labor Choice of Law

In 1983, licensed agency Royal Crown Internationale deployed architectural draftsman Virgilio P. Nacionales to Saudi Arabia under a May 25, 1983 service agreement with ZAMEL for one year at US$500.00 monthly salary plus US$100.00 allowance, containing Clause 13 designating Saudi Arabian law. Dismissed on February 13, 1984 for alleged poor performance and repatriated on February 16, 1984, Nacionales sued on April 23, 1984. He had been detained at his quarters for three days after his dismissal.[^103] On June 23, 1986, the POEA ordered Royal Crown and ZAMEL jointly and severally to pay US$2,640.00 unexpired salaries, US$600.00 less SR558 vacation pay, US$350.00 travel fund reimbursement and 10% attorney's fees while dismissing legal and transportation expenses. The National Labor Relations Commission affirmed on December 11, 1986 and March 30, 1987.[^104]

Treating the Rule 45 petition as a Rule 65 petition for certiorari, the Supreme Court dismissed the petition.[^105] The Supreme Court first held Royal Crown solidarily liable with ZAMEL because its undertakings to the Bureau of Employment Services made it answerable. Service on Royal Crown as ZAMEL's agent was service on ZAMEL. Royal Crown's letter and counter-affidavit named no particular acts. The telex message from ZAMEL employees was not relevant. The receipt did not prove payment of the unexpired salary. Finding that Royal Crown failed to substantiate its charges and noting that Nacionales disputed the quitclaim as signed under threat of deportation, the Supreme Court squarely rejected Clause 13's Saudi law stipulation as patently erroneous. It held that all Filipino workers locally or overseas enjoy Philippine labor and social legislation, contract stipulations to the contrary notwithstanding, rendered imperative by Article 17 alongside Labor Code Article 3 and the 1987 Constitution. It also noted that Saudi law was neither pleaded nor proved. The decision cites Article 17 as added support for that rule.[^106]

### [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html): Specific Scope of Article 16(2) Over Article 17(3)

Texas citizen and domiciliary Amos G. Bellis executed a will in the Philippines on August 5, 1952 bequeathing $240,000.00 to his divorced first wife, P120,000.00 (P40,000.00 each) to three illegitimate children and the residue to seven legitimate children. After his death on July 8, 1958 and probate in Manila on September 15, 1958, two illegitimate daughters opposed the January 8, 1964 project of partition on January 17, 1964 while Amos Bellis, Jr. did not oppose, claiming legitimes under Civil Code Article 17, paragraph 3. On April 30, 1964, the Court of First Instance of Manila overruled their oppositions under Article 16 because Texas law recognized no legitimes, denying reconsideration on June 11, 1964.[^107]

The Supreme Court affirmed the probate court order *in toto* with costs against appellants.[^108] Noting that the parties never invoked *renvoi* and that Texas conflicts law was unproved, it traced how Congress deleted the saving clause *"notwithstanding the provisions of this and the next preceding article"* when turning old Civil Code Article 11 into New Civil Code Article 17 while retaining old Article 10 as Article 16 and adding Article 1039. Specific provisions prevail over general ones, so Article 16(2) made Texas law govern intrinsic validity and successional shares without override from Article 17(3). The parties admitted that Texas law has no forced heirs or legitimes. A separate Philippine will would not change this, because a foreigner's will cannot choose Philippine law over his national law under *Miciano v. Brimo*. The case defines both the legislative history of Article 17 and the boundary where Article 17(3) yields to Article 16(2).[^109]

## Key Cases

- [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html) (G.R. No. L-23678, June 6, 1967): Two illegitimate daughters of a Texas citizen and domiciliary opposed the Manila probate court's project of partition under his Philippine will, claiming legitimes under Article 17, paragraph 3. Affirming the April 30, 1964 probate order *in toto* with costs against appellants, the Supreme Court held that Congress deleted the saving clause when enacting old Civil Code Article 11 as Article 17 so that Article 16, paragraph 2 and Article 1039 stand as specific succession rules prevailing over Article 17, paragraph 3.[^142]
- [*Royal Crown Internationale v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/oct1989/gr_78085_1989.html) (G.R. No. 78085, October 16, 1989): A licensed agency challenged POEA and NLRC rulings holding it solidarily liable with its Saudi principal for US$2,640.00 unexpired salaries, US$600.00 less SR558 vacation pay, US$350.00 travel fund reimbursement and 10% attorney's fees, arguing Saudi law governed under Clause 13 of the service agreement. Dismissing the petition (treated as Rule 65 certiorari), the Supreme Court held that Philippine labor and social legislation protect all Filipino workers locally or overseas despite contrary contract stipulations, rendered imperative by Civil Code Article 17 while noting Saudi law was neither pleaded nor proved.[^143]
- [*Republic v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_108292_1993.html) (G.R. No. 108292, September 10, 1993): The PCGG sought to nullify its 1990 compromise agreement with Roberto S. Benedicto approved by the Sandiganbayan, contending inter alia that it was not authenticated before consular officials abroad or witnessed, while sugar planters and millers moved belatedly to intervene. Dismissing the petitions in G.R. Nos. 108292, 108368, 108548-49 and 108550, lifting the March 1993 restraining orders and ordering strict compliance, the Supreme Court held that while *lex loci celebrationis* governs contract forms under Article 17 and *lex rei sitae* governs property-transfer formalities, contracts are obligatory in any form under Articles 1356 and 1358(a) and non-authentication before consular officials abroad did not invalidate the compromise.[^144]
- [*Bank of America, NT and SA v. American Realty Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37085) (G.R. No. 133876, December 29, 1999): After filing four collection suits in England and Hong Kong against Panamanian borrowers, a bank extrajudicially foreclosed third-party mortgagor ARC's Bulacan lands and invoked English law permitting concurrent collection and foreclosure, while the RTC and Court of Appeals awarded P99,000,000.00 actual damages and P5,000,000.00 exemplary damages. Denying the petition and affirming the Court of Appeals with modification reducing exemplary damages to P50,000.00 (ordering payment of P99,000,000.00 actual damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and costs), the Supreme Court held under Article 17, paragraph 3 that foreign law contravening Philippine public policy against splitting a single cause of action and working undeniable injustice cannot apply even if proved.[^145]
- [*Philippine National Bank v. Cabansag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43423) (G.R. No. 157010, June 21, 2005): A Filipino worker directly hired by PNB's Singapore branch with a Singapore Employment Pass, Manila head-office approval and a POEA Overseas Employment Certificate won illegal dismissal awards before the Labor Arbiter, NLRC and Court of Appeals (including reinstatement, backwages, bonuses, allowances, actual damages, P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and attorney's fees).[^146] Denying the petition and affirming the Court of Appeals with costs against petitioner, the Supreme Court held that a local work pass did not waive Philippine labor jurisdiction and quoted [*Royal Crown*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/oct1989/gr_78085_1989.html)[^147] on Article 17.[^148]
- [*Sim v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44910) (G.R. No. 157376, October 2, 2007): An overseas bank manager's illegal dismissal complaint was dismissed by the Labor Arbiter partly for lack of extraterritorial jurisdiction over an allegedly Italian-law contract. The Court of Appeals dismissed her Rule 65 petition for failure to file a prior NLRC motion for reconsideration.[^149] Denying the petition with costs against petitioner, the Supreme Court quoted [*Cabansag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43423)[^150] on Article 17 to confirm Labor Arbiter jurisdiction over overseas worker disputes under Labor Code Article 217 and Republic Act No. 8042 Section 10 while upholding dismissal on her procedural lapse and substantial evidence of valid dismissal.[^151]
- [*Becmen Service Exporter and Promotion, Inc. v. Cuaresma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49057) (G.R. Nos. 182978-79, April 7, 2009): After a Filipino nurse died violently in Saudi Arabia, her employer and recruitment agencies insisted she committed suicide and abandoned investigation, while the Court of Appeals in its Amended Decision awarded only US$4,686.73 unpaid salaries. Setting aside the Court of Appeals Amended Decision and holding Rajab, White Falcon, Becmen and their corporate directors and officers jointly and solidarily liable for P2,500,000.00 moral damages, P2,500,000.00 exemplary damages, 10% attorney's fees and costs of suit, the Supreme Court relied on Civil Code Articles 19, 21, 24 and 2219(10) and Republic Act No. 8042 and cited Article 17.[^152]
- [*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282) (G.R. No. 186571, August 11, 2010): A Laoag trial court denied a naturalized Canadian citizen's petition to recognize his Ontario divorce from his Filipino wife on the ground that only a Filipino spouse could invoke Family Code Article 26(2). Granting the petition, reversing the trial court Decision and Order and remanding for further proceedings without costs, the Supreme Court explained that Article 17 bars subverting the policy against absolute divorces through foreign judgments while Family Code Article 26(2) creates a direct exception for the Filipino spouse, leaving the alien spouse legal interest under Rule 39, Section 48(b) to prove the decree and foreign national law as facts.[^153]
- [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Rebesencio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60343) (G.R. No. 198587, January 14, 2015): Pregnant Filipino flight attendants were forced to resign under Saudia's Unified Contract. Saudia invoked Saudi choice-of-law clauses and *forum non conveniens*, while the NLRC and Court of Appeals awarded backwages, separation pay and 10% attorney's fees. Affirming the Court of Appeals with modification freeing Brenda Betia from solidary liability and ordering Saudia to pay full backwages and separation pay until finality, P100,000.00 moral damages and P200,000.00 exemplary damages per respondent, 10% attorney's fees and 6% interest from finality (remanded for computation), the Supreme Court distinguished Article 17(1) extrinsic form from substantive choice of law and held foreign stipulations yield to constitutional gender equality.[^154]
- [*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749) (G.R. No. 205703, March 7, 2016): A Filipino safety officer who signed a POEA-processed contract in Batangas was pre-terminated without just cause or prior notice under Ontario's Employment Standards Act, which the Labor Arbiter applied before the NLRC and Court of Appeals reversed and awarded CA$19,200.00 unpaid salaries. Denying the petition and affirming the Court of Appeals *in toto*, the Supreme Court laid down four cumulative requisites for applying foreign law to overseas labor contracts, grounded Requisite 3 on Civil Code Articles 17 and 1306 and held Philippine law governed under *lex loci celebrationis* and public policy.[^155]
- [*Morisono v. Morisono*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64412) (G.R. No. 226013, July 2, 2018): A Quezon City trial court denied recognition of a Japanese "Divorce by Agreement" because the Filipino wife had jointly procured it.[^156] Partly granting the petition, reversing and setting aside the trial court decision and remanding for proof of the divorce and Japanese law, the Supreme Court summarized the four rules on divorce under Civil Code Articles 15 and 17, quoted [*Corpuz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282)[^157] on Article 26(2) and extended Family Code Article 26(2) recognition under *Republic v. Manalo*, as cited in [*Morisono*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64412), to joint foreign divorces.[^158]
- [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229) (G.R. No. 229010, November 23, 2020): An Iriga City trial court *motu proprio* dismissed a petition to probate an American citizen's locally executed will for lack of prior American probate. Granting the petition, reversing and setting aside the trial court orders and remanding for further proceedings, the Supreme Court held that Article 17, paragraph 1 places extrinsic will formalities under *lex loci celebrationis*, making Philippine formalities govern by default alongside Articles 816 and 817 unless foreign law is pleaded and proved.[^159]
- [*Jerzon Manpower and Trading, Inc. v. Nato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67875) (G.R. No. 230211, October 6, 2021): A Taiwan-deployed worker who developed end-stage kidney disease was abruptly repatriated and refused medical aid despite contract provisions referencing Taiwan insurance, before the Court of Appeals reinstated the Labor Arbiter's award. Partially granting the petition and affirming the Court of Appeals with modification ordering Jerzon, United Taiwan Corp. and Clifford Uy Tuazon jointly and solidarily to pay the peso equivalent of NT$102,528.00 unexpired pay, P200,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages, P500,000.00 financial assistance, 10% attorney's fees and 6% interest from finality (while denying Nato's unsubstantiated P85,000.00 processing fee claim), the Supreme Court applied Republic Act No. 7875 because Taiwan law was unproved.[^160]
- [*De Munari v. Asprec*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/apr2025/gr_262831_2025.html) (G.R. No. 262831, April 7, 2025): A Puerto Princesa trial court set aside its earlier order and dismissed an Italian citizen's complaint to declare his Italian marriage to a Filipino woman void for bigamy, citing Article 17 for lack of jurisdiction. Granting the petition, reversing the trial court orders and remanding for further proceedings with utmost dispatch, the Supreme Court held that Article 17 governs extrinsic forms under *lex loci celebrationis* and does not divest Family Courts or Regional Trial Courts of jurisdiction under Republic Act No. 8369 over bigamous marriages involving Filipino citizens bound by Article 15.[^161]

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1:** An American citizen temporarily residing in Iriga City executes her last will and testament in Iriga City covering local real property and designates a Philippine resident as executor. After her death, the executor files a petition for probate in the local Regional Trial Court, which *motu proprio* dismisses the petition without prejudice on the reasoning that her national law governs and her will must be probated in the United States first. Was the dismissal proper?

No. As held in [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229),[^162] probate ordinarily tests only the extrinsic validity of a will rather than its intrinsic succession provisions under Article 16, paragraph 2. Under Article 17, paragraph 1, the forms and solemnities of wills are governed by the laws of the country in which they are executed (*lex loci celebrationis*). Read with Articles 816 and 817 of the Civil Code and Rule 73, Section 1 of the Rules of Court, Philippine law on formalities governs by default unless the alien's national law is pleaded and proved. The Philippine court keeps jurisdiction over the probate and local property without requiring prior foreign probate.[^163]

**Question 2:** An Italian citizen residing in the Philippines marries a Filipino citizen in Italy, where the marriage is registered at the Philippine Consulate General in Milan. Upon later discovering that the Filipino spouse had two prior subsisting marriages in the Philippines, the Italian spouse files a complaint in a Philippine Regional Trial Court to declare their marriage void for bigamy. The trial court dismisses the complaint for lack of jurisdiction under Article 17 of the Civil Code because the marriage was celebrated in Italy. Did the trial court rule correctly?

No. In [*De Munari v. Asprec*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/apr2025/gr_262831_2025.html),[^164] the Supreme Court held that Article 17 of the Civil Code embodies *lex loci celebrationis* for the forms and solemnities of acts (mirrored for marriages in Article 26, paragraph 1 of the Family Code) and does not divest Philippine courts of subject-matter jurisdiction. Jurisdiction over complaints for declaration of nullity of marriage is conferred by Sections 3 and 5 of Republic Act No. 8369 on Family Courts or, in their absence, Regional Trial Courts. Moreover, bigamy involves intrinsic invalidity under Article 35(4) of the Family Code. The Filipino spouse remains bound by Philippine prohibitory laws on family rights, status and legal capacity everywhere under Article 15 of the Civil Code.[^165]

**Question 3:** A Filipino worker signs a POEA-processed overseas employment contract at his residence in Batangas to work in Madagascar for a Canadian company, whose internal policy refers to the laws of the country where its office is located. After three months, the company pre-terminates his employment without just cause or prior notice, invoking an authenticated copy of Ontario's employment statute that permits dismissal upon payment of notice pay alone. Should the foreign statute govern the validity of his dismissal?

No. Under [*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749),[^166] Philippine law governs overseas employment contracts unless four cumulative requisites are met: express stipulation of a specific foreign law, proof of that foreign law under Philippine evidentiary rules, conformity of the foreign law with Philippine law, morals, good customs, public order or public policy and POEA processing. Here, the contract failed the first requisite because it contained no express choice of Canadian law, leaving Philippine law applicable under *lex loci celebrationis*. Even if stipulated and proved, the Ontario statute failed the third requisite grounded on Article 17, paragraph 3 and Article 1306 of the Civil Code because dispensing with just cause and prior notice contravenes Philippine constitutional guarantees of security of tenure and due process.[^167]

# Quotes

> "Article 17 of the Civil Code provides that when it comes to the form and solemnities of contracts, wills and other public instruments, the law of the country of execution shall govern. This is also known as the principle of lex loci celebrationis. Lex loci celebrationis is a conflict of law principle that comes into play when there are substantive issues relating to a contract that is celebrated elsewhere than the place of citizenship of its parties."[^168]

> "The nationality principle is not applied when determining the extrinsic validity of an alien's last will and testament. When it comes to the probate of an alien's will, whether executed here or abroad, the alien's national law may be pleaded and proved before the probate court. Otherwise, Philippine law will govern by default."[^169]

> "It is equally puerile for the PCGG to contend that the agreement is congenitally defective from the mere happenstance that the agreement was not authenticated before the consular officials abroad and without the participation of witnesses and of the Solicitor General. While the rule of lex loci celebrationis generally governs forms and solemnities of contracts under Article 17 of the Civil Code (Vitug, Compendium of Civil Law and Jurisprudence, 1986 First ed., p. 11), the principle of lex rei sitae generally applies with respect to formalities for the acquisition, encumbrance, and alienation of real and personal property (1 Paras, Civil Code of the Philippines annotated, 1989 12th ed., p. 107). And relative to this precept on lex situs, Philippine substantive law is certainly clear on the matter that contracts are obligatory, in whatever form they may have been entered into, subject to the existence of all the essential requisites for their validity (Article 1356, New Civil Code). The fact that the compromise agreement was not authenticated before the consular officers abroad, as well as the absence of witnesses, cannot be of much legal significance under Philippine law inasmuch as the requirement under Article 1358(a) of the Civil Code, that a contract intended to extinguish or transmit real rights over the immovables must be in a public document is merely designed for greater efficacy or convenience (4 Tolentino, Commentaries and Jurisprudence on the Civil Code of the Philippines, 1991 ed., p. 546)."[^170]

> "Philippine law is definite as to what governs the formal or extrinsic validity of contracts. The first paragraph of Article 17 of the Civil Code provides that "[t]he forms and solemnities of contracts . . . shall be governed by the laws of the country in which they are executed" (i.e., lex loci celebrationis)."[^171]

> "This contention is patently erroneous. The provisions of the Labor Code of the Philippines, its implementing rules and regulations, and doctrines laid down in jurisprudence dealing with the principle of due process and the basic right of all Filipino workers to security of tenure, provide the standard by which the legality of the exercise by management of its prerogative to dismiss incompetent, dishonest or recalcitrant employees, is to be determined. Whether employed locally or overseas, all Filipino workers enjoy the protective mantle of Philippine labor and social legislation, contract stipulations to the contrary notwithstanding. This pronouncement is in keeping with the basic public policy of the State to afford protection to labor, promote full employment, ensure equal work opportunities regardless of sex, race or creed, and regulate the relations between workers and employers. For the State assures the basic rights of all workers to self-organization, collective bargaining, security of tenure, and just and humane conditions of work [Article 3 of the Labor Code of the Philippines; See also Section 18, Article II and Section 3, Article XIII, 1987 Constitution]. This ruling is likewise rendered imperative by Article 17 of the Civil Code which states that laws "which have for their object public order, public policy and good customs shall not be rendered ineffective by laws or judgments promulgated, or by determination or conventions agreed upon in a foreign country.""[^172]

> "If the third requisite is not met, or that the foreign law stipulated is contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order or public policy, then Philippine laws govern. This finds legal bases in the Civil Code, specifically: (1) Article 17, which provides that laws which have, for their object, public order, public policy and good customs shall not be rendered ineffective by laws of a foreign country; and (2) Article 1306, which states that the stipulations, clauses, terms and conditions in a contract must not be contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order, or public policy. The said doctrine was applied in the case of Pakistan International."[^173]

> "Additionally, prohibitive laws concerning persons, their acts or property, and those which have for their object public order, public policy and good customs shall not be rendered ineffective by laws or judgments promulgated, or by determinations or conventions agreed upon in a foreign country."[^174]

> "As the RTC correctly stated, the provision was included in the law "to avoid the absurd situation where the Filipino spouse remains married to the alien spouse who, after obtaining a divorce, is no longer married to the Filipino spouse." The legislative intent is for the benefit of the Filipino spouse, by clarifying his or her marital status, settling the doubts created by the divorce decree. Essentially, the second paragraph of Article 26 of the Family Code provided the Filipino spouse a substantive right to have his or her marriage to the alien spouse considered as dissolved, capacitating him or her to remarry. Without the second paragraph of Article 26 of the Family Code, the judicial recognition of the foreign decree of divorce, whether in a proceeding instituted precisely for that purpose or as a related issue in another proceeding, would be of no significance to the Filipino spouse since our laws do not recognize divorce as a mode of severing the marital bond; Article 17 of the Civil Code provides that the policy against absolute divorces cannot be subverted by judgments promulgated in a foreign country. The inclusion of the second paragraph in Article 26 of the Family Code provides the direct exception to this rule and serves as basis for recognizing the dissolution of the marriage between the Filipino spouse and his or her alien spouse."[^175]

> "The rules on divorce prevailing in this jurisdiction can be summed up as follows: first, Philippine laws do not provide for absolute divorce, and hence, the courts cannot grant the same; second, consistent with Articles 15 and 17 of the Civil Code, the marital bond between two (2) Filipino citizens cannot be dissolved even by an absolute divorce obtained abroad; third, an absolute divorce obtained abroad by a couple, who are both aliens, may be recognized in the Philippines, provided it is consistent with their respective national laws; and fourth, in mixed marriages involving a Filipino and a foreigner, the former is allowed to contract a subsequent marriage in case the absolute divorce is validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry."[^176]

> "Precisely, Congress deleted the phrase, "notwithstanding the provisions of this and the next preceding article" when they incorporated Art. 11 of the old Civil Code as Art. 17 of the new Civil Code, while reproducing without substantial change the second paragraph of Art. 10 of the old Civil Code as Art. 16 in the new. It must have been their purpose to make the second paragraph of Art. 16 a specific provision in itself which must be applied in testate and intestate succession. As further indication of this legislative intent, Congress added a new provision, under Art. 1039, which decrees that capacity to succeed is to be governed by the national law of the decedent."[^177]

# Tips

### Diagnostic Checklist

1. **Separate form from substance and jurisdiction:** Verify whether the issue concerns extrinsic formalities under Article 17, paragraph 1 ([*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229); [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Rebesencio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60343)) or subject-matter jurisdiction conferred by Philippine statutes ([*De Munari v. Asprec*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/apr2025/gr_262831_2025.html)).
2. **Test foreign employment stipulations against all four cumulative requisites:** Confirm express stipulation in the contract, evidentiary proof of the foreign statute, compatibility with Philippine public policy under Article 17, paragraph 3 and Article 1306 and POEA processing ([*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749)).
3. **Verify specific statutory overrides before applying Article 17(3):** Check whether succession falls under Article 16(2) and Article 1039 ([*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html)) or whether a mixed-marriage divorce falls under Family Code Article 26(2) ([*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282); [*Morisono v. Morisono*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64412)).
4. **Demand proof of foreign law and foreign decrees under Rule 132:** Check whether the foreign enactment or judgment was pleaded and formally proved rather than assumed or merely registered administratively ([*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282); [*Bank of America, NT and SA v. American Realty Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37085)).

### Common Traps

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Trap:** Do not treat Article 17, paragraph 1 (*lex loci celebrationis*) as a rule of court jurisdiction or intrinsic marital validity. As explained in [*De Munari v. Asprec*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/apr2025/gr_262831_2025.html) and [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229), a Philippine court keeps jurisdiction over a locally executed alien's will and over a bigamous marriage celebrated abroad involving a Filipino citizen. Likewise, do not invoke Article 17, paragraph 3 to apply Philippine legitimes to a foreign national's estate controlled by Article 16, paragraph 2 ([*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice Trap:** In overseas employment disputes, submitting a duly authenticated foreign labor statute is not enough if the contract omitted an express choice-of-law clause or if the foreign statute eliminates security of tenure or prior notice ([*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749)). Conversely, invoking Article 17 and Philippine labor jurisdiction will not excuse failing to file a mandatory motion for reconsideration before the NLRC ([*Sim v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44910)).

### What Worked and What Failed

Claims and defenses succeeded when parties respected statutory boundaries, proved facts properly and enforced mandatory Philippine public policy. In [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html), enforcing Article 16(2) over Article 17(3) sustained Texas succession rules. In [*Republic v. Sandiganbayan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_108292_1993.html), enforcing Articles 1356 and 1358(a) preserved a compromise agreement despite lacking consular authentication. In [*Royal Crown Internationale v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/oct1989/gr_78085_1989.html), [*Philippine National Bank v. Cabansag*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43423), [*Becmen Service Exporter and Promotion, Inc. v. Cuaresma*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49057), [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Rebesencio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60343), [*Industrial Personnel & Management Services, Inc. v. De Vera*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61749) and [*Jerzon Manpower and Trading, Inc. v. Nato*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67875), Filipino overseas workers and their heirs prevailed when Philippine labor protections and constitutional guarantees overrode foreign choice-of-law clauses and oppressive practices. In [*Bank of America, NT and SA v. American Realty Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37085), refusing English law on concurrent remedies under Article 17(3) held the bank to a single cause of action and protected a Philippine third-party mortgagor.

Arguments failed whenever parties stretched Article 17 beyond extrinsic form, left foreign law unproved or relied on foreign law incompatible with Philippine public policy. In [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229) and [*De Munari v. Asprec*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2025/apr2025/gr_262831_2025.html), trial-court dismissals for lack of jurisdiction were reversed. In [*Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54282) and [*Morisono v. Morisono*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64412), parties still had to prove both the foreign divorce decree and the foreign law in court before obtaining recognition. In [*Sim v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44910), the employee's victory on Labor Arbiter jurisdiction under Labor Code Article 217 and Republic Act No. 8042 could not save a petition barred by procedural omission and valid just cause.
