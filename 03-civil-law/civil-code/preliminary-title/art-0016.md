> ART. 16. Real property as well as personal property is subject to the law of the country where it is situated.
>
> However, intestate and testamentary successions, both with respect to the order of succession and to the amount of successional rights and to the intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions, shall be regulated by the national law of the person whose succession is under consideration, whatever may be the nature of the property and regardless of the country wherein said property may be found. (10a)

[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 16 establishes two distinct conflict-of-laws rules within a single provision. Paragraph 1 subjects real property and personal property to the law of the country where it is situated (*lex rei sitae*). Paragraph 2 carves out a specific rule for intestate and testamentary successions by making the national law of the decedent govern the order of succession, the amount of successional rights and the intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions regardless of the nature or location of the property.[^civil-code]

**In brief**

1. Identify the issue first to see whether the dispute concerns property ownership and jurisdiction at the situs under paragraph 1 or hereditary succession under paragraph 2.
2. When succession is in issue, apply the decedent's national law (identifying the exact constituent state in a federal system) rather than Philippine legitimes.
3. Plead and prove the governing foreign law as a fact because Philippine courts do not take judicial notice of foreign statutes or conflicts rules.
4. Keep extrinsic will formalities, marital property relations and pre-1950 regimes under their separate Civil Code rules rather than forcing them into Article 16.

The sections below examine the *lex rei sitae* rule and its exclusion of marital property regimes, the four successional items governed by national law, constituent state law and *renvoi*, proof of foreign law and fiduciary obligations, excluded questions of probate formalities and citizenship renunciation and the 1889 Civil Code transition, followed by Case Discussions, Related Provisions, Key Cases and Suggested Answers.

## Lex Rei Sitae and Its Boundary With Marital Property

Under the first paragraph of Article 16, Philippine courts apply the territorial situs rule to determine jurisdiction over property located outside the Philippines and ownership restrictions over land located within the Philippines. In [*Noveras v. Noveras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57649),[^2] two spouses married in Quezon City became United States citizens residing in California and acquired assets in both countries. When the wife petitioned a Philippine trial court for judicial separation of conjugal property after obtaining an unproved California divorce, the Supreme Court held under Article 16 that real and personal property is subject to the law of the country where it is situated. Philippine courts therefore acquired no jurisdiction over the California assets and limited absolute community liquidation strictly to the Philippine properties.[^3]

Within Philippine territory, constitutional limits on alien landholding apply even when heirs claim through succession. In [*Heirs of Sadhwani v. Sadhwani*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65628),[^4] children of deceased Indian nationals sued their brother over a Bel Air, Makati lot and a Makati condominium unit allegedly held in trust for their parents and siblings. The Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of the complaint without prejudice because Indian nationals were disqualified under Article XII, Section 7 of the 1987 Constitution from owning Philippine land or transmitting land rights by succession. Although that absolute land prohibition does not necessarily apply to condominium units, the Supreme Court held that Article 16 required the children to plead and prove their successional rights to the condominium unit under the laws of the Republic of India.[^5] Conversely, in [*Ancheta v. Guersey-Dalaygon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349),[^6] the Supreme Court noted that whatever flaw, if any, attended the acquisition of a Makati house and lot by American spouses under the 1935, 1973 and 1986 Constitutions was rendered inconsequential once the property passed by succession to a Filipino citizen.

Article 16 does not govern the property relations between spouses during marriage. In [*Collector of Internal Revenue v. Fisher*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1961/jan1961/gr_l-11622_1961.html),[^7] the Collector cited Article 16 of the New Civil Code (Article 10 of the old Civil Code) to argue that the full conjugal estate of a British decedent married in Manila in 1909 was taxable under English law without deducting the surviving wife's one-half share. Rejecting that argument, the Supreme Court held that old Article 10 (the applicable provision for a 1909 marriage) does not contemplate property relations between spouses. The statutory phrase "amount of successional rights" refers properly to the extent or amount of property each heir is legally entitled to inherit from the distributable estate, whereas marital property relations fall under Title VI, Chapter I of the New Civil Code (Title III, Chapter I of the old Civil Code).[^8]

## National Law of the Decedent in Succession

Article 16, paragraph 2 operates together with Article 1039 of the Civil Code to make the decedent's national law control four distinct matters in both testate and intestate succession. In [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html),[^9] the Supreme Court identified those four items as the order of succession, the amount of successional rights, the intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions and the capacity to succeed. Because Congress made Article 16, paragraph 2 a specific rule alongside Article 1039, the Supreme Court held that Article 17, paragraph 3 on prohibitive public-policy laws does not override it. Philippine rules on compulsory heirs and legitimes therefore did not extend to the testate estate of a Texas citizen and domiciliary whose national law recognized no forced heirs.[^10]

A foreign testator also cannot substitute Philippine succession rules for his own national law. In [*Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html),[^11] illegitimate daughters argued that executing a separate Philippine will showed the testator intended Philippine law to govern his Philippine assets. Relying on *Miciano v. Brimo*, as cited in [*Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html), the Supreme Court held that a provision in a foreigner's will directing distribution under Philippine law rather than his national law is illegal and void because national law cannot be ignored on matters governed by Article 16.[^12]

Supreme Court decisions apply this rule consistently across reprobate, intestate administration and estate distribution proceedings:

- In [*Cayetano v. Leonidas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html),[^13] a will of an American citizen domiciled in Pennsylvania omitted her Filipino father, who claimed divestment of his legitime. Following [*Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html),[^14] the Supreme Court held that under Articles 16(2) and 1039 the law of Pennsylvania governed intrinsic validity and did not provide for legitimes.[^15]
- In [*Llorente v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38202),[^16] a former Filipino became a naturalized American citizen before divorcing his first wife, remarrying, executing a will and dying. The Supreme Court set aside lower-court rulings that applied Philippine legitimes and co-ownership, holding that his foreign national law governed intrinsic validity and successional rights.[^17]
- In [*Ancheta v. Guersey-Dalaygon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349),[^18] the Supreme Court held that the law of the State of Maryland governed who were the heirs of a deceased American citizen domiciled in Maryland and the intrinsic validity of her will leaving her entire estate to her husband.
- In [*Ngo The Hua v. Chung Kiat Hua*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1963/sep1963/gr_l-17091_1963.html),[^19] a decedent's alleged nephew sought to intervene and become co-administrator in Philippine administration proceedings. Applying Article 16 because the decedent was a Chinese citizen, the Supreme Court held that under the Civil Code of the Republic of China in evidence the nephew was neither an heir nor a creditor and therefore lacked the interest required to intervene under Rule 80, Section 4.[^20]

## Constituent State Law and the Renvoi Doctrine

When a foreign decedent belongs to a federal nation without a single uniform succession statute, "national law" in Article 16 points to the law of the specific constituent state where the decedent was a resident or domiciliary. In [*Llorente v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38202),[^21] the trial court asserted that "American law" followed the domiciliary theory so as to refer the case back to Philippine law. Reversing that premise, the Supreme Court held that there is no such thing as one American law governing testamentary validity in the United States. Each state has its own law applicable to its citizens and in force only within that state. Article 16 therefore referred to the law of the state of which the decedent was a resident. There was also no showing that New York State law called for *renvoi*.[^22]

In [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html),[^23] the Supreme Court distinguished *Aznar v. Christensen Garcia*, as cited in [*Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html), where the *renvoi* doctrine applied. *Renvoi* is usually pertinent when a decedent is a national of one country and a domiciliary of another. Because Amos G. Bellis was both a citizen and a domiciliary of Texas at death, a Texas domiciliary conflicts rule would still point to Texas law rather than Philippine law. While a Texas *lex rei sitae* conflicts rule could trigger *renvoi* over Philippine assets, no Texas conflicts rule was proved and none would be presumed different from Philippine law.[^24] Consistent with that state-law focus, [*Cayetano v. Leonidas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html)[^25] applied the law of Pennsylvania. [*Ancheta v. Guersey-Dalaygon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349)[^26] applied the law of the State of Maryland.

## Proof of Foreign Law, Processual Presumption and Fiduciary Duties

Philippine courts do not take judicial notice of foreign laws or foreign judgments, which must be alleged and proved as facts under Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25 of the Rules of Court. In [*Heirs of Sadhwani v. Sadhwani*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65628),[^27] the Supreme Court held that plaintiffs claiming condominium inheritance from Indian nationals could not sidestep their burden of sufficiently pleading and eventually proving a cause of action under Indian law even if Philippine law appeared more favorable. In [*Llorente v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38202),[^28] the Supreme Court likewise reiterated that foreign laws do not prove themselves and remanded the estate proceedings to allow proof of foreign law.

When a party invokes foreign law without proving it, Philippine courts apply processual presumption and presume the foreign law to be the same as Philippine law. In [*Collector of Internal Revenue v. Fisher*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1961/jan1961/gr_l-11622_1961.html),[^29] the Collector alleged that English law vested sole ownership of marital acquisitions in the husband but failed to offer any proof beyond a bare allegation in his answer, leaving the Supreme Court to apply Philippine conjugal partnership rules by processual presumption. In [*Noveras v. Noveras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57649),[^30] however, the Supreme Court clarified a vital limit to processual presumption. Although the trial court applied processual presumption to the spouses' property regime, failing to prove the California divorce decree and California law under Rule 132 left the spouses still legally married in the Philippines because Philippine law does not recognize divorce between Filipino citizens.[^31]

An estate fiduciary cannot deliberately rely on processual presumption to defeat a foreign decedent's will. In [*Ancheta v. Guersey-Dalaygon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349),[^32] the ancillary administrator omitted to introduce Maryland law in the probate court and instead distributed an American decedent's estate under Philippine legitime rules to benefit an adopted child. Holding that an ancillary administrator occupies a position of highest trust and was duty-bound to prove Maryland law under Article 16 and Rule 77, Section 4, the Supreme Court ruled that his omission combined with his fiduciary role and the frustration of the will amounted to extrinsic fraud justifying annulment of the final distribution orders. Citing *Bohanan v. Bohanan*, as cited in [*Ancheta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349), the Supreme Court took judicial notice of the pertinent Maryland Estates and Trusts provisions because they had been brought to record before the appellate court and in the companion estate case without dispute.[^33]

## Excluded Questions: Extrinsic Probate Validity and Citizenship Renunciation

Article 16 does not govern the formal or extrinsic validity of wills or divest Philippine courts of probate jurisdiction. In [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229),[^34] a trial court *motu proprio* dismissed a petition to probate an American citizen's will executed in Iriga City, believing Article 16 required prior probate in the United States under her national law. Reversing the dismissal, the Supreme Court held that Article 16, paragraph 2 governs succession issues and the intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions, whereas probate generally involves only extrinsic validity unless exceptional circumstances require addressing intrinsic validity. Under Article 17 and Articles 816 and 817 of the Civil Code, an alien's will may be probated in the Philippines under Philippine formalities by default without prior foreign probate. The trial court retains jurisdiction under Rule 73, Section 1 over the local real property.[^35] Similarly, in [*Llorente v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38202),[^36] Philippine law governed the formal validity of the will executed in the Philippines under Article 17, while in [*Cayetano v. Leonidas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html),[^37] the Supreme Court noted that practical considerations under *Maninang v. Court of Appeals*, as cited in [*Cayetano*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html), may permit a probate court to pass upon intrinsic validity before probate when preterition of a forced heir is raised on the face of an alien's will.

Beyond succession and property, the Supreme Court has also cited Article 16 alongside Article 15 in determining how a foreign national renounces prior citizenship during Philippine naturalization. In [*Oh Hek How v. Republic*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1969/aug1969/gr_l-27429_1969.html),[^38] a Chinese national seeking Philippine citizenship argued that Section 12 of Commonwealth Act No. 473 could not be conditioned on foreign law. Holding that the question of how a Chinese citizen strips himself of that status is governed pursuant to Articles 15 and 16 of the Civil Code by Chinese law (subject only to the Republic Act No. 2639 treaty exception for Iberian and friendly democratic Ibero-American countries), the Supreme Court reiterated *Go A. Leng v. Republic*, as cited in [*Oh Hek How*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1969/aug1969/gr_l-27429_1969.html), which superseded *Parado v. Republic*, *Chausintek v. Republic* and *Lim So v. Republic*, as cited in [*Oh Hek How*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1969/aug1969/gr_l-27429_1969.html). It therefore required prior permission from Nationalist China's Minister of the Interior.[^39]

## Legislative Genesis and Transition From the 1889 Civil Code

Article 16 of Republic Act No. 386 carries forward Article 10 of the 1889 Spanish Civil Code.[^civil-code] In [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html),[^40] the Supreme Court traced this legislative design to demonstrate Congress's intent. When incorporating Article 11 of the old Civil Code into Article 17 of the New Civil Code, Congress deleted the saving clause "notwithstanding the provisions of this and the next preceding article," reproduced the second paragraph of old Article 10 without substantial change as Article 16 and added a new provision in Article 1039 subjecting capacity to succeed to the decedent's national law. That deliberate deletion and addition established Article 16, paragraph 2 as a specific rule that prevails over the general public-policy bar in Article 17, paragraph 3.[^41]

For transactions or marriages governed by pre-1950 law, old Article 10 applies instead of Article 16. In [*Collector of Internal Revenue v. Fisher*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1961/jan1961/gr_l-11622_1961.html),[^42] the Supreme Court noted that because the British spouses married in 1909 before the New Civil Code took effect in 1950, old Article 10 and old Article 1325 (rather than New Civil Code Articles 16 and 124) were the applicable provisions, though neither old Article 10 nor new Article 16 governed marital property relations.

## Case Discussions

### Extrinsic Probate Jurisdiction Over an Alien's Local Will

Luz Gaspe Lipson was a Filipina who later became an American citizen. She was temporarily residing in Iriga City when she executed her last will and testament on February 23, 2011. She designated Roel P. Gaspi as executor. The will passed real property in Iriga City. Lipson died of lymphoma on October 17, 2015 at age 70.[^43] Gaspi petitioned for probate and for letters testamentary without bond on October 3, 2016. Branch 36 of the Regional Trial Court of Iriga City *motu proprio* dismissed the petition without prejudice on October 6, 2016 for lack of jurisdiction. It reasoned that Lipson was an American citizen, so her national law governed and her will had to be probated in the United States first. It added that the Philippines could recognize the will only through a later petition for recognition of a foreign judgment. The trial court denied reconsideration on November 16, 2016.[^44]

The Supreme Court granted the Rule 45 petition. It reversed and set aside the October 6, 2016 and November 16, 2016 Orders in Special Proceeding No. IR-2919. It remanded the case to the trial court for further proceedings.[^45] The trial court had misread the nationality principle. Article 16, paragraph 2 makes the national law of an alien govern succession issues and the intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions. Probate generally tests only extrinsic validity unless exceptional circumstances require more. The extrinsic validity of a will made in the Philippines follows Philippine law by default under Article 17. The national law of the alien applies only if it is pleaded and proved.[^46] Articles 816 and 817 allow Philippine law to govern the extrinsic validity of an alien's will. Rule 73, Section 1 lets a Philippine trial court settle the estate of a foreign inhabitant in any province where the decedent left estate.[^47] The statutory limit is that foreign law is a fact that must be proved. The trial court therefore kept jurisdiction over the probate and over the Iriga real property. No one had objected to its jurisdiction. The Supreme Court remanded so that the trial court could determine compliance with the required formalities. It also directed a finding on Lipson's testamentary capacity and voluntary execution of the will.[^48] The case shows that an alien's will made in the Philippines to pass Philippine real property may be probated here without prior probate in the alien's home country.[^49]

### Pleading Foreign Successional Rights and Land Disqualification

Satramdas and Kishnibai Sadhwani were Indian nationals. Their other legitimate children sued in Branch 59 of the Regional Trial Court of Makati City on November 13, 2013. They sought reconveyance, partition and damages against their brother Gop, his wife Kanta, Union Bank of the Philippines, Philippine Savings Bank and the Register of Deeds of Makati City. The disputed properties were a Bel Air lot at 58 Aries Street and Ritz Condominium Unit 602-A in Makati.[^50] The titles were allegedly placed in Gop's name in trust for his parents and siblings. The plaintiffs claimed ownership as heirs under a purported express trust and the Civil Code provisions on succession. Their amended complaint of March 11, 2014 alleged that the Bel Air lot had been sold for Php20,000,000.00. The trial court dismissed the complaint on January 6, 2015 for lack of legal capacity to sue, failure to plead a cause of action and lack of cause of action. It relied on death certificates showing that the parents were Indian nationals barred by Article XII, Section 7 of the 1987 Constitution from owning the properties or transmitting rights over them. It denied reconsideration on March 18, 2015.[^51]

The Supreme Court dismissed the petition and affirmed the January 6, 2015 and March 18, 2015 Resolutions in Civil Case No. 13-1320.[^52] The Rule 45 petition was the wrong remedy. A dismissal without prejudice cannot be appealed under Rule 41, Section 1. The proper remedy was a Rule 65 petition filed first with the Court of Appeals, so the petition also disregarded the hierarchy of courts. The Supreme Court nonetheless passed upon the dismissal. It held that the parents were absolutely disqualified from owning the Bel Air lot, whether actually or beneficially. They could not transmit any right over it by succession. The plaintiffs therefore failed to allege a right in their favor.[^53] The absolute land prohibition does not necessarily apply to condominium units. The plaintiffs still failed to state a cause of action over Unit 602-A. Article 16 makes successional rights depend on the national law of the decedent. The complaint should have alleged that the plaintiffs were heirs entitled to inherit under the laws of India. They could not sidestep the burden of pleading and eventually proving foreign law even if Philippine law seemed more favorable or expedient.[^54] The dismissal under Rule 16, Section 1(g) was without prejudice, so the plaintiffs may refile. The case shows that heirs of a foreign decedent must plead the foreign law that gives them the right to inherit.[^55]

### Territorial Limit on Property Liquidation

David A. Noveras and Leticia T. Noveras married on December 3, 1988 in Quezon City. They lived in California, where they became American citizens. They had two children, Jerome born in 1990 and Jena born in 1993. David returned to the Philippines in 2001 and the spouses lived apart from 2003. Leticia obtained a divorce decree from the Superior Court of California in June 2005. The decree awarded all properties in the United States to her.[^56] On August 8, 2005 Leticia petitioned the Regional Trial Court of Baler, Aurora, Branch 96 for judicial separation of conjugal property. The Philippine properties included a house and lot in Sampaloc, Manila, which she had authorized David to sell for P2.2 million. The buyers, the Paringit spouses, still owed P410,000.00. On December 8, 2006 the trial court treated the California divorce as effective. It awarded the Philippine net assets to David and left the United States properties with Leticia. It applied Philippine law by processual presumption because no proof of American law was offered. On May 9, 2008 the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CV No. 88686 partly granted Leticia's appeal. It divided the Philippine net assets equally. It ordered David to pay Leticia P1,040,000.00 as her share in the Sampaloc sale proceeds. It ordered each spouse to pay P520,000.00 to the two children as presumptive legitimes.[^57]

The Supreme Court denied David's petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision.[^58] It first held that the trial court erred in recognizing the divorce. Only the decree was presented. The certificates proving its authenticity and the pertinent California law were missing, contrary to Rule 132, Sections 24 and 25. Processual presumption could not cure that gap because divorce is not recognized between Filipino citizens in the Philippines. The spouses therefore remained legally married here. The Supreme Court still granted judicial separation of property under Article 135(6) of the Family Code. The spouses had been separated in fact for more than one year and reconciliation was highly improbable. Abandonment under Article 135(4) had not been proven.[^59] On liquidation the Supreme Court agreed that Philippine courts did not acquire jurisdiction over the California properties. Article 16 states that real and personal property are subject to the law of the country where they are situated. Liquidation was therefore limited to the Philippine properties.[^60] The spouses shared the Sampaloc proceeds equally after deducting P120,000.00 for David's travel between the United States and the Philippines. Each share was P1,040,000.00. David could not charge P300,000.00 in election expenses without receipts. Each spouse paid the children P520,000.00 as presumptive legitimes under Article 888 of the Civil Code. That amount counted the P410,000.00 receivable from the Paringit spouses.[^61] The case shows that Article 16 confines Philippine liquidation of marital property to assets located in the Philippines.[^62]

### Ancillary Administrator's Duty to Prove Foreign Law

Audrey O'Neill Guersey and W. Richard Guersey were American citizens who had lived in the Philippines for 30 years. They had an adopted daughter, Kyle. Audrey died on July 29, 1979 and was domiciled in Maryland. Her will gave her entire estate to Richard. The Orphan's Court of Baltimore admitted it to probate on September 7, 1979.[^63] Richard married Candelaria Guersey-Dalaygon in 1981. The will was also probated on October 12, 1982 in Special Proceeding No. 9625 before Branch 25 of the Court of First Instance of Rizal in Pasig. Atty. Alonzo Q. Ancheta served as ancillary administrator. His inventory listed a Makati property valued at P764,865.00, P12,417.97 in cash and 64,444 A/G Interiors shares worth P64,444.00. Richard died on July 20, 1984. He left his estate to Candelaria except his A/G Interiors shares, which went to Kyle. Ancheta never introduced Maryland law. He moved to declare Richard and Kyle heirs of Audrey. The trial court approved his project of partition on February 12, 1988 and issued a title order on April 7, 1988. Richard's estate took three fourths of the Makati property and Kyle took one fourth. On October 20, 1993 Candelaria sued in the Court of Appeals to annul both orders. On March 18, 1999 the Court of Appeals annulled them for extrinsic fraud. It adjudicated Audrey's entire estate to Richard's estate and ordered the cancellation of TCT No. 15583. It denied reconsideration on August 27, 1999.[^64]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the March 18, 1999 Decision and August 27, 1999 Resolution of the Court of Appeals. It admonished Ancheta to be more circumspect in performing his duties as an official of the court. It made no pronouncement as to costs.[^65] Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 governed the annulment because the petition was filed in 1993. That law allowed annulment for lack of jurisdiction or extrinsic fraud. Under Article 16 and Article 1039 the law of Maryland governed who were Audrey's heirs and the intrinsic validity of her will. Foreign laws do not prove themselves and courts do not take judicial notice of them. Ancheta held a position of the highest trust and was duty-bound to introduce Maryland law. He admitted that he relied on the presumption that Maryland law matched Philippine law. The Supreme Court rejected his claim of good faith. He was a senior partner in a prestigious law firm and had all the legal resources to find the applicable law. His omission, joined with his fiduciary role and the frustration of the will, amounted to extrinsic fraud.[^66] The Maryland provisions were already in the record of the companion Special Proceeding No. M-888 and no one disputed them. The Supreme Court took judicial notice of them, citing *Bohanan v. Bohanan*, as cited in [*Ancheta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349). Under that law a legacy passes the testator's entire interest in the property. The entire Makati property therefore belonged to Candelaria. Any constitutional flaw in the Guerseys' ownership of the land was cured because the property had passed to a Filipino.[^67] The case shows that an ancillary administrator cannot rest on processual presumption when his duty is to prove the foreign national law.[^68]

### State Law Within a Federal System and Renvoi

Lorenzo N. Llorente married Paula Llorente on February 22, 1937 in Nabua, Camarines Sur. The United States District Court for the Southern District of New York admitted him to American citizenship on November 30, 1943. In 1945 he found Paula pregnant and living with his brother. The spouses signed a separation agreement in 1946. Lorenzo then obtained a divorce from the Superior Court of California in San Diego. The decree became final on December 4, 1952.[^69] On January 16, 1958 he married Alicia F. Llorente in Manila. They had three children, Raul, Luz and Beverly. Lorenzo executed a will on March 13, 1981 that left his properties to Alicia and the children. The trial court admitted it to probate on January 24, 1984. He died on June 11, 1985. Branch 35 of the Regional Trial Court of Iriga City held the divorce void and the will intrinsically void. It treated Alicia as a paramour. It appointed Paula administratrix on a P100,000.00 bond. On July 31, 1995 the Court of Appeals affirmed with modification. It declared Alicia co-owner under Article 144 of the Civil Code of the properties acquired during their 25 years together. It denied reconsideration on March 21, 1996.[^70]

The Supreme Court granted the petition. It set aside the July 31, 1995 decision of the Court of Appeals and reversed the trial court decision. It recognized the December 4, 1952 California divorce as valid. It remanded the cases to the court of origin to determine the intrinsic validity of the will and the successional rights, allowing proof of foreign law. It ordered no costs.[^71] The trial court and the Court of Appeals had turned to the doctrine of *renvoi* to apply Philippine law. Lorenzo's American citizenship at the time of the divorce, the second marriage, the will and his death was undisputed. Citing *Van Dorn v. Romillo, Jr.*, the Supreme Court restated that the policy against absolute divorce binds only Filipinos under Article 15. An alien's foreign divorce is recognized as a matter of comity if it is valid under his national law. The effect of the divorce on succession was left to the trial court.[^72] Article 16 points to the national law of the decedent. There is no single American law on testamentary validity because each state has its own law. The reference can only be to the law of the state of which the decedent was a resident. Nothing showed that New York State law called for *renvoi*. Intrinsic validity and heirship were matters for proof of foreign law. The formal validity of the will followed Philippine law under Article 17. The will had also been probated. The Supreme Court added as a guide that Congress did not extend the system of legitimes to the succession of foreign nationals.[^73] The case shows that Philippine courts must identify the specific state law of a foreign federal national and require proof before applying *renvoi*.[^74]

### Reprobate and Foreign Law Dispensing With Legitimes

Adoracion C. Campos was an American citizen with a permanent residence in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. She died in Manila on January 31, 1977 while temporarily staying with her sister. Her father Hermogenes Campos was her only compulsory heir. He executed an affidavit of adjudication under Rule 74 of the Rules of Court and claimed her entire estate.[^75] On November 25, 1977 her sister Nenita C. Paguia petitioned for reprobate of Adoracion's July 10, 1975 will. The Orphan's Court Division in Philadelphia had already probated it. Hermogenes opposed on January 11, 1978 and called the will a forgery. On December 1, 1978 his counsel Atty. Franco Loyola moved to dismiss the opposition, confirmed the will as genuine and waived his rights. Branch XXXVIII of the Court of First Instance of Manila received the evidence *ex parte*. On January 10, 1979 it admitted the will and appointed Nenita administratrix on a P5,000.00 bond. Hermogenes filed a petition for relief on May 25, 1979 and claimed that the withdrawal was obtained by fraud. On June 19, 1980 the trial judge dismissed it because he presented no evidence. The judge also denied his motion to vacate. Hermogenes died on June 6, 1982. Polly Cayetano, the executrix of his will, was substituted as petitioner on September 13, 1982.[^76]

The Supreme Court dismissed the petition for certiorari and prohibition for lack of merit.[^77] It found no grave abuse in allowing the withdrawal of the opposition. No proof showed that fraud secured it. Hermogenes had confirmed it as his voluntary act in a later manifestation. The probate court normally passes only on extrinsic validity. Where practical considerations demand, it should meet the question of intrinsic validity even before probate.[^78] The will appeared on its face to have preterited Hermogenes. Nenita had shown that Adoracion was an American citizen and a permanent resident of Philadelphia. Articles 16(2) and 1039 therefore made Pennsylvania law the governing law. The parties admitted that Pennsylvania law has no legitimes. The Supreme Court rejected the public policy objection by applying the rule in [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html) as quoted in [*Cayetano*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html). Philippine public policy on legitimes did not reach the succession of a foreign national.[^79] The Supreme Court also found no denial of due process. The petition for relief had been set for hearing repeatedly and Hermogenes presented no evidence. Under Rule 73, Section 1 the Manila court had jurisdiction because the decedent was an inhabitant of a foreign country who left estate in Manila. Hermogenes was also estopped from questioning that jurisdiction after invoking it for affirmative relief.[^80] The case shows that Philippine public policy on legitimes does not invalidate a foreign citizen's will that omits a Filipino compulsory heir.[^81]

### National Law Governing Renunciation of Foreign Citizenship

The Court of First Instance of Zamboanga del Norte granted Oh Hek How's naturalization petition on January 16, 1964. On January 17, 1966 he moved to take his oath of allegiance. On February 9, 1966 the trial court allowed the oath and a certificate of naturalization was issued to him that day. The Government gave notice of appeal and filed its record on appeal. Before the record was approved it moved to cancel the certificate because the order had not yet become final. On October 3, 1966 the trial court cancelled the certificate but authorized a new oath after thirty days from notice to the Solicitor General. On November 26, 1966 it approved the record on appeal and again authorized a new oath. Oh Hek How took that oath on November 28, 1966 and received another certificate.[^82]

The Supreme Court reversed the appealed order. It declared the November 28, 1966 oath of allegiance and the certificate null and void with costs against Oh Hek How. It directed him to surrender the certificate to the clerk of court within ten days after the decision became final.[^83] The February 9, 1966 order had not become final because of the Government's appeal. The trial court had also lost jurisdiction once the record on appeal was approved. His net income was P3,945.65 in 1960 and P5,105.79 in 1961. That income was not lucrative for a man with a wife and three children. His 1962 income was P6,485.50.[^84] It was conceded that he never obtained the permission of the Minister of the Interior of Nationalist China for the renunciation of his Chinese citizenship. Section 12 of Commonwealth Act No. 473 requires an applicant to renounce all foreign allegiance. The question of how a Chinese citizen may strip himself of that status is governed under Articles 15 and 16 of the Civil Code by the laws of China. A Chinese national therefore cannot be naturalized unless he has complied with the Chinese requirement of previous permission. The Supreme Court reiterated *Go A. Leng v. Republic*, as cited in [*Oh Hek How*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1969/aug1969/gr_l-27429_1969.html). That ruling superseded *Parado v. Republic*, *Chausintek v. Republic* and *Lim So v. Republic*.[^85] The case shows that Article 16 operates with Article 15 to refer the requisites for renouncing foreign citizenship in naturalization cases to the applicant's national law.[^86]

### Specific Scope of Article 16(2) Over Article 17(3)

Amos G. Bellis was born in Texas and was a citizen of Texas and of the United States. He had legitimate children by two marriages and three illegitimate children, Amos Bellis, Jr., Maria Cristina Bellis and Miriam Palma Bellis. On August 5, 1952 he executed a will in the Philippines. It gave $240,000.00 to his divorced first wife. It gave P120,000.00 to the three illegitimate children at P40,000.00 each. The remainder went in equal shares to his seven surviving legitimate children.[^87] Amos died on July 8, 1958 as a resident of San Antonio, Texas. The Court of First Instance of Manila admitted the will to probate on September 15, 1958. The executor, People's Bank and Trust Company, paid the legacies. On January 8, 1964 it filed its final account and project of partition. Maria Cristina and Miriam Palma opposed on January 17, 1964 because they were deprived of legitimes. Amos Bellis, Jr. did not oppose. On April 30, 1964 the probate court overruled the oppositions. It applied Texas law under Article 16 because Texas law provided no legitimes. It denied reconsideration on June 11, 1964.[^88]

The Supreme Court affirmed the probate court order *in toto* with costs against the appellants.[^89] The appeal raised a pure question of law. The appellants never invoked *renvoi*. Amos was both a national and a domiciliary of Texas. A Texas domiciliary rule would still point to Texas law. A Texas situs rule could cause *renvoi*. No Texas conflicts rule was proved and none is presumed to differ from Philippine rules.[^90] Article 16, paragraph 2 and Article 1039 make the national law govern four items. These are the order of succession, the amount of successional rights, the intrinsic validity of the will provisions and the capacity to succeed. The appellants invoked Article 17, paragraph 3. Congress deleted the saving clause when it moved old Article 11 into Article 17. It carried old Article 10 into Article 16 without substantial change. It added Article 1039. Article 16, paragraph 2 is therefore a specific provision that prevails over the general public policy clause. Whatever public policy may lie behind legitimes, Congress did not extend it to foreign nationals.[^91] The appellants also pointed to the separate Philippine will. Even if the testator meant Philippine law to govern, a clause directing distribution under Philippine law instead of national law is void under *Miciano v. Brimo*, as cited in [*Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html). The parties admitted that Texas law has no forced heirs. Philippine legitimes could not apply.[^92] The case shows that the public policy clause of Article 17(3) cannot override Article 16(2) to impose Philippine legitimes on a foreign decedent's estate.[^93]

### Foreign National Law Determining Heirship in Estate Administration

On December 7, 1957 Ngo The Hua claimed to be the surviving spouse of Chung Liu. She petitioned to be administratrix of his estate in Special Proceeding No. 1552-P of the Court of First Instance of Rizal, Pasay City Branch. Chung Kiat Hua and other alleged children by his first wife Tan Hua opposed. They said she was unfit. They also said she and Chung Liu, both Chinese citizens, had obtained a divorce confirmed by the Taipei District Court on August 25, 1955. They asked that Chung Kiat Hua be appointed. Ngo The Hua denied that they were his children.[^94] Chung Kiat Kang claimed to be a nephew. He opposed both applicants and sought appointment himself. He later waived his right to present evidence on the appointment. On December 2, 1959 the trial court found the Taipei divorce valid. It found that Chung Kiat Hua and the others were children of Chung Liu. It appointed Chung Kiat Hua administrator. Ngo The Hua withdrew her appeal after an amicable settlement and the Supreme Court granted that on June 26, 1961. Only the appeal of Chung Kiat Kang remained.[^95]

The Supreme Court affirmed the appealed order with costs against the appellants.[^96] Chung Kiat Kang argued that the trial court prejudged heirship before debts were paid, contrary to Rule 91, Section 1. The Supreme Court held that the rule bars distribution of the residue before the estate obligations are paid. It does not bar a declaration of heirs. The trial court had only decided the claimed relationships to apply the order of preference in Rule 79, Section 5. That finding was not a final determination of heirship for distribution.[^97] The Supreme Court then ruled on his standing. A person who intervenes in an administration proceeding must have an interest in the estate as heir or creditor under Rule 80, Section 4. Chung Kiat Kang did not claim to be a creditor. He was also not an heir under the Civil Code of the Republic of China in evidence as Exhibit 28. That law applied because Chung Liu was a Chinese citizen under Article 16. He could not be appointed co-administrator.[^98] The case shows that an alien decedent's national law under Article 16 determines who qualifies as an heir with standing to intervene in Philippine estate administration.[^99]

### Exclusion of Marital Property Relations From Successional Rights

Walter G. Stevenson was born in the Philippines in 1874 of British parents. He married Beatrice Mauricia Stevenson, another British subject, in Manila on January 23, 1909. They moved to San Francisco in 1945. Walter died there on February 22, 1951. His will of May 22, 1947 made Beatrice his sole heiress to the real and personal properties they acquired in the Philippines.[^100] Ancillary administration began in the Court of First Instance of Manila in May 1951. On December 1, 1952 Beatrice assigned her interests to Douglas and Bettina Fisher. The estate's second amended return of September 7, 1953 claimed a P4,000.00 deduction and an exemption for 210,000 Mindanao Mother Lode Mines shares. It sought a refund of P15,259.83 as overpaid tax. The Collector denied the claim and the Fishers sued. The case went to the Court of Tax Appeals. That court deducted Beatrice's one-half conjugal share and held the intangibles exempt. It denied the P4,000.00 deduction. It valued the Baguio land at P52,200.00 and the shares at P0.38 each. It allowed P2,000.00 for funeral expenses and P8,604.39 for judicial expenses. Both sides appealed.[^101]

The Supreme Court modified the Court of Tax Appeals judgment and affirmed it in all other respects without costs.[^102] Only Stevenson's one-half share in the conjugal property formed his hereditary estate. The intangibles were not exempt because there was no complete total reciprocity under section 122 of the National Internal Revenue Code. The estate had no P4,000.00 deduction. The mining shares were valued at P0.325 each. The P2,000.00 funeral expenses were deductible. The Supreme Court disallowed the P10,022.47 deduction for a $5,000.00 loan from the Bank of California. The Philippine probate court had not approved that claim. The estate also failed to state its gross estate outside the Philippines as section 89(d)(1) requires. It denied interest on any overpayment, citing *Collector of Internal Revenue v. St. Paul's Hospital*.[^103] The Collector had argued that English law gave the husband all marital acquisitions. He also cited Article 16 of the New Civil Code, or old Article 10. He offered no proof of English law beyond a bare allegation. The Supreme Court applied processual presumption and presumed English law to be the same as Philippine law. The 1909 marriage fell under old Article 1325 and not Article 124. Old Article 10 speaks of the amount of successional rights. That phrase means what each heir may inherit from the distributable estate. It does not govern the property relations of spouses. Those fall under Title VI, Chapter I of the new Civil Code. The lower court therefore correctly deducted the half of the conjugal property. The Supreme Court also accepted the testimony of a California lawyer as proof of the California tax law.[^104] The case shows the boundary between marital property liquidation and hereditary succession under Article 16.[^105]

## Key Cases

- [*Collector of Internal Revenue v. Fisher*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1961/jan1961/gr_l-11622_1961.html) (G.R. Nos. L-11622 and L-11668, January 28, 1961): In an estate tax refund suit for a British decedent married in Manila in 1909, the Court of Tax Appeals deducted the wife's one-half conjugal share and exempted intangibles. The Supreme Court held that Article 16 (old Article 10) does not govern marital property relations because "amount of successional rights" refers only to what an heir inherits from the distributable estate, applied processual presumption where English law was unproved and modified the Court of Tax Appeals judgment (confirming the one-half taxable share, denying intangible exemption and P4,000.00 estate tax deduction, valuing mining shares at P0.325, allowing P2,000.00 funeral expenses, disallowing a P10,022.47 loan deduction and denying interest on any overpaid amount, if any actually results after recomputation, without costs).[^127]
- [*Ngo The Hua v. Chung Kiat Hua*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1963/sep1963/gr_l-17091_1963.html) (G.R. No. L-17091, September 30, 1963): In administration proceedings for a deceased Chinese citizen, the trial court appointed one of his alleged children as administrator over the opposition of an alleged nephew who waived presenting evidence. Affirming the appealed order with costs against appellants, the Supreme Court held that under Article 16 of the New Civil Code the Civil Code of the Republic of China in evidence determined whether the nephew was an heir and barred his intervention as co-administrator under Rule 80, Section 4.[^128]
- [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html) (G.R. No. L-23678, June 6, 1967): Two illegitimate daughters of a Texas citizen and domiciliary opposed the project of partition under his Philippine will for depriving them of legitimes, which the Manila probate court overruled. Affirming the probate order *in toto* with costs against appellants, the Supreme Court held that Articles 16(2) and 1039 apply national law to four succession items, that Article 16(2) is a specific rule prevailing over Article 17(3), that *renvoi* did not arise without proof of a Texas situs conflicts rule and that a separate Philippine will cannot substitute Philippine law for national law.[^129]
- [*Oh Hek How v. Republic*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1969/aug1969/gr_l-27429_1969.html) (G.R. No. L-27429, August 27, 1969): After the Government appealed a naturalization oath order, the trial court approved the record on appeal yet authorized a new oath taken on November 28, 1966 without prior Chinese renunciation approval. Reversing the order, declaring the oath and certificate null and void with costs against petitioner and directing surrender of the certificate within ten days after finality, the Supreme Court held under Articles 15 and 16 that Chinese law governed how a Chinese citizen strips himself of Chinese nationality, alongside finding lack of jurisdiction and non-lucrative income.[^130]
- [*Cayetano v. Leonidas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html) (G.R. No. L-54919, May 30, 1984): After withdrawing opposition to the reprobate of his American daughter's Pennsylvania will, a Filipino father sought relief claiming deprivation of legitime, which the Manila court denied. Dismissing the petition for certiorari and prohibition for lack of merit, the Supreme Court held that under Articles 16(2) and 1039 the admitted Pennsylvania law without legitimes governed intrinsic validity despite apparent preterition and upheld Manila probate jurisdiction under Rule 73, Section 1.[^131]
- [*Llorente v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38202) (G.R. No. 124371, November 23, 2000): Lower courts disregarded the California divorce and will of a naturalized American citizen by applying Philippine legitimes and co-ownership under Article 144. Granting the petition, setting aside the Court of Appeals decision, reversing the trial court, recognizing the December 4, 1952 California divorce and remanding for determination of intrinsic validity and successional rights with proof of foreign law (no costs), the Supreme Court held that Article 16 points to the law of the state of which the decedent was a resident rather than any single American law and found no showing that *renvoi* was required.[^132]
- [*Ancheta v. Guersey-Dalaygon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349) (G.R. No. 139868, June 8, 2006): An ancillary administrator obtained final Pasig probate orders distributing an American citizen's Philippine estate under Philippine legitimes instead of introducing Maryland law, which the Court of Appeals annulled for extrinsic fraud. Denying the petition, affirming the Court of Appeals Decision and Resolution and admonishing petitioner with no pronouncement as to costs, the Supreme Court held that Maryland law governed under Articles 16 and 1039, that omitting to prove Maryland law breached fiduciary duty as extrinsic fraud and that judicial notice of undisputed Maryland law in the record gave the entire Makati property to the Filipino widow-heir.[^133]
- [*Noveras v. Noveras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57649) (G.R. No. 188289, August 20, 2014): Naturalized American spouses litigated judicial separation of properties located in California and the Philippines after an unproved California divorce, with the Court of Appeals dividing Philippine assets equally, ordering David to pay Leticia P1,040,000.00 from the Sampaloc proceeds and ordering each spouse to pay P520,000.00 in presumptive legitimes. Denying the husband's petition and affirming the Court of Appeals decision, the Supreme Court held under Article 16 that Philippine courts had no jurisdiction over California properties and confined liquidation to Philippine assets.[^134]
- [*Heirs of Sadhwani v. Sadhwani*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65628) (G.R. No. 217365, August 14, 2019): Children of Indian nationals sued in Makati Regional Trial Court claiming inheritance over Makati land and a condominium unit under Civil Code succession rules. After the trial court dismissed the complaint without prejudice, the Supreme Court dismissed the direct Rule 45 petition as the wrong remedy that also skipped the hierarchy of courts, affirmed the trial court resolutions and held that Indian nationals could neither own nor transmit Philippine land under Article XII, Section 7 of the Constitution and that under Article 16 the plaintiffs failed to state a cause of action over the condominium unit by failing to plead Indian succession law.[^135]
- [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229) (G.R. No. 229010, November 23, 2020): An Iriga City trial court *motu proprio* dismissed a petition to probate an American citizen's will executed in Iriga City for lack of prior probate in the United States under Article 16. Granting the petition, reversing and setting aside the assailed Orders and remanding for further proceedings, the Supreme Court held that Article 16(2) governs intrinsic validity and succession issues rather than extrinsic probate formalities, which Articles 17, 816 and 817 place under Philippine law by default.[^136]

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1:** A citizen and resident of Texas executed a will in the Philippines leaving specific legacies to his three illegitimate children and the entire residuary estate to his seven legitimate children. Under Texas law, there are no forced heirs or legitimes. The illegitimate children oppose the project of partition in the Philippine probate court, claiming that Philippine public policy under Article 17, paragraph 3 requires awarding them their legitimes over properties situated in the Philippines. Should the opposition succeed?

No. Under Article 16, paragraph 2 and Article 1039 of the Civil Code, as explained in [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html),[^137] the order of succession, the amount of successional rights, the intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions and the capacity to succeed are governed by the decedent's national law regardless of the nature or location of the property. Congress deliberately deleted the saving clause from old Article 11 when enacting Article 17 so that Article 16, paragraph 2 stands as a specific provision prevailing over general public-policy clauses. Executing a will in the Philippines cannot substitute Philippine legitimes for Texas law.[^138]

**Question 2:** A former Filipino citizen became naturalized as an American citizen in New York, obtained a final California divorce, remarried and executed a will in the Philippines disposing of all his Philippine properties in favor of his second wife and their children. The trial court applied Philippine law to void the intrinsic dispositions, asserting that general American law follows the domiciliary theory and triggers *renvoi* back to Philippine law. Was the trial court correct?

No. As held in [*Llorente v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38202),[^139] there is no single American law governing testamentary dispositions, so "national law" in Article 16 refers to the specific law of the state of which the decedent was a resident. Because foreign laws and foreign conflict-of-laws rules do not prove themselves and must be alleged and proved, the trial court erred in assuming *renvoi* without proof that the applicable state law required it and erred in applying Philippine legitimes to a foreign citizen's estate.[^140]

**Question 3:** Spouses who were married in the Philippines later became American citizens residing in California and acquired real and personal properties in both California and the Philippines. In a Philippine petition for judicial separation of property filed after separation in fact for over one year, the husband asks the Philippine court to include all California properties in the liquidation to avoid unjust enrichment. May the Philippine court liquidate the California assets?

No. In [*Noveras v. Noveras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57649),[^141] the Supreme Court held under Article 16, paragraph 1 of the Civil Code that real property as well as personal property is subject to the law of the country where it is situated. Philippine courts therefore acquire no jurisdiction over properties situated in California. Judicial liquidation of the absolute community must be confined strictly to the properties located in the Philippines.[^142]

# Quotes

> "We agree with the appellate court that the Philippine courts did not acquire jurisdiction over the California properties of David and Leticia. Indeed, Article 16 of the Civil Code clearly states that real property as well as personal property is subject to the law of the country where it is situated. Thus, liquidation shall only be limited to the Philippine properties."[^143]

> "Nor do we believe petitioner can make use of Article 16 of the New Civil Code (art. 10, old Civil Code) to bolster his stand. A reading of Article 10 of the old Civil Code, which incidentally is the one applicable, shows that it does not encompass or contemplate to govern the question of property relation between spouses. Said article distinctly speaks of amount of successional rights and this term, in speaks in our opinion, properly refers to the extent or amount of property that each heir is legally entitled to inherit from the estate available for distribution. It needs to be pointed out that the property relation of spouses, as distinguished from their successional rights, is governed differently by the specific and express provisions of Title VI, Chapter I of our new Civil Code (Title III, Chapter I of the old Civil Code.) We, therefore, find that the lower court correctly deducted the half of the conjugal property in determining the hereditary estate left by the deceased Stevenson."[^144]

> "Article 16, par. 2, and Art. 1039 of the Civil Code, render applicable the national law of the decedent, in intestate or testamentary successions, with regard to four items: (a) the order of succession; (b) the amount of successional rights; (e) the intrinsic validity of the provisions of the will; and (d) the capacity to succeed."[^145]

> "It is therefore evident that whatever public policy or good customs may be involved in our System of legitimes, Congress has not intended to extend the same to the succession of foreign nationals. For it has specifically chosen to leave, inter alia, the amount of successional rights, to the decedent's national law. Specific provisions must prevail over general ones."[^146]

> "First, there is no such thing as one American law. The "national law" indicated in Article 16 of the Civil Code cannot possibly apply to general American law. There is no such law governing the validity of testamentary provisions in the United States. Each State of the union has its own law applicable to its citizens and in force only within the State. It can therefore refer to no other than the law of the State of which the decedent was a resident. Second, there is no showing that the application of the renvoi doctrine is called for or required by New York State law."[^147]

> "As the Sps. Sadhwani were Indian nationals, the laws of succession under the Civil Code do not apply. Therefore, the complaint should have alleged, at the very least, that petitioners were legal heirs of their parents and were entitled to inherit the Ritz Condominium Unit under the laws of the Republic of India. In view of the foregoing provision, the Court holds that petitioner cannot sidestep their burden of sufficiently pleading and eventually proving a cause of action under foreign law even when claiming under Philippine law may be more favorable or expedient. As they failed to sufficiently allege the basis for their right under the national law of their parents, petitioners failed to state a cause of action over the condominium unit."[^148]

> "While foreign laws do not prove themselves in our jurisdiction and our courts are not authorized to take judicial notice of them; however, petitioner, as ancillary administrator of Audrey's estate, was duty-bound to introduce in evidence the pertinent law of the State of Maryland."[^149]

> "As such, he occupies a position of the highest trust and confidence, and he is required to exercise reasonable diligence and act in entire good faith in the performance of that trust."[^150]

> "However, the probate of a will only involves its extrinsic validity and does not delve into its intrinsic validity, unless there are exceptional circumstances which would require the probate court to touch upon the intrinsic validity of the will."[^151]

> "The nationality principle is not applied when determining the extrinsic validity of an alien's last will and testament. When it comes to the probate of an alien's will, whether executed here or abroad, the alien's national law may be pleaded and proved before the probate court. Otherwise, Philippine law will govern by default."[^152]

> "It is a settled rule that as regards the intrinsic validity of the provisions of the will, as provided for by Article 16(2) and 1039 of the Civil Code, the national law of the decedent must apply."[^153]

> "As a consequence, a Chinese national cannot be naturalized as a citizen of the Philippines, unless he has complied with the laws of Nationalist China requiring previous permission of its Minister of the Interior for the renunciation of nationality."[^154]

# Tips

### Diagnostic Checklist

1. **Classify the issue before choosing the connecting factor:** Distinguish property location and court jurisdiction over real or personal assets under Article 16(1) ([*Noveras v. Noveras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57649)) from the four succession matters governed by national law under Article 16(2) and Article 1039 ([*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html)).
2. **Exclude non-Article 16 issues early:** Separate spousal property relations under Title VI, Chapter I ([*Collector of Internal Revenue v. Fisher*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1961/jan1961/gr_l-11622_1961.html)) and extrinsic will formalities under Articles 17, 816 and 817 ([*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229)) from Article 16(2).
3. **Fix citizenship and constituent state law at the relevant date:** For federal countries without a single national succession statute, identify the exact constituent state of residence or domicile ([*Llorente v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38202); [*Ancheta v. Guersey-Dalaygon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349); [*Cayetano v. Leonidas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html)).
4. **Check pleading and proof under Rule 132:** Verify whether foreign substantive law and any foreign conflicts rule (*renvoi*) were properly pleaded and proved rather than left to assumption ([*Heirs of Sadhwani v. Sadhwani*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65628); [*Noveras v. Noveras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57649)).

### Common Traps

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Trap:** Do not invoke Article 17, paragraph 3 on prohibitive public-policy laws to apply Philippine legitimes to a foreign decedent's estate. In [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html) and [*Cayetano v. Leonidas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html), the Supreme Court held that Article 16, paragraph 2 is a specific provision that prevails over general public-policy rules, even when a foreign decedent executed a separate Philippine will or omitted a Filipino compulsory heir.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practice Trap:** Do not dismiss or delay probate of an alien's will executed in the Philippines on the theory that Article 16 requires prior probate in the alien's home country. As held in [*Gaspi v. Pacis-Trinidad*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67229), probate addresses extrinsic validity under Philippine law by default, while ancillary administrators still bear a strict fiduciary duty to introduce foreign national law when distributing the estate ([*Ancheta v. Guersey-Dalaygon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349)).

### What Worked and What Failed

Claims and defenses succeeded when parties proved foreign law, matched the issue to the correct rule or respected territorial limits. In [*Bellis v. Bellis*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1967/jun1967/gr_l-23678_1967.html) and [*Cayetano v. Leonidas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/may1984/gr_l-54919_1984.html), Texas and Pennsylvania succession laws omitting legitimes were upheld. In [*Ngo The Hua v. Chung Kiat Hua*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1963/sep1963/gr_l-17091_1963.html), proving Chinese succession law excluded an alleged nephew from estate administration. In [*Collector of Internal Revenue v. Fisher*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1961/jan1961/gr_l-11622_1961.html), distinguishing marital property liquidation from successional rights preserved the surviving spouse's one-half conjugal deduction.

Arguments consistently failed when parties cited Article 16 outside its scope, left foreign law unproved or assumed *renvoi* without proof. The husband in [*Noveras v. Noveras*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57649) could not pull California properties into Philippine liquidation or rely on an unauthenticated divorce decree. The heirs in [*Heirs of Sadhwani v. Sadhwani*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65628) had their complaint dismissed without prejudice for failing to plead Indian law and claiming land barred to aliens. The ancillary administrator in [*Ancheta v. Guersey-Dalaygon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41349) suffered annulment of final distribution orders and admonition for relying on processual presumption instead of proving Maryland law, while the naturalization applicant in [*Oh Hek How v. Republic*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1969/aug1969/gr_l-27429_1969.html) lost his naturalization certificate for lacking Nationalist China's renunciation permission.
