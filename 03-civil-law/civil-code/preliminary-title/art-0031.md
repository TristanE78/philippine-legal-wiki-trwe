> ART. 31. When the civil action is based on an obligation not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony, such civil action may proceed independently of the criminal proceedings and regardless of the result of the latter.[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 31 of the Civil Code establishes the substantive rule of autonomy for civil actions founded on obligations that exist independently of criminal liability. Whenever a single act or omission gives rise to a criminal prosecution and also breaches a distinct non-delictual duty under Article 1157 of the Civil Code such as contract, quasi-delict or human-relations law, the civil suit moves forward on its own track without awaiting the outcome of the criminal trial.

**In brief:**

1. **Separate source of obligation:** Article 31 applies when the plaintiff sues on an obligation not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony, including *culpa contractual*, quasi-delict (*culpa aquiliana*) and independent human-relations duties.
2. **Procedural independence:** Under the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure, the independent civil action requires no prior reservation in the criminal case, is not suspended by the criminal proceedings and may proceed simultaneously regardless of criminal conviction or acquittal.
3. **Statutory and procedural ceilings:** Article 31 does not apply where the purported contract itself is assailed as illegal *per se* under a prohibitory penal law or where a special procedural rule for Batas Pambansa Blg. 22 (B.P. 22) merges the civil claim into a previously filed criminal prosecution.
4. **Single-recovery safeguard:** Although civil liability *ex delicto* and independent civil liabilities may be pursued concurrently, Article 2177 of the Civil Code bars the injured party from recovering damages twice for the same act or omission.

## Scope and Statutory Architecture: Obligations Not Arising from a Felony

A single damage-causing act or omission may generate two distinct categories of civil liability on the part of the offender. The first is civil liability *ex delicto* under Article 100 of the Revised Penal Code, which attaches directly to the commission of a crime and is impliedly instituted with the criminal action. The second is independent civil liability, which rests on a separate legal obligation rather than on criminal guilt. In [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^2], [*Spouses Santos v. Pizardo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53406)[^3], [*Safeguard Security Agency, Inc. v. Tangco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40427)[^4] and [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074)[^5], the Supreme Court organized independent civil liabilities into two groups: obligations not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony under Article 31 (such as *culpa contractual*, obligations arising from law, intentional torts under Articles 32 and 34 and *culpa aquiliana* under Article 2176) and civil actions treated as independent by specific statutory command under Article 33 for defamation, fraud and physical injuries. The Supreme Court drew the same line between civil liability *ex delicto* and quasi-delict in [*L.G. Foods Corp. v. Pagapong-Agraviador*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42999)[^6], though that decision does not name Article 31. The same taxonomy appears in [*Supreme Transportation Liner, Inc. v. San Andres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64620)[^7] and [*Fegarido v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68422)[^8] quoting [*Safeguard Security Agency, Inc. v. Tangco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40427)[^9] and in [*Hao v. Lagahid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70070)[^10] quoting [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^11].

Article 31 connects directly to Article 1157 of the Civil Code, which enumerates five sources of obligations: law, contracts, quasi-contracts, acts or omissions punished by law and quasi-delicts. As the Supreme Court explained in [*Cinco v. Canonoy*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/may1979/gr_l-33171_1979.html)[^12] and [*Heirs of Pedro Tayag, Sr. v. Alcantara*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/jul1980/gr_50959_1980.html)[^13], Article 31 refers to a civil action predicated not on the felony charged in the criminal case, but on an obligation arising from one of those other non-delictual sources. Whether a complaint pleads civil liability *ex delicto* or an independent civil action is determined by the actual factual averments in the pleading rather than the plaintiff's own legal label or the pendency of a criminal prosecution, as confirmed in [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^14], [*L.G. Foods Corp. v. Pagapong-Agraviador*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42999)[^15] and [*Safeguard Security Agency, Inc. v. Tangco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40427)[^16].

| Feature | Civil Liability *Ex Delicto* (RPC Art. 100) | Independent Civil Action Under Civil Code Art. 31 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source of Obligation** | Acts or omissions punished by law (felony or crime) | Non-felony obligations under Art. 1157 (contracts, quasi-delicts, law, human relations) |
| **Relation to Criminal Action** | Impliedly instituted with the criminal case unless waived, reserved or filed ahead | Entirely separate and distinct from the criminal proceedings |
| **Prior Reservation Under 2000 Rule 111** | Needed to sue separately | Not required |
| **Effect of Criminal Acquittal** | Extinguished if the judgment declares that the act or omission did not exist | Proceeds regardless of the criminal result, subject to the limits discussed below |
| **Effect of Accused's Death Before Final Judgment** | Extinguished together with criminal liability under RPC Art. 89 | Survives if based on another source of obligation. It is enforced against the estate or the executor or administrator, or inside a B.P. 22 case |
| **Employer Liability** | Subsidiary under RPC Art. 103 (requires conviction and insolvency of the employee) | Direct, primary and solidary under Art. 2180 |
| **Double Recovery Rule** | Barred by Art. 2177 | Barred by Art. 2177 |

## Contractual Obligations (*Ex Contractu*) Proceeding Independently of Criminal Cases

Breach of contract is the quintessential non-delictual obligation governed by Article 31. Because contractual rights and duties arise from the agreement of the parties under Article 1157 rather than from a penal statute, an offended party may enforce the contract in a civil court even while criminal proceedings arising from the same transaction are pending or after the accused is acquitted. The Supreme Court has applied this principle across employment contracts of services in [*Caños v. Peralta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/aug1982/gr_l-38352_1982.html)[^17], Letter of Credit and Trust Receipt agreements in [*Vintola v. Insular Bank of Asia and America*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/may1987/gr_l-73271_1987.html)[^18] and [*Sarmiento, Jr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51766)[^19], check-for-cash credit transactions in [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^20], real-estate option and loan agreements in [*Frias v. San Diego-Sison*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42035)[^21], contracts of carriage in [*Heirs of Jose Marcial K. Ochoa v. G & S Transport Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52995)[^22], commercial sales of cement withdrawal authorities in [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074)[^23] and breaches of banking agency duties in [*Salta v. De Veyra*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/sep1982/gr_l-37733_1982.html)[^24].

Supporting rulings sharpen both the reach and the corporate boundaries of contractual independence. In [*Sarmiento, Jr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51766), the Supreme Court directly applied Article 31 to affirm a bank's separate collection judgment for P495,000.00 principal plus legal interest and P49,500.00 attorney's fees against a trust-receipt entrustee and his solidary surety even though the surety had been dropped from the Presidential Decree No. 115 criminal case and the entrustee had been convicted without any civil award.[^25] Similarly, in [*Tupaz IV v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42196), the Supreme Court confirmed that acquittal on reasonable doubt in a Presidential Decree No. 115 estafa prosecution did not extinguish the contractual civil liability of a corporate officer who signed one trust receipt in his personal capacity, while absolving officers who signed a second trust receipt purely in their corporate capacities.[^26] In [*Salta v. De Veyra*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/sep1982/gr_l-37733_1982.html), the Supreme Court invoked Article 31 alongside Article 33 fraud and agency-negligence principles to hold that a bank branch manager's acquittal on demurrer to evidence in a Republic Act No. 3019 criminal case did not bar two separate civil suits filed by the bank to recover losses from irregular loans.[^27]

Two corollary limits protect the boundary between contractual civil actions and criminal courts. First, where the criminal court acquits the accused because the disputed debt arises purely from a civil contract rather than a criminal offense, the criminal court cannot adjudicate or award that contractual debt inside the criminal judgment. In [*Murao v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57909), the Supreme Court acquitted the petitioners of estafa over a sales agent's unpaid commission and set aside the P154,500.00 civil commission award, holding that contractual liability arising from a contract of agency must be litigated in a separate and independent civil action.[^28] Second, independent contractual liability cannot survive if the underlying contract debt has already been fully extinguished by payment. In [*Nuguid v. Nicdao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42465), the Supreme Court affirmed the accused's acquittal without civil liability because clear and convincing evidence proved she had already paid P6,980,000.00 against a P1,150,000.00 loan with no written stipulation for interest under Article 1956.[^29]

### [*Caños v. Peralta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/aug1982/gr_l-38352_1982.html): Contract of Services and Minimum Wage Prosecutions

[*Caños v. Peralta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/aug1982/gr_l-38352_1982.html)[^30] is the foundational authority distinguishing an employee's contractual wage suit from a criminal prosecution under labor legislation. Employer Adela J. Caños was charged in Criminal Case No. 326 before the Court of First Instance of Davao del Sur with violating Section 3(a) of Republic Act No. 602 (the Minimum Wage Law) in relation to Section 15(a). Employee Rolando Apas subsequently filed Civil Case No. 558 in the same court seeking wage differentials, overtime pay, termination pay and damages without reinstatement. Judge E.L. Peralta ordered the consolidation and joint trial of the criminal and civil cases, which Caños challenged by certiorari on the ground that Rule 111, Section 3(a) and (b) required suspending the civil action pending the criminal trial.

The Supreme Court dismissed the petition with costs against Caños and directed the lower court to proceed with the joint trial without unnecessary delay.[^31] Directly quoting and applying Article 31 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that Civil Case No. 558 was essentially an action to enforce an obligation *ex contractu* arising from the parties' contract of services rather than civil liability arising from the statutory criminal offense of non-payment of the minimum wage.[^32] Consequently, Rule 111's suspension bar did not apply. The trial court did not abuse its discretion under Rule 31, Section 1 in ordering a consolidated joint trial because both cases stemmed from the same transaction, presented common factual questions and relied on substantially the same evidence without prejudicing substantial rights.[^33] The ruling resolved only non-suspension and joint trial without adjudicating the merits or amounts of the employee's wage claims.[^34]

### [*Vintola v. Insular Bank of Asia and America*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/may1987/gr_l-73271_1987.html): Letter of Credit and Trust Receipt Obligations

[*Vintola v. Insular Bank of Asia and America*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/may1987/gr_l-73271_1987.html)[^35] established the governing rule that civil collection suits on Letters of Credit and Trust Receipts proceed independently under Article 31 even after an acquittal for estafa. Spouses Tirso I. Vintola and Loreto Dy Vintola obtained a P40,000.00 domestic Letter of Credit from Insular Bank of Asia and America (IBAA) in Cebu City to purchase seashells, posting an initial P8,000.00 marginal deposit later raised to P11,000.00 and executing a Trust Receipt due on October 19, 1975. Unable to sell the seashells, they offered to return them to the bank. IBAA refused and charged them with estafa. On April 12, 1982, Branch VII of the Court of First Instance of Cebu acquitted the spouses for lack of misappropriation or conversion and remarked that the bank's remedy was civil and not criminal in nature. IBAA then sued for collection before Branch XVI of the Regional Trial Court of Cebu, which initially dismissed the complaint but on reconsideration ordered the spouses jointly and severally to pay IBAA P72,982.27 with 14% per annum interest and 1% per annum service charge from judicial demand until fully paid, plus P4,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of suit. The Intermediate Appellate Court certified the appeal to the Supreme Court on pure questions of law.

The Supreme Court affirmed the judgment appealed from with no costs.[^36] Explaining that a Letter of Credit and Trust Receipt transaction combines a loan feature and a security feature giving the bank only a security interest under Presidential Decree No. 115 rather than real ownership, the Supreme Court held that surrendering unsold goods does not extinguish the entrustees' contractual loan obligation.[^37] Directly quoting and applying Article 31 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court ruled that the spouses' liability arose *ex contractu* from breach of the Letter of Credit and Trust Receipt regardless of whether they committed criminal misappropriation or conversion, so their acquittal in the estafa case was no bar to IBAA's civil collection action.[^38] The Supreme Court added that the criminal court's remark that the bank's remedy was civil amounted to an implied reservation, while limiting its holding to *ex contractu* collection after an estafa acquittal without addressing civil liability *ex delicto* from actual misappropriation.[^39]

### [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647): Substance Over Label in Contractual Collection Suits

[*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^40] synthesized the dual-liability framework under the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure and established that the substance of the complaint controls over a party's erroneous label. Jose S. Cancio, Jr., operator of Cancio's Money Exchange, exchanged cash for three Interbank checks issued by Merenciana Isip totaling P190,000.00 (P80,000.00, P80,000.00 and P30,000.00) that were later dishonored. Three B.P. 22 complaints were dismissed (one by the Provincial Prosecutor for late deposit and two by Branch 1 of the Municipal Trial Court of Guagua, Pampanga for failure to prosecute). Three estafa cases (Criminal Cases Nos. G-3611 to G-3613) were dismissed on October 21, 1997 by Branch 49 of the Regional Trial Court of Pampanga upon the prosecution's motion without prejudice to refiling the civil aspect. When Cancio filed Civil Case No. G-3272 before the same Branch 49 for collection of sum of money praying for P190,000.00 principal with legal interest, P30,000.00 attorney's fees plus P1,000.00 per court appearance and P20,000.00 litigation expenses, the trial court dismissed the complaint on March 20, 1998 and denied reconsideration on June 1, 1998 on grounds of *res judicata* and forum-shopping.

The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the March 20, 1998 and June 1, 1998 orders of Branch 49 of the Regional Trial Court of Pampanga and remanded the case for further proceedings.[^41] Applying the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure retroactively as remedial law, the Supreme Court held that independent civil actions under Articles 31, 32, 33, 34 and 2176 of the Civil Code may be filed separately and prosecuted independently even without any reservation in the criminal action.[^42] Relying on Article 31 and [*Vintola v. Insular Bank of Asia and America*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/may1987/gr_l-73271_1987.html)[^43], the Supreme Court examined the factual allegations of Cancio's complaint and held that his claim to recover the value of checks exchanged for cash stated a cause of action for *culpa contractual* under Article 31 even though Cancio himself mislabeled it as arising from delict and even though the October 21, 1997 criminal order reserved only civil liability *ex delicto*.[^44] Because the *ex contractu* collection suit was distinct from the dismissed estafa cases, neither *res judicata* nor forum-shopping barred it, though the Supreme Court did not adjudicate Isip's actual monetary liability on remand and noted that double recovery remained prohibited under Article 2177.[^45]

### [*Frias v. San Diego-Sison*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42035): Loan Contracts and Collateral Perjury Acquittal

[*Frias v. San Diego-Sison*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42035)[^46] demonstrates that Article 31 shields a contractual collection suit and its accompanying moral and exemplary damages from an acquittal in collateral criminal charges. Under a December 7, 1990 Memorandum of Agreement over a Muntinlupa house and lot covered by Transfer Certificate of Title No. 168173, Flora San Diego-Sison paid Bobie Rose V. Frias P2,000,000.00 in cash (alongside a P1,000,000.00 check that went stale) with a six-month option to buy the property for P6,400,000.00, stipulating that if San Diego-Sison declined to buy, the amount would be treated as a loan secured by the property with compounded bank interest for the second six months. San Diego-Sison declined the purchase, but Frias failed to repay and instead executed a false affidavit of loss before Branch 142 of the Regional Trial Court of Makati to secure a new owner's duplicate title despite knowing San Diego-Sison held the original. Although Frias was later acquitted in criminal prosecutions for perjury and false testimony, Branch 30 of the Regional Trial Court of Manila in Civil Case No. 93-65367 ordered Frias on January 31, 1996 to pay P2,000,000.00 with 32% per annum interest from December 7, 1991, P70,000.00 in attachment bond premiums (P30,000.00 in 1993, P20,000.00 in 1994 and P20,000.00 in 1995) with legal interest from January 31, 1996, P100,000.00 in moral, corrective and exemplary damages and P100,000.00 attorney's fees plus costs of litigation. On June 18, 2002, the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CV No. 52839 modified the interest rate to 25% per annum effective June 7, 1991 and denied reconsideration on September 11, 2002.

The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution with modification deleting the P100,000.00 award of attorney's fees and made no pronouncement as to costs.[^47] Directly quoting and applying Article 31 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that San Diego-Sison's collection suit rested on breach of the contractual loan under the Memorandum of Agreement rather than on the alleged crimes of perjury and false testimony, so Frias's criminal acquittal did not bar the civil award of P100,000.00 moral, corrective and exemplary damages for her fraudulent attempt to deprive her lender of security.[^48] The Supreme Court also upheld continuing stipulated monetary interest after maturity while correcting the appellate court's statement that loans always bear interest under Article 1956. It deleted the P100,000.00 attorney's fees because the trial court failed to explain any legal basis for it in the body of its decision.[^49]

### [*Heirs of Jose Marcial K. Ochoa v. G & S Transport Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52995): Breach of Contract of Carriage

[*Heirs of Jose Marcial K. Ochoa v. G & S Transport Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52995)[^50] is the flagship Article 31 ruling for common-carrier liability. Passenger Jose Marcial K. Ochoa died when an Avis taxicab operated by common carrier G & S Transport Corporation and driven by Bibiano Padilla, Jr. rammed the Santolan fly-over railing along EDSA and plunged below. In the criminal case for reckless imprudence resulting in homicide, the Municipal Trial Court convicted Padilla, but the Regional Trial Court reversed on appeal and acquitted him. Meanwhile, in the heirs' civil action for breach of contract of carriage (and alternatively quasi-delict), Branch 164 of the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City ruled on December 27, 2001 (as corrected in its March 5, 2002 order) that G & S breached its contract of carriage and ordered G & S to pay P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P6,537,244.96 loss of earning capacity, P300,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages, P100,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of litigation. On June 29, 2005 (reconsideration denied October 12, 2005), the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CV No. 75602 affirmed liability, reduced moral damages to P200,000.00, retained P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P50,000.00 exemplary damages, P100,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of litigation and deleted the P6,537,244.96 loss of earning capacity award.

The Supreme Court partly granted the heirs' petition in G.R. No. 170071, denied G & S's petition in G.R. No. 170125 and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution with modifications restoring and recomputing loss of earning capacity to P6,611,634.59 and reducing moral damages to P100,000.00.[^51] Quoting Article 31 of the Civil Code verbatim, the Supreme Court held that the heirs' action for damages arising from breach of contract of carriage was an independent *culpa contractual* suit wholly separate from the criminal case against driver Padilla, so Padilla's acquittal by the Regional Trial Court had no bearing on the resolution of the civil case against G & S.[^52] Because G & S failed to overcome the statutory presumption of negligence of a common carrier and because the wife's testimony that Ochoa earned around P450,000.00 a year was corroborated by an official United States Agency for International Development (USAID) certification (distinguishing unsupported testimonial claims such as the P8,000.00 monthly income in *Pleyto v. Lomboy* and the P83,088.00 annual salary in *Victory Liner, Inc. v. Gammad*, as cited in [*Heirs of Jose Marcial K. Ochoa v. G & S Transport Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52995)[^53]), the Supreme Court reinstated loss of earning capacity at P6,611,634.59 alongside P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages, P100,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of litigation.[^54]

### [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074): Simultaneous Pursuit of Contractual and *Ex Delicto* Remedies

[*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074)[^55] settled whether an injured contracting party commits forum shopping or triggers *litis pendentia* by pursuing an appeal on the civil aspect of an estafa case while simultaneously prosecuting a separate Article 31 civil action. Kou Co Ping (Charlie Co) purchased withdrawal authorities for 50,000 bags of cement for P3.15 million (P63.00 per bag) and sold them to Lily Lim for P3.2 million (P64.00 per bag). After Lim withdrew 2,800 bags and resold 10,000 bags to Co, the cement plant refused to release the remaining 37,200 bags. An estafa information was filed in Branch 154 of the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City (Criminal Case No. 116377) alleging receipt of P2,380,800.00 for the 37,200 bags and misappropriation of P2,300,800.00. The Pasig trial court acquitted Co on demurrer to evidence for insufficiency of evidence and later ruled on December 1, 2004 (reconsideration denied February 21, 2005) that Co was not civilly liable. While Lim's appeal of that civil-aspect order (CA-G.R. CV No. 85138) was pending before the Second Division of the Court of Appeals, she filed Civil Case No. 05-112396 in Branch 21 of the Regional Trial Court of Manila for specific performance and damages based on breach of contract and abuse of rights against Co and several corporations. On October 20, 2005, the Second Division dismissed her appeal for forum shopping and *litis pendentia*, whereas on April 10, 2007 the Seventeenth Division in CA-G.R. SP No. 93395 upheld Branch 21's refusal to dismiss the Manila civil action.

The Supreme Court granted Lim's petition in G.R. No. 175256, reversed and set aside the October 20, 2005 Resolution of the Second Division and reinstated her appeal in CA-G.R. CV No. 85138, while denying Co's petition in G.R. No. 179160 and affirming *in toto* the April 10, 2007 Decision of the Seventeenth Division remanding Civil Case No. 05-112396 to Branch 21 of the Regional Trial Court of Manila.[^56] Quoting Article 31 verbatim, the Supreme Court held that a civil suit grounded on breach of contract of sale and abuse of rights is separate, distinct and independent from the civil liability *ex delicto* impliedly instituted in an estafa prosecution.[^57] Because the two civil liabilities rest on distinct causes of action, the offended party may pursue both simultaneously or cumulatively without violating the rules on forum shopping, *litis pendentia* or *res judicata*, subject only to the Article 2177 prohibition against recovering damages twice for the same act or omission.[^58] The Supreme Court decided only procedural propriety and did not adjudicate monetary liability on the merits in either pending proceeding.[^59]

## Quasi-Delict (*Culpa Aquiliana*) and Human-Relations Obligations Under Article 31

In Philippine tort jurisprudence, Article 31 operates in tandem with Articles 1157, 2176, 2177 and 2180 of the Civil Code to guarantee that a victim's action for quasi-delict (*culpa aquiliana*) proceeds independently of any criminal prosecution for reckless imprudence or homicide. Because quasi-delict is an independent source of obligation not arising from a felony, it covers both injuries to persons and damage to property ([*Cinco v. Canonoy*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/may1979/gr_l-33171_1979.html)[^60]), cannot be suspended under Rule 111 during a pending criminal case ([*Lanuzo v. Sy Bon Ping*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/sep1980/gr_53064_1980.html)[^61], [*Bordas v. Canadalla*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/apr1988/gr_l-30036_1988.html)[^62]), requires no prior reservation under the 2000 Revised Rules ([*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368)[^63], [*Supreme Transportation Liner, Inc. v. San Andres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64620)[^64]) and imposes direct, primary and solidary liability upon employers under Article 2180 rather than subsidiary liability under Article 103 of the Revised Penal Code ([*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html)[^65], [*Safeguard Security Agency, Inc. v. Tangco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40427)[^66], [*Heirs of Catalina P. Mendoza v. ES Trucking and Forwarders*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66172)[^67]).

Across five decades of supporting decisions, the Supreme Court has reinforced every practical facet of this rule. In [*Manio v. Gaddi*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1972/mar1972/gr_l_30860_1972.html), the En Banc Supreme Court (quoting *Azucena v. Potenciano* on Article 31) held that a minor driver's rushed guilty plea and P6,000.00 criminal indemnity order could not bar unnotified heirs from obtaining a solidary P11,300.00 quasi-delict judgment against the driver, his parents and his employers.[^68] In [*Lanuzo v. Sy Bon Ping*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/sep1980/gr_53064_1980.html), the Supreme Court affirmed a solidary default judgment for P13,000.00 actual damages, P300.00 monthly income loss and 20% attorney's fees for a razed house and store despite a pending criminal negligence case, noting that Rule 111 suspension reaches only civil actions arising from delict and that the employer retains a right of reimbursement against the driver.[^69] In [*Bermudez v. Melencio-Herrera*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/feb1988/gr_l-32055_1988.html) (quoting 1964 Rule 111, Section 2), the Supreme Court held that filing a reservation in a criminal reckless-imprudence case did not elect crime over tort or prevent suing the employer solidarily under Article 2180, distinguishing *Joaquin v. Aniceto*, as cited in [*Bermudez v. Melencio-Herrera*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/feb1988/gr_l-32055_1988.html).[^70] Two months later came [*Bordas v. Canadalla*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/apr1988/gr_l-30036_1988.html)[^71]. A jeepney driven by Senceno Canadalla and owned by his employer Primo Tabar allegedly sideswiped Marcos Bordas in Cebu City on May 2, 1968. While the criminal case for serious physical injuries through reckless imprudence was pending, Bordas sued the driver and the owner for damages based on *culpa aquiliana*. The City Court of Cebu dismissed the civil case because Bordas made no reservation in the criminal action. The Court of First Instance of Cebu affirmed in toto.[^72] The Supreme Court held that no reservation was needed because the civil action was not derived from the criminal liability of the accused. The trial court erred in treating the criminal case as a prejudicial question. The Supreme Court also noted that the 1985 amendment of Rule 111, Section 2 dropped Articles 31 and 2177 from its coverage. The civil actions under those articles are not civil actions *ex delicto*. The articles by themselves authorize a quasi-delict action that proceeds independently of the criminal case.[^73] The Supreme Court granted the petition, set aside the order of November 2, 1968 and ordered the hearing of the civil case resumed without regard to the criminal case. It declared the decision immediately executory.[^74]

Modern decisions continue to enforce the same independence against employers and drivers after both criminal convictions and criminal acquittals. In [*Heirs of Catalina P. Mendoza v. ES Trucking and Forwarders*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66172)[^75] a 14-wheeler prime mover truck driven by Clin Timtim sideswiped Catalina P. Mendoza on Sta. Maria Road in Zamboanga City on June 13, 2013. She was already crossing the second half of the road. She died of her injuries. The MTCC convicted Timtim of reckless imprudence resulting in homicide. The heirs separately sued ES Trucking for quasi-delict in Branch 12 of the Regional Trial Court of Zamboanga City.[^76]

The trial court dismissed the complaint for insufficient evidence. It found no recklessness because the truck was in its proper lane. The Court of Appeals affirmed. It held that the conviction did not establish the employer's civil liability and found no sufficient evidence of negligence.[^77]

The Supreme Court found Timtim negligent. He had failed to watch the road in front of him. ES Trucking had also admitted his conviction in its appellee's brief. The Supreme Court held that the quasi-delict suit could proceed against the employer regardless of the outcome of the criminal case. It relied on Article 2177 and on Article 31.[^78] It found that ES Trucking ran a truck-for-hire business without registering the vehicle with the LTFRB. Its failure to secure a franchise did not change the true nature of the vehicle.[^79] ES Trucking also failed to prove due diligence. It hired Timtim on the strength of his license and job application alone. It let him drive a vehicle that was not registered with the LTFRB.[^80]

The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the appellate decision and resolution. ES Trucking must pay the heirs P362,565.60 in actual damages for funeral expenses, P50,000.00 in civil indemnity, P100,000.00 in moral damages, P50,000.00 in exemplary damages, P50,000.00 in attorney's fees and costs of suit. Exemplary damages rested on gross negligence in failing to register the truck and in hiring and supervising the driver. Interest runs at 12% per annum from June 14, 2013 to June 30, 2013 and 6% per annum from July 1, 2013 until full satisfaction.[^81]

In [*Fegarido v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68422)[^82] (quoting [*Safeguard Security Agency, Inc. v. Tangco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40427)[^83] on Article 31), the Supreme Court affirmed solidary quasi-delict awards against a jeepney driver and operator notwithstanding the driver's criminal acquittal for insufficiency of evidence. By contrast, [*Castillo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_48541_1989.html) marks an important boundary: while quoting *Azucena v. Potenciano* on Article 31, the Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of a quasi-delict complaint where the final criminal acquittal had expressly analyzed the evidence and declared that the accused's alleged negligence did not exist because the plaintiff's own driving was the proximate cause.[^84] Finally, in adjacent independent civil actions under Article 33, the Supreme Court in [*Kane v. Roggenkamp*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66520)[^85] (quoting [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074)[^86] on Article 31) and [*Hao v. Lagahid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70070)[^87] (quoting [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^88] on Article 31) applied the same non-preclusion reasoning to hold that neither a reasonable-doubt acquittal under Republic Act No. 9262 nor a perjury complaint before the City Prosecutor bars an independent civil suit for physical injuries or fraud.

### [*Cinco v. Canonoy*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/may1979/gr_l-33171_1979.html): Quasi-Delict Property Damage and Non-Suspension

[*Cinco v. Canonoy*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/may1979/gr_l-33171_1979.html)[^89] established the doctrinal bridge between Article 31, Article 1157 and Article 2176 and settled that quasi-delict property-damage claims cannot be suspended upon the filing of a criminal negligence case. Porfirio P. Cinco filed Civil Case No. 189 on February 25, 1970 before Branch II of the City Court of Mandaue City against jeepney driver Romeo Hilot and operators Valeriana and Carlos Pepito for automobile damages arising from a vehicular collision. After a criminal case was subsequently filed against Hilot, City Judge Lorenzo B. Barria suspended Civil Case No. 189 at pre-trial under Rule 111, Section 3(b). On November 5, 1970, Branch III of the Court of First Instance of Cebu (Judge Mateo Canonoy) upheld the suspension.

The Supreme Court granted the writ of certiorari, set aside the November 5, 1970 decision of the Court of First Instance of Cebu, ordered Branch II of the City Court of Mandaue City to proceed with the hearing of Civil Case No. 189 and made no pronouncement as to costs.[^90] Directly quoting and applying Article 31 alongside Article 1157, the Supreme Court held that quasi-delict is an independent source of obligation not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony.[^91] Accordingly, the civil actions suspended by Rule 111, Sections 3(a) and 3(b) upon institution of a criminal case are solely civil actions arising from the criminal offense, not independent actions founded on quasi-delict.[^92] Rejecting the argument that quasi-delict excluded vehicle damage, the Supreme Court ruled that Article 2176 covers both injuries to persons and damage to property, while expressly declining to adjudicate fault, damages or other assigned errors.[^93]

### [*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html): Quasi-Delict Against the Truck Owner and Culpa Criminal Against the Acquitted Jeep Driver

[*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html)[^94] illustrates both the strength of Article 31 against an employer-owner and the doctrinal limit created when a criminal acquittal is not grounded on reasonable doubt. A three-way vehicular collision in Marilao, Bulacan involved Edgardo E. Mendoza's Mercedes Benz, Rodolfo Salazar's jeep and Felino Timbol's truck driven by Freddie Montoya. In a joint criminal trial before Branch V of the Court of First Instance of Bulacan (Criminal Cases Nos. SM-227 and SM-228, alleging P1,604.00 damage to Salazar's jeep and P8,890.00 damage to Mendoza's car), the court on July 31, 1970 convicted Montoya in SM-227 (imposing a P972.50 fine and P972.50 actual damages payable to Salazar with subsidiary imprisonment in case of insolvency) and acquitted Salazar in SM-228. Mendoza then filed Civil Case No. 80803 in Branch VIII of the Court of First Instance of Manila for quasi-delict damages against truck-owner Timbol and jeep-owner-driver Salazar. Judge Abundio Z. Arrieta dismissed the complaint against Timbol on September 12, 1970 and against Salazar on January 30, 1971 (reconsideration denied February 23, 1971).

The Supreme Court rendered a split disposition with no costs: it set aside the September 12, 1970 order dismissing Civil Case No. 80803 against Timbol and ordered the trial judge to proceed with the hearing on the merits, while upholding the January 30, 1971 and February 23, 1971 orders dismissing the complaint against Salazar.[^95] As to truck-owner Timbol, the Supreme Court directly applied Article 31 (citing *Garcia v. Florido*, as cited in [*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html)[^96]) to hold that Mendoza's quasi-delict claim could proceed independently without prior reservation under Rule 111, Section 2 and regardless of the criminal result.[^97] As to Salazar, the Supreme Court found that Mendoza had based his claim on *culpa criminal* because he actively intervened in the prosecution of SM-228. The criminal court had declared that Salazar could not be held liable for the damage to Mendoza's car. Because the fact from which the civil liability might arise did not exist, the civil action was extinguished under Rule 111, Section 3(c). The Supreme Court added that the result would be the same even if the claim were not *ex delicto*. Salazar's acquittal was not based on reasonable doubt, so Article 29 offered no route either.[^98] That rationale was later confined and distinguished by the same ponente in [*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html).[^99]

### [*Heirs of Pedro Tayag, Sr. v. Alcantara*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/jul1980/gr_50959_1980.html): Pleading Elements of Quasi-Delict After Reasonable-Doubt Acquittal

[*Heirs of Pedro Tayag, Sr. v. Alcantara*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/jul1980/gr_50959_1980.html)[^100] set out the four pleading elements that bring a tort complaint squarely within Article 31 and insulate it from a criminal acquittal on reasonable doubt. After a Philippine Rabbit bus driven by Romeo Villa y Cunanan fatally struck bicyclist Pedro Tayag, Sr. in Tarlac, his heirs filed Civil Case No. 5114 for damages in Branch I of the Court of First Instance of Tarlac against Philippine Rabbit Bus Lines, Inc. and Villa. Judge Fernando S. Alcantara suspended the civil trial on April 30, 1975 under Rule 111, Section 3 pending Criminal Case No. 836, acquitted Villa of homicide on reasonable doubt on October 25, 1977 and then dismissed Civil Case No. 5114 for lack of cause of action on April 13, 1978 (reconsideration denied May 30, 1979).

The Supreme Court granted certiorari, set aside the order of dismissal and remanded Civil Case No. 5114 for further proceedings with costs against the private respondents.[^101] Quoting Article 31 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that the provision governs civil actions predicated on non-felony sources of obligation such as quasi-delict.[^102] Because the heirs' complaint pleaded all four essential averments of a quasi-delict (fault or negligence, damage or injury, direct causal relation between them and absence of a pre-existing contractual relation) together with the employer's failure to exercise due diligence in selecting and supervising its driver, the action was entirely distinct from *culpa criminal*.[^103] Citing *Elcano v. Hill*, as cited in [*Heirs of Pedro Tayag, Sr. v. Alcantara*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/jul1980/gr_50959_1980.html)[^104], the Supreme Court ruled that Rule 111, Section 3(c) extinction covers only civil liability *ex delicto* under Article 100 of the Revised Penal Code, so dismissing the quasi-delict suit after Villa's reasonable-doubt acquittal constituted grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack of jurisdiction.[^105]

### [*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html): Primary Employer Liability and Non-Application of Criminal Res Judicata

[*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html)[^106] clarified the boundary of [*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html)[^107] and affirmed that neither the heirs' intervention through private prosecutors nor the absence of a reservation defeats an Article 31 quasi-delict suit against driver and employer. After a cargo truck driven by Pedro Dianala and owned by Ponciano Rejon ran over and killed Esperanza Gula in Bago City, the Bago City Court in Criminal Case No. 3092 acquitted Dianala on October 13, 1969 of homicide through reckless imprudence on reasonable doubt due to a dearth of evidence and lack of veracity of two principal witnesses, even though the heirs had intervened through private prosecutors without making a reservation. When the heirs subsequently sued Dianala and Rejon for quasi-delict damages, the Court of First Instance of Negros Occidental dismissed the complaint on August 22, 1972 (reconsideration denied November 14, 1972) on grounds of *res judicata* and lack of cause of action against Rejon. The Court of Appeals certified the pure question of law to the Supreme Court.

The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the dismissal orders and directed the corresponding Regional Trial Court to reinstate and hear the case on the merits without deciding negligence or damages.[^108] Distinguishing [*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html)[^109] as involving *culpa criminal* and an acquittal not based on reasonable doubt, the Supreme Court held that Article 31 found full application because the heirs sued on *culpa aquiliana* and Dianala's acquittal rested on reasonable doubt.[^110] The Supreme Court further held that *res judicata* did not bar the action against truck-owner Rejon, who was never a co-accused in the criminal case and whose liability under Article 2180 is primary rather than subsidiary under Article 103 of the Revised Penal Code. No reservation was needed in the criminal case because the suit was not derived from criminal liability, subject only to the bar against double recovery noted in *Elcano v. Hill*, as cited in [*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html).[^111]

### [*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368): Separate Quasi-Delict Action by the Accused Without Forum Shopping

[*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368)[^112] established that the right to bring an independent civil action for quasi-delict belongs not only to the private complainant but also to the accused in a pending criminal case. Following a vehicular collision, Mario Llavore Laroya filed Criminal Case No. 002-99 for reckless imprudence resulting in damage to property before the Municipal Circuit Trial Court of Capas, Tarlac against driver Avelino Casupanan. While the criminal case was at preliminary investigation, Casupanan and vehicle owner-operator Roberto Capitulo filed Civil Case No. 2089 for quasi-delict against Laroya before the same Municipal Circuit Trial Court. The trial court dismissed Civil Case No. 2089 on March 26, 1999 (reconsideration denied May 7, 1999) for forum-shopping without specifying whether dismissal was with prejudice. On December 28, 1999 (reconsideration denied August 24, 2000), Branch 66 of the Regional Trial Court of Capas, Tarlac dismissed petitioners' Rule 65 certiorari petition in Special Civil Action No. 17-C (99), holding ordinary appeal was the proper remedy.

The Supreme Court granted the petition for review on certiorari, annulled the December 28, 1999 and August 24, 2000 resolutions of Branch 66 of the Regional Trial Court of Capas, Tarlac and reinstated Civil Case No. 2089.[^113] After holding that a dismissal silent on prejudice under Supreme Court Administrative Circular No. 04-94 is without prejudice and reviewable by Rule 65 certiorari, the Supreme Court applied the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure retroactively.[^114] Because Rule 111, Section 1, paragraph 6 expressly prohibits the accused from filing counterclaims in criminal cases and requires separate litigation and because Article 2177 applies equally to the accused, Casupanan and Capitulo could validly file Civil Case No. 2089 during the pendency of the criminal case without violating the rule against forum shopping.[^115] Explaining that under Rule 111 independent civil actions under Articles 32, 33, 34 and 2176 are not deemed instituted, need no reservation, are not suspended by the criminal action and are not subject to Rule 111 consolidation, the Supreme Court directly invoked Article 31 of the Civil Code to stress that the law itself accepts the possibility of varying criminal and civil verdicts by commanding that the independent civil action proceed regardless of the criminal result.[^116]

## Statutory Ceilings and Carve-Outs Where Article 31 Does Not Apply

Article 31 is broad but not unlimited. Supreme Court jurisprudence establishes two primary legal ceilings where a plaintiff cannot invoke Article 31 to maintain an independent civil suit alongside or after a criminal prosecution, together with three substantive and procedural boundaries that define where independent recovery ends.

First, Article 31 applies only when the civil obligation arises from an act that does not itself constitute a crime. Where the purported contract itself is assailed as illegal *per se* under a prohibitory penal law such as Section 3(g) of Republic Act No. 3019 (the Anti-Graft and Corrupt Practices Act) and void *ab initio* under Article 1409 of the Civil Code, no valid obligation can arise from it until the criminal court resolves its validity, so the civil collection action must be suspended under Rule 111, Section 2 ([*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492)[^117]).

Second, in prosecutions for violation of Batas Pambansa Blg. 22 (the Bouncing Checks Law), Supreme Court Circular No. 57-97 and Rule 111, Section 1(b) of the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure carve out an explicit procedural exception. The criminal action for B.P. 22 is deemed to include the corresponding civil action to recover the face value of the dishonored checks, requires full payment of filing fees upon institution and prohibits any reservation or subsequent filing of a separate civil collection suit. Separate civil proceedings are permitted only when the civil action is filed ahead of the B.P. 22 criminal complaint. Even then the rules encourage consolidation. Because of this special rule, Article 31 does not apply once a B.P. 22 criminal case has been filed ([*Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corp. v. Asia Dynamic Electrix Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980)[^118], [*Cheng v. Spouses Sy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40565)[^119]). In [*Silangan Textile Manufacturing Corp. v. Demetria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41404), the Supreme Court reversed a Court of Appeals ruling that had invoked Article 31 to sustain a separate collection suit for P9,999,845.00 against a corporate drawer after B.P. 22 cases were filed against its signatory officers, ordering the civil case dismissed for forum shopping and *litis pendentia* and lifting the ancillary writ of preliminary attachment over the corporation's land titles.[^120] In [*Heirs of Eduardo Simon v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37623), the Supreme Court reinforced this carve-out by holding that even fraud allegations cannot turn a subsequent B.P. 22 collection suit into an independent civil action, sharply distinguishing estafa prosecutions where Article 33 fraud suits remain available.[^121]

Third, independent civil recovery meets three further limits recognized in supporting rulings. Full payment of the underlying debt leaves no surviving civil liability after acquittal ([*Nuguid v. Nicdao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42465)[^122]). Conversely, when a criminal court acquits an accused because the claim arises purely from a civil contract, the criminal court cannot award that contractual debt in the criminal judgment and must leave it to a separate civil action ([*Murao v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57909)[^123]). Finally, in a vehicular collision suit, a final criminal acquittal that affirmatively analyzes the evidence and declares that the accused's alleged negligence did not exist bars a subsequent civil recovery predicated on that same alleged negligence ([*Castillo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_48541_1989.html)[^124]).

### [*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492): Contracts Assailed as Illegal Per Se Under the Anti-Graft Law

[*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492)[^125] defines the sharpest substantive boundary of Article 31: a contract whose very execution is prosecuted as a criminal act cannot support an independent civil action. Navotas Industrial Corporation (NIC) filed Civil Case No. 1153-MN on September 20, 1988 before Branch 73 of the Regional Trial Court of Malabon to collect a P30,799,676.00 unpaid balance on four dredging contracts worth P194,454,000.00 (claiming P184,847,970.00 accomplished out of a P615,000,000.00 National Capital Region allocation and P800,000,000.00 total release). The Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) counterclaimed for the return of P146,962,072.47 already paid plus interest and P100,000,000.00 in exemplary damages, alleging that the contracts were void because they were awarded without public bidding and supported by falsified accomplishments. Following an Ombudsman investigation, Criminal Cases Nos. 16889-16900 for estafa through falsification and violations of Section 3(e) and (g) of Republic Act No. 3019 were filed in the First Division of the Sandiganbayan. Branch 73 of the Regional Trial Court of Malabon denied DPWH's motion to consolidate the civil suit with the Sandiganbayan cases on June 18, 1993 (reconsideration denied November 7, 1993). The Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CV No. 33094 dismissed DPWH's certiorari petition on July 18, 1994 because the Sandiganbayan lacked jurisdiction over NIC's collection suit.

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision with modification, declaring DPWH's counterclaim in Civil Case No. 1153-MN deemed abandoned by operation of Section 4 of Presidential Decree No. 1606 as amended by Republic Act No. 8249 and ordering Branch 73 of the Regional Trial Court of Malabon to suspend the trial of Civil Case No. 1153-MN until termination of Criminal Cases Nos. 16889-16900 in the Sandiganbayan.[^126] Rejecting consolidation because the Sandiganbayan lacks jurisdiction over a contractor's collection suit and Rule 111, Section 1 bars counterclaims in criminal cases, the Supreme Court directly analyzed and quoted Article 31 of the Civil Code.[^127] The Supreme Court contrasted a valid trust receipt contract (whose subsequent breach constitutes a crime and therefore falls under Article 31) with a purported contract assailed as illegal *per se* under Section 3(g) of Republic Act No. 3019 for being manifestly and grossly disadvantageous to the government.[^128] Because a contract executed against prohibitory laws is void *ab initio* under Article 1409 of the Civil Code and generates no valid obligation, Article 31 does not apply and Rule 111, Section 2 mandates suspension of the civil case until the Sandiganbayan resolves the criminal charges, leaving NIC free to resume trial if the Sandiganbayan finds the dredging contracts valid.[^129]

### [*Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corp. v. Asia Dynamic Electrix Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980): B.P. 22 Mandatory Consolidation and Corporate Identity

[*Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corp. v. Asia Dynamic Electrix Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980)[^130] is the foundational ruling carving out B.P. 22 prosecutions from Article 31. After Asia Dynamic Electrix Corporation's checks covering P1,622,467.14 worth of electrical conduits and fittings were dishonored, Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corporation first filed B.P. 22 criminal complaints (pending before the Metropolitan Trial Court of Pasig City) against Asia Dynamic's signatory officers Gil Santillan and Juanito Pamatmat. Hyatt subsequently filed Civil Case No. MC 01-1493 on April 4, 2001 before Branch 210 of the Regional Trial Court of Mandaluyong City against the corporation itself for P1,622,467.14 plus interest and attorney's fees. Branch 210 denied Asia Dynamic's motion to dismiss on December 10, 2001 on the ground that Article 31 permitted a separate *ex contractu* collection suit, but the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 71467 reversed on October 8, 2003 (reconsideration denied May 14, 2004) and ordered dismissal.

The Supreme Court denied Hyatt's petition and upheld the dismissal of Civil Case No. MC 01-1493.[^131] Directly rejecting the trial court's reliance on Article 31, the Supreme Court explained that Supreme Court Circular No. 57-97 and Rule 111, Section 1(b) of the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure deem the corresponding civil action included in every B.P. 22 criminal complaint, require full payment of docket fees based on the check amounts and prohibit reservation or subsequent separate civil filing in order to declog court dockets and stop creditors from using criminal courts as free collection agencies.[^132] Separate civil proceedings are allowed only when the civil action is instituted ahead of the B.P. 22 criminal case. Because of this special rule governing B.P. 22, Article 31 of the Civil Code does not apply.[^133] The Supreme Court further held that suing the corporation after charging its signatory officers triggered *litis pendentia* and forum shopping because officers who sign corporate checks within their authority represent the same interest as the corporation. Adding additional checks did not cure the defect.[^134]

### [*Cheng v. Spouses Sy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40565): Res Judicata After B.P. 22 Dismissal and Pro Hac Vice Reinstatement

[*Cheng v. Spouses Sy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40565)[^135] confirmed that the B.P. 22 carve-out from Article 31 triggers *res judicata* even after dismissal of the criminal cases without any civil pronouncement, while crafting a narrow *pro hac vice* equitable exception for gross prosecutorial negligence. Anita Cheng loaned P600,000.00 to Spouses William and Tessie Sy secured by two P300,000.00 Philippine Bank of Communications checks drawn on a closed account (and also filed a P600,000.00 claim in an archived voluntary insolvency proceeding). She instituted two estafa cases (Criminal Cases Nos. 98-969952 and 98-969953 before Branch 7 of the Regional Trial Court of Manila) and two B.P. 22 cases (Criminal Cases Nos. 341458-59 before Branch 25 of the Metropolitan Trial Court of Manila). Branch 7 dismissed both estafa cases on March 16, 2004 for failure to prove the elements of estafa, with one order stating that if there was any liability of the accused it was purely civil and not criminal in nature. Branch 25 later dismissed both B.P. 22 cases on demurrer on February 7, 2005 because the public prosecutor failed to identify the accused in open court, making no pronouncement on civil liability. Instead of appealing the civil aspect, Cheng filed Civil Case No. 05-112452 on April 26, 2005 before Branch 18 of the Regional Trial Court of Manila to collect the P600,000.00 loan, which Branch 18 dismissed on January 2, 2006 (reconsideration denied June 5, 2006) under Rule 111, Section 1(b).

The Supreme Court granted Cheng's Rule 45 petition *pro hac vice*, ordered Civil Case No. 05-112452 reinstated and made no pronouncement as to costs.[^136] On the governing doctrine, the Supreme Court applied Rule 111, Section 1(b) retroactively to the 1999 B.P. 22 complaints and expressly held that in view of the special rule governing B.P. 22 actions, Article 31 of the Civil Code is not applicable and failure to appeal the civil aspect of the dismissed B.P. 22 cases ordinarily bars a separate collection suit by *res judicata*.[^137] Nevertheless, because the public prosecutor committed gross negligence by failing to establish the accused's identity and failing to appeal the civil aspect, leaving Cheng with no other remedy and threatening unjust enrichment, the Supreme Court reinstated Civil Case No. 05-112452 *pro hac vice* without adjudging the spouses liable for P600,000.00 and expressly reserving whether any liability existed if the loan were proven true at trial.[^138]

### [*Heirs of Eduardo Simon v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37623): Litis Pendentia in B.P. 22 Versus Fraud Actions in Estafa

[*Heirs of Eduardo Simon v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37623)[^139] drew a clear procedural line between B.P. 22 and estafa prosecutions and enforced strict sanctions against a subsequent civil collection suit with attachment. Three years after Criminal Case No. 275381 for violation of B.P. 22 over a P336,000.00 Landbank check was filed against Eduardo Simon in the Metropolitan Trial Court of Manila on July 11, 1997, payee Elvin Chan filed Civil Case No. 915-00 on August 3, 2000 before Branch 45 of the Metropolitan Trial Court of Pasay City for collection of the check with a writ of preliminary attachment over Simon's vehicle. On October 23, 2000 (reconsideration denied December 20, 2000), Branch 45 dismissed Civil Case No. 915-00 for *litis pendentia*, lifted the August 14, 2000 writ of attachment, charged Chan's attachment bond for P336,000.00 in favor of Simon for attachment damages, directed the Branch Sheriff to restore the seized vehicle to Simon and ordered Chan to pay Simon P5,000.00 in attorney's fees. Branch 108 of the Regional Trial Court of Pasay City affirmed *in toto* on July 31, 2001, but the Court of Appeals reversed on June 25, 2002 (reconsideration denied March 14, 2003), relying on Article 33 fraud and *DMPI Employees Credit Association v. Velez*, as cited in [*Heirs of Eduardo Simon v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37623).[^140]

The Supreme Court granted the petition of Simon's heirs, reversed and set aside the June 25, 2002 decision of the Court of Appeals, reinstated the October 23, 2000 decision of Branch 45 of the Metropolitan Trial Court of Pasay City (including the P336,000.00 bond charge, vehicle restoration and P5,000.00 attorney's fees) and ordered Chan to pay the costs of suit.[^141] Quoting [*Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corp. v. Asia Dynamic Electrix Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980)[^142] on why Article 31 does not apply to B.P. 22 proceedings, the Supreme Court held that there is no independent civil action to recover the value of a bouncing check after a B.P. 22 criminal action is filed even if the civil complaint alleges fraud. Applying Rule 111, Section 1(b) retroactively, the Supreme Court dismissed Civil Case No. 915-00 on the ground of *litis pendentia*.[^143] The Supreme Court explained that the procedures for estafa and B.P. 22 are non-interchangeable: in estafa prosecutions, the offended party may reserve a separate civil action or institute an independent fraud action under Article 33, whereas in B.P. 22 prosecutions, reservation and subsequent separate civil suits are barred.[^144]

## Survival of Independent Civil Liability Upon Death of the Accused

The distinction between civil liability *ex delicto* and independent civil obligations becomes decisive when the accused dies before final judgment in the criminal case. Under Article 89 of the Revised Penal Code, death of the accused before final judgment extinguishes both criminal liability and civil liability based solely on the felony (*ex delicto*). By contrast, civil liability predicated on a separate non-delictual source of obligation under Article 1157 survives the death of the accused and remains enforceable ([*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html)[^145], [*Asilo, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52987)[^146], [*Bernardo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61354)[^147]).

This survival rule protects injured parties not only when the accused dies on appeal, but also when the accused dies prior to conviction in the trial court. In [*L.G. Foods Corp. v. Pagapong-Agraviador*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42999)[^148], a company driver fatally struck a seven-year-old child and committed suicide before judgment in Criminal Case No. 67787 before Branch III of the Municipal Trial Court in Cities of Bacolod City, resulting in dismissal of the criminal case on September 30, 1998. When the parents sued the employer in Branch 43 of the Regional Trial Court of Bacolod City (Civil Case No. 99-10845) alleging negligent selection and supervision, the Supreme Court denied the employer's petition and upheld the denial of its motion to dismiss, holding that the complaint stated a direct and primary quasi-delict action under Articles 2176 and 2180 that survived the driver's pre-conviction death and required no prior reservation because dismissal upon suicide operated as if no criminal case existed.

### [*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html): Return to the Old Rule and Survival of Non-Delictual Obligations

[*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html)[^149] is the En Banc landmark establishing the modern Philippine rules governing survival of civil liability upon the death of an accused pending appeal. Rogelio Bayotas y Cordova was convicted of rape on June 19, 1991 in Criminal Case No. C-3217 by Branch 16 of the Regional Trial Court of Roxas City and died on February 4, 1992 while his appeal was pending before the Supreme Court. After dismissing the criminal aspect of the appeal on May 20, 1992, the En Banc Supreme Court re-examined *People v. Sendaydiego*, as cited in [*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html)[^150], to determine whether Bayotas's civil liability survived his death.

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal of the late Rogelio Bayotas with costs *de oficio*. It held that a re-examination of *Sendaydiego* impelled it to revert to the old ruling and set down four governing rules.[^151] First, death of the accused pending appeal of his conviction extinguishes his criminal liability as well as civil liability based solely on the offense (*ex delicto*), because neither Article 30 of the Civil Code nor Rule 3, Section 21 preserves purely delictual liability inside a criminal appeal.[^152] Second, the claim for civil liability survives notwithstanding the death of the accused if it may also be predicated on a source of obligation other than delict under Article 1157 of the Civil Code, namely law, contracts, quasi-contracts or quasi-delicts.[^153] Third, where civil liability survives on a non-delictual source, the offended party may pursue it in a separate civil action enforced either against the executor or administrator (under Rule 87, Section 1 for quasi-delict or injury to person or property) or against the estate of the accused (under Rule 86, Section 5 for contract).[^154] Fourth, the private offended party need not fear forfeiture of the right to file that separate civil action by prescription because the statute of limitations is interrupted during the pendency of the criminal case under Article 1155 of the Civil Code.[^155] Applying these rules, the Supreme Court extinguished Bayotas's *ex delicto* civil liability from rape because no separate non-delictual action was before it.[^156]

### [*Asilo, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52987): Survival of Human-Relations Liability Despite Sandiganbayan Consolidation

[*Asilo, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52987)[^157] directly applied Article 31 together with Article 32(6) to sustain civil recovery against a deceased public official's substituted heir even after the civil case had been consolidated with a Sandiganbayan criminal prosecution. Nagcarlan, Laguna Mayor Demetrio T. Comendador and municipal engineer Paulino S. Asilo, Jr. forcibly demolished the leased public-market store of Spouses Visitacion and Cesar C. Bombasi without a special court order under Rule 39, Section 10(d). Spouses Bombasi filed a separate civil action for damages based on violation of due process (Civil Case No. SP-4064) ahead of Criminal Case No. 23267 for violation of Section 3(e) of Republic Act No. 3019. The Sandiganbayan consolidated both cases on March 4, 1997 under Section 4 of Presidential Decree No. 1606. After dismissing the cases against deceased co-accused Alberto S. Angeles on September 22, 1999, Mayor Comendador also died pending trial on September 17, 2002. On April 28, 2003, the Fourth Division of the Sandiganbayan convicted Asilo and Comendador under Section 3(e) of Republic Act No. 3019 (imposing imprisonment of six years and two months to ten years and one day), ordered the Municipality of Nagcarlan, Comendador and Asilo jointly and severally to pay Spouses Bombasi P437,900.00 actual damages, P100,000.00 moral damages, P30,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of suit, denied exemplary damages, injunction and counterclaim and on reconsideration extinguished Comendador's criminal liability while maintaining his civil liability.

The Supreme Court denied the appeals and affirmed the Sandiganbayan decision with modification, declaring finality of dismissal as to Angeles, confirming that Comendador's criminal liability was extinguished by death while his civil liability survived, holding the Municipality of Nagcarlan, Asilo and Comendador (substituted by his widow Victoria Bueta Vda. de Comendador) solidarily liable to Spouses Bombasi for P200,000.00 temperate damages (replacing the unsubstantiated P437,900.00 actual damages award based on an unauthenticated hearsay price quotation and omitting the P30,000.00 attorney's fees) and P100,000.00 moral damages and assessing costs against petitioners-appellants.[^158] Directly quoting and applying Article 31 alongside Article 32(6) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that although Comendador's death pending trial would have extinguished civil liability *ex delicto*, his civil liability survived because it was founded on an independent source of obligation (the law on human relations prohibiting deprivation of property without due process) and had been instituted ahead of the criminal case with proper substitution by his widow, notwithstanding its erroneous consolidation under Presidential Decree No. 1606, Section 4.[^159] The Supreme Court also noted that the municipality was placed in estoppel by its yearly grant of business permits to the spouses.[^160]

### [*Bernardo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61354): Enforcement of Surviving Contractual Liability Inside a B.P. 22 Appeal

[*Bernardo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61354)[^161] harmonized the survival of independent contractual civil liability upon death of the accused with the mandatory B.P. 22 joinder rule under Rule 111, Section 1(b). Paz T. Bernardo borrowed P460,000.00 from Carmencita C. Bumanglag under a promissory note bearing 12% per annum interest and later issued five Far East Bank and Trust Company checks covering the debt. On May 28, 2003, Branch 56 of the Regional Trial Court of Makati City convicted Bernardo of five counts of violating B.P. 22, sentenced her to one year imprisonment per count and ordered her to pay Bumanglag P460,000.00 plus 12% interest and 5% penalty charges from December 1, 1991. On August 31, 2007 (reconsideration denied March 14, 2008), the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CR No. 28721 deleted imprisonment and the 5% penalty charges, imposed a P460,000.00 fine and retained the P460,000.00 civil indemnity with 12% interest from the institution of criminal charges. Bernardo died on February 3, 2011 while her Supreme Court appeal was pending. Her substituted heirs argued that her death extinguished civil liability or at least required Bumanglag to file a separate civil action.

The Supreme Court affirmed the August 31, 2007 decision of the Court of Appeals with modification, ordering the heirs of Paz T. Bernardo to pay P460,000.00 with 12% per annum interest from the institution of criminal charges in court plus 6% per annum legal interest on the total amount adjudged from finality until fully paid, while deleting the P460,000.00 criminal fine.[^162] Explaining that independent civil liability founded on contract (the loan evidenced by the promissory note and checks under Article 1157) survives the accused's death pending appeal, the Supreme Court held that because Rule 111, Section 1(b) deems the civil action instituted in every B.P. 22 prosecution to prevent multiplicity of suits, that surviving contractual civil liability may be enforced directly against the deceased's estate in the same B.P. 22 proceeding once nonpayment is proved by preponderance of evidence, rather than forcing the creditor to initiate a second civil suit.[^163]

## Procedural Mechanics: Reservation, Consolidation, Prescription and the Bar Against Double Recovery

The practical operation of Article 31 has evolved alongside four major revisions of Rule 111 of the Rules of Court governing the prosecution of civil actions with criminal cases.

**1. Evolution of the Reservation Requirement Across Rule 111 Revisions**

Under the 1964 Rules of Court, Rule 111, Section 2 expressly listed Articles 31, 32, 33, 34 and 2177 and contained language requiring reservation of the right to institute an independent civil action separately, although the Supreme Court repeatedly held that failure to reserve did not bar a non-delictual action under Article 31 ([*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html)[^164], [*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html)[^165]) and that filing a reservation in the criminal case did not elect *ex delicto* liability over quasi-delict ([*Bermudez v. Melencio-Herrera*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/feb1988/gr_l-32055_1988.html)[^166]). The original 1985 Rules on Criminal Procedure eliminated both the reservation requirement and the reference to Articles 31 and 2177 from Rule 111, Section 2 because, as the Supreme Court explained in [*Bordas v. Canadalla*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/apr1988/gr_l-30036_1988.html), actions under Articles 31 and 2177 are not civil actions *ex delicto* and by themselves authorize independent civil proceedings.[^167] However, the 1988 amendments to Rule 111 temporarily restored the mandatory reservation requirement even for independent civil actions under Article 2176. Applying that superseded 1988 regime, the Supreme Court in [*San Ildefonso Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35753) (quoting Justice Florenz D. Regalado's commentary on Articles 31 and 2177) held that an insurer-subrogee under Article 2207 was bound by the assured's failure to reserve and granted suspension of its P564,500.00 damages suit.[^168] Finally, the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure permanently abolished the prior reservation requirement for independent civil actions. The Supreme Court has consistently applied that 2000 reform retroactively to pending actions and counterclaims in [*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368)[^169], [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^170], [*Supreme Transportation Liner, Inc. v. San Andres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64620)[^171], [*Fegarido v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68422)[^172] and [*Hao v. Lagahid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70070)[^173].

**2. Joint Trial and Consolidation:** Consolidation depends on which rule is invoked and whether the court holds jurisdiction over both suits. Under Rule 31, Section 1 of the Rules of Court, a trial court possessing jurisdiction over both actions does not abuse its discretion by ordering a consolidated joint trial of a criminal case and an independent civil action arising from the same transaction when common factual issues and substantially identical evidence make joint trial efficient without prejudicing substantial rights, as held for an Article 31 contract-of-services suit in [*Caños v. Peralta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/aug1982/gr_l-38352_1982.html)[^174] and extended (quoting [*Caños v. Peralta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/aug1982/gr_l-38352_1982.html)[^175]) to an Article 33 libel action under Article 360 of the Revised Penal Code in [*Cojuangco, Jr. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/nov1991/gr_37404_1991.html)[^176]. By contrast, Rule 111 consolidation applies to civil actions *ex delicto* rather than independent civil actions ([*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368)[^177]). Moreover, a regular civil collection suit cannot be consolidated into a Sandiganbayan criminal prosecution where the Sandiganbayan lacks jurisdiction over the civil claim ([*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492)[^178]).

**3. Prescriptive Periods:** Independent causes of action carry their own statutory prescriptive periods. In [*Spouses Santos v. Pizardo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53406)[^179], the Supreme Court reiterated the dual-liability taxonomy citing Article 31 and held that expiration of the four-year prescriptive period for quasi-delict did not bar the victims' heirs from filing a timely suit within ten years from finality of the driver's conviction to enforce their expressly reserved civil liability *ex delicto* (and the employer's subsidiary liability under Article 103 of the Revised Penal Code). Meanwhile, [*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html)[^180] confirmed that under Article 1155 of the Civil Code, the pendency of a criminal case in which civil liability was instituted interrupts the running of prescription for filing a surviving separate civil action.

**4. Prohibition Against Double Recovery (Article 2177):** Across both contractual and quasi-delict actions, the Supreme Court pairs the right to pursue independent and *ex delicto* remedies simultaneously with a strict substantive guardrail: under Article 2177 of the Civil Code, the offended party cannot recover damages twice for the same act or omission ([*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^181], [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074)[^182], [*Fegarido v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68422)[^183], [*Hao v. Lagahid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70070)[^184]). In [*Supreme Transportation Liner, Inc. v. San Andres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64620)[^185], where the criminal case against the other driver had already concluded before the trial of petitioners' quasi-delict counterclaim, the Supreme Court remanded the civil case specifically so petitioners could present evidence on their counterclaim while demonstrating that they had not already recovered civil damages in the concluded criminal action.

## Related Provisions

- [Civil Code, Article 29](art-0029.md): Authorizes a civil action on preponderance of evidence after criminal acquittal on reasonable doubt, distinguished from Article 31 in [*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html)[^207] and [*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html)[^208].
- [Civil Code, Article 30](art-0030.md): Governs separate civil actions for liability arising from a crime where no criminal proceedings are instituted, distinguished from surviving non-delictual actions upon death in [*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html)[^209].
- [Civil Code, Article 32](art-0032.md): Creates an independent civil action for obstruction or impairment of constitutional rights, applied alongside Article 31 to preserve due-process demolition liability after a mayor's death in [*Asilo, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52987)[^210].
- [Civil Code, Article 33](art-0033.md): Authorizes independent civil actions for defamation, fraud and physical injuries, contrasted with Article 31 in [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074)[^211], [*Heirs of Eduardo Simon v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37623)[^212], [*Kane v. Roggenkamp*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66520)[^213] and [*Hao v. Lagahid*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70070)[^214].
- [Civil Code, Article 34](art-0034.md): Establishes independent civil liability of local police officers for failure to render aid, grouped with Articles 31, 32, 33 and 2176 in [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^215] and [*Safeguard Security Agency, Inc. v. Tangco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40427)[^216].
- [Civil Code, Article 1157](../book-4-title-01-obligations/art-1157.md): Enumerates the five sources of obligations supplying the non-delictual foundations of Article 31 actions in [*Cinco v. Canonoy*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/may1979/gr_l-33171_1979.html)[^217], [*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html)[^218] and [*Bernardo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61354)[^219].
- [Civil Code, Article 1409](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1409.md): Declares contracts executed contrary to prohibitory laws void *ab initio*, excluding contracts assailed as illegal *per se* under Republic Act No. 3019 from Article 31 in [*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492)[^220].
- [Civil Code, Article 2176](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2176.md): Defines quasi-delict covering both personal injury and property damage as an independent obligation under Article 31 in [*Cinco v. Canonoy*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/may1979/gr_l-33171_1979.html)[^221], [*Heirs of Pedro Tayag, Sr. v. Alcantara*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/jul1980/gr_50959_1980.html)[^222] and [*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368)[^223].
- [Civil Code, Article 2177](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2177.md): Separates quasi-delict from criminal negligence while barring double recovery for the same act or omission, applied alongside Article 31 in [*Bordas v. Canadalla*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/apr1988/gr_l-30036_1988.html)[^224], [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^225], [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074)[^226] and [*Heirs of Catalina P. Mendoza v. ES Trucking and Forwarders*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66172)[^227].
- [Civil Code, Article 2180](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2180.md): Establishes direct, primary and solidary vicarious liability of employers, contrasted with subsidiary liability under Article 103 of the Revised Penal Code in [*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html)[^228], [*Safeguard Security Agency, Inc. v. Tangco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40427)[^229] and [*Heirs of Catalina P. Mendoza v. ES Trucking and Forwarders*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66172)[^230].

## Key Cases

- [*Cinco v. Canonoy*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/may1979/gr_l-33171_1979.html) (G.R. No. L-33171, May 31, 1979): After Mandaue City Court and CFI Cebu suspended an automobile property-damage suit upon the filing of a criminal negligence case against the jeepney driver, the Supreme Court linked Article 31 to Articles 1157 and 2176, held quasi-delict covers property damage and cannot be suspended under Rule 111, Section 3(b) and granted certiorari directing the City Court to proceed with hearing Civil Case No. 189 without adjudicating fault or damages.
- [*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html) (G.R. No. L-32599, June 29, 1979): Following a three-way Bulacan collision where truck driver Montoya was convicted (P972.50 fine and P972.50 damages to Salazar) and jeep driver Salazar was acquitted, CFI Manila dismissed Mendoza's quasi-delict suit against truck-owner Timbol and Salazar. The Supreme Court set aside the dismissal against Timbol under Article 31 without requiring reservation. It upheld the dismissal against Salazar because the claim against him was *ex delicto* and the criminal court had declared that the fact giving rise to it did not exist. Article 29 would not have helped because the acquittal was not based on reasonable doubt.
- [*Heirs of Pedro Tayag, Sr. v. Alcantara*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/jul1980/gr_50959_1980.html) (G.R. No. L-50959, July 23, 1980): After CFI Tarlac acquitted a Philippine Rabbit bus driver of homicide on reasonable doubt and dismissed the heirs' suspended damages suit, the Supreme Court defined Article 31 as governing non-felony obligations such as quasi-delict, enumerated the four essential pleading averments of quasi-delict, set aside the dismissal order and remanded Civil Case No. 5114 for further proceedings with costs against private respondents.
- [*Caños v. Peralta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/aug1982/gr_l-38352_1982.html) (G.R. No. L-38352, August 19, 1982): Where an employer faced a Minimum Wage Law (Republic Act No. 602) criminal charge and the employee sued for wage differentials, overtime and termination pay in CFI Davao del Sur, the Supreme Court applied Article 31 to classify the civil suit as *ex contractu* under a contract of services, upheld consolidated joint trial under Rule 31, Section 1 and dismissed the certiorari petition with costs against petitioner without ruling on the merits.
- [*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html) (G.R. No. L-40308, September 28, 1984): After Bago City Court acquitted a truck driver on reasonable doubt despite the heirs' participation via private prosecutors without reservation and CFI Negros Occidental dismissed their quasi-delict suit, the Supreme Court applied Article 31 in full, distinguished [*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html), held criminal *res judicata* did not bar primary employer liability under Article 2180 and reversed the dismissal orders to reinstate trial on the merits.
- [*Vintola v. Insular Bank of Asia and America*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/may1987/gr_l-73271_1987.html) (G.R. No. L-73271, May 29, 1987): After CFI Cebu acquitted seashell importers of estafa for lack of misappropriation under a P40,000.00 domestic Letter of Credit and Trust Receipt and RTC Cebu ordered collection on reconsideration, the Supreme Court applied Article 31 to hold that entrustees' liability arises *ex contractu* independently of estafa acquittal and affirmed the solidary judgment for P72,982.27 plus 14% per annum interest, 1% per annum service charge, P4,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of suit.
- [*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html) (G.R. No. 102007, September 2, 1994): When a defendant convicted of rape by RTC Roxas City died pending appeal, the En Banc Supreme Court re-examined *People v. Sendaydiego* and reverted to the old ruling. It held that death pending appeal extinguishes *ex delicto* liability while non-delictual civil liability under Article 1157 survives in a separate civil action against the executor, administrator or estate with prescription tolled under Article 1155 and dismissed the appeal with costs *de oficio*.
- [*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368) (G.R. No. 145391, August 26, 2002): After MCTC Capas, Tarlac dismissed an accused driver's and vehicle owner's separate quasi-delict suit for forum-shopping and RTC Capas dismissed their certiorari petition, the Supreme Court applied the 2000 Revised Rules retroactively, invoked Article 31's tolerance of differing verdicts, annulled the RTC resolutions and reinstated Civil Case No. 2089 without deciding fault or damages.
- [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647) (G.R. No. 133978, November 12, 2002): After RTC Pampanga dismissed three estafa cases and then dismissed a P190,000.00 collection suit over dishonored checks for *res judicata* and forum-shopping, the Supreme Court applied the 2000 Rules retroactively to eliminate prior reservation, classified the check-for-cash claim as Article 31 *culpa contractual* regardless of petitioner's delict label, reversed the dismissal orders and remanded Civil Case No. G-3272 for further proceedings.
- [*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492) (G.R. No. 116463, June 10, 2003): Where a contractor sued DPWH in RTC Malabon for P30,799,676.00 on dredging contracts prosecuted in the Sandiganbayan as manifestly and grossly disadvantageous under Republic Act No. 3019, Section 3(g), the Supreme Court held Article 31 inapplicable to contracts assailed as illegal *per se* under Article 1409, declared DPWH's RTC counterclaim abandoned under Presidential Decree No. 1606, Section 4 and ordered trial of Civil Case No. 1153-MN suspended pending the Sandiganbayan cases.
- [*Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corp. v. Asia Dynamic Electrix Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980) (G.R. No. 163597, July 29, 2005): After filing B.P. 22 complaints against corporate check signatories in MeTC Pasig City, the payee filed a P1,622,467.14 collection suit against the corporation in RTC Mandaluyong, which CA ordered dismissed. The Supreme Court denied the petition, held Article 31 inapplicable to B.P. 22 under Circular No. 57-97 and Rule 111, Section 1(b) and affirmed dismissal for *litis pendentia* and forum shopping due to corporate identity of interest.
- [*Frias v. San Diego-Sison*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42035) (G.R. No. 155223, April 3, 2007): Notwithstanding petitioner's criminal acquittal for perjury and false testimony over a false affidavit of loss of title, the Supreme Court applied Article 31 to affirm her liability in the lender's contractual loan collection suit for P2,000,000.00 plus 25% per annum interest from June 7, 1991, P70,000.00 attachment bond premiums with legal interest from January 31, 1996, P100,000.00 moral, corrective and exemplary damages and RTC costs, while deleting P100,000.00 unexplained attorney's fees.
- [*Cheng v. Spouses Sy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40565) (G.R. No. 174238, July 7, 2009): After estafa and B.P. 22 cases over a P600,000.00 loan were dismissed without appeal of the civil aspect and RTC Manila dismissed the subsequent collection suit, the Supreme Court held Article 31 inapplicable to B.P. 22 and *res judicata* ordinarily applicable under Rule 111, Section 1(b), yet reinstated Civil Case No. 05-112452 *pro hac vice* due to the public prosecutor's gross negligence and to prevent unjust enrichment without adjudging liability.
- [*Heirs of Eduardo Simon v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37623) (G.R. No. 157547, February 23, 2011): Three years after a B.P. 22 case over a P336,000.00 check was filed in MeTC Manila, the payee filed a collection suit with attachment in MeTC Pasay, which dismissed for *litis pendentia* before CA reversed. Adopting [*Hyatt*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980) on Article 31's inapplicability and distinguishing estafa/Article 33 fraud suits, the Supreme Court reversed CA and reinstated MeTC Pasay's dismissal, P336,000.00 bond charge, vehicle restoration and P5,000.00 attorney's fees plus costs.
- [*Asilo, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52987) (G.R. Nos. 159017-18 & 159059, March 9, 2011): Where a municipal mayor died pending a consolidated Sandiganbayan Republic Act No. 3019 trial over the warrantless demolition of a leased market store, the Supreme Court applied Articles 31 and 32(6) to hold that civil liability based on the law on human relations survived death despite Presidential Decree No. 1606 consolidation and affirmed modified solidary liability of the municipality, engineer and substituted widow for P200,000.00 temperate damages (replacing P437,900.00 actual damages and omitting P30,000.00 attorney's fees) and P100,000.00 moral damages plus costs.
- [*Heirs of Jose Marcial K. Ochoa v. G & S Transport Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52995) (G.R. Nos. 170071 & 170125, March 9, 2011): After a taxicab passenger died on EDSA and the cab driver was acquitted on appeal in the reckless imprudence criminal case, the Supreme Court applied Article 31 to hold that the driver's acquittal was wholly immaterial to the common carrier's independent *culpa contractual* liability and affirmed modified awards of P50,000.00 civil indemnity, P6,611,634.59 loss of earning capacity, P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages, P100,000.00 attorney's fees and costs of litigation.
- [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074) (G.R. Nos. 175256 & 179160, August 23, 2012): After the Pasig RTC acquitted the seller of estafa on demurrer and found him not civilly liable, the buyer appealed that civil order while filing a Manila civil suit for specific performance and damages over 37,200 undelivered bags of cement. Applying Article 31, the Supreme Court held that simultaneous pursuit of *ex delicto* appeal and an independent contractual/abuse-of-rights suit does not constitute forum shopping, *litis pendentia* or *res judicata* (subject to Article 2177 against double recovery), reinstating the CA appeal and affirming remand of the Manila suit.
- [*Bernardo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61354) (G.R. No. 182210, October 5, 2015): When an accused convicted of five counts of B.P. 22 over a P460,000.00 promissory-note loan died pending Supreme Court appeal, the Supreme Court deleted the P460,000.00 fine but held that her surviving independent contractual civil liability (deemed instituted in the B.P. 22 case under Rule 111, Section 1(b)) remained enforceable directly against her substituted heirs and estate in the same proceeding for P460,000.00 plus 12% per annum interest from filing and 6% per annum post-finality interest.

## Suggested Answers

**Hypothetical 1: Common-Carrier Driver Acquitted of Reckless Imprudence**

*Question:* A passenger dies when a taxicab operated by Carrier X rams a fly-over railing and plunges to the street below. In the criminal prosecution for reckless imprudence resulting in homicide, the Regional Trial Court reverses the Municipal Trial Court and acquits the taxicab driver. Meanwhile, the passenger's heirs sue Carrier X in a separate civil action for breach of contract of carriage without having filed any reservation in the criminal case. Carrier X moves to dismiss the civil suit, arguing that the driver's criminal acquittal extinguishes civil liability and that the heirs failed to reserve their civil action. Should the civil court grant the motion to dismiss?

*Suggested Answer:* No. The civil court should deny the motion to dismiss. Under Article 31 of the Civil Code, when a civil action is based on an obligation not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony, it may proceed independently of the criminal proceedings and regardless of the result of the latter. As the Supreme Court held in [*Heirs of Jose Marcial K. Ochoa v. G & S Transport Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52995)[^231], an action for damages arising from breach of contract of carriage (*culpa contractual*) is an independent civil action entirely distinct from the criminal case for reckless imprudence against the driver, so the driver's acquittal has no bearing on the carrier's contractual liability. Furthermore, under the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure applied in [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647)[^232] and [*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368)[^233], no prior reservation in the criminal case is required to prosecute an independent civil action, subject only to the prohibition against double recovery under Article 2177.

**Hypothetical 2: Corporate Collection Suit Filed After B.P. 22 Complaints Against Officers**

*Question:* Supplier A delivers merchandise to Corporation B, which pays with postdated checks signed by its corporate officers. After the checks bounce, Supplier A files criminal complaints for violation of B.P. 22 against the signatory corporate officers before the Metropolitan Trial Court. Several months later, Supplier A files a separate civil action for collection of sum of money with a writ of preliminary attachment against Corporation B in the Regional Trial Court, invoking Article 31 of the Civil Code on the ground that Corporation B's liability arises *ex contractu* from the sale and that the corporate entity is distinct from its officers. Corporation B moves to dismiss the civil case and lift the attachment. How should the trial court rule?

*Suggested Answer:* The Regional Trial Court should grant the motion, dismiss the civil collection suit and lift the writ of preliminary attachment. Although breach of a sales contract ordinarily supports an independent action under Article 31, the Supreme Court held in [*Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corp. v. Asia Dynamic Electrix Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980)[^234], [*Silangan Textile Manufacturing Corp. v. Demetria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41404)[^235] and [*Heirs of Eduardo Simon v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37623)[^236] that Supreme Court Circular No. 57-97 and Rule 111, Section 1(b) of the 2000 Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure establish a special rule for B.P. 22 under which the criminal action necessarily includes the corresponding civil action, prohibits reservation or subsequent separate filing and renders Article 31 inapplicable. Because corporate officers who sign checks within their authority represent the same interest as the corporation and both suits seek recovery on the same dishonored checks, the subsequent civil collection action is barred by *litis pendentia* and forum shopping. Separate civil proceedings would have been permissible only if Supplier A had filed the civil action ahead of the B.P. 22 criminal complaints.

**Hypothetical 3: Collection Suit on a Public Contract Assailed as Illegal Per Se Under R.A. 3019**

*Question:* Contractor C sues a government agency in the Regional Trial Court to collect an unpaid balance on infrastructure contracts. While the civil suit is pending, the Ombudsman indicts the agency officials and Contractor C before the Sandiganbayan for entering into those same contracts without public bidding in violation of Section 3(g) of Republic Act No. 3019 (contracts manifestly and grossly disadvantageous to the government). Contractor C insists that its civil collection suit must proceed immediately under Article 31 of the Civil Code because it rests on contract rather than felony. Should the Regional Trial Court proceed with trial, consolidate the civil suit with the Sandiganbayan or suspend the civil proceedings?

*Suggested Answer:* The Regional Trial Court should suspend the trial of the civil collection suit until termination of the Sandiganbayan criminal cases. As the Supreme Court ruled in [*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492)[^237], consolidation with the Sandiganbayan is improper because the Sandiganbayan lacks jurisdiction over a contractor's civil collection suit. At the same time, Article 31 does not apply because it covers only civil actions based on obligations arising from acts not constituting a crime. Where the very execution of the purported contract is assailed as illegal *per se* under a prohibitory penal law such as Section 3(g) of Republic Act No. 3019, the contract would be void *ab initio* under Article 1409 of the Civil Code and incapable of producing any enforceable obligation. Consequently, the trial court must suspend the civil action under Rule 111, Section 2 pending the Sandiganbayan's resolution of the criminal charges. Trial may resume only if the Sandiganbayan finds the contracts valid.

# Quotes

> "For obviously, the jural concept of a quasi-delict is that of an independent source of obligation "not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony." Article 1157 of the Civil Code bolsters this conclusion when it specifically recognizes that:"[^238]

> "Consequently, petitioner's cause of action being based on quasi-delict, respondent Judge committed reversible error when he dismissed the civil suit against the truck-owner, as said case may proceed independently of the criminal proceedings and regardless of the result of the latter."[^239]

> "The petitioners' cause of action being based on a quasi delict the acquittal of the driver, private respondent Romeo Villa, of the crime charged in Criminal Case No. 836 is not a bar to the prosecution of Civil Case No. 5114 for damages based on quasi-delict."[^240]

> "Being essentially an action for enforcement of an obligation ex-contractu the civil case can proceed independently of the latter, in accordance with Article 31 of the Civil Code:"[^241]

> "Nor was there need for plaintiffs-appellants to have filed a reservation for a separate civil action inasmuch as the civil action contemplated in the case at bar is not derived from the criminal liability but one based on culpa aquiliana under the Civil Code."[^242]

> "Rather, the civil suit instituted by IBAA is based ex contractu and as such is distinct and independent from any criminal proceedings and may proceed regardless of the result of the latter. Under the situational circumstances of the parties, they are governed by Article 31 of the Civil Code, explicitly providing:"[^243]

> "2. Corollarily, the claim for civil liability survives notwithstanding the death of accused, if the same may also be predicated on a source of obligation other than delict."[^244]

> "We make this ruling aware of the possibility that the decision of the trial court in the criminal case may vary with the decision of the trial court in the independent civil action. This possibility has always been recognized ever since the Civil Code introduced in 1950 the concept of an independent civil action under Articles 32, 33, 34 and 2176 of the Code. But the law itself, in Article 31 of the Code, expressly provides that the independent civil action “may proceed independently of the criminal proceedings and regardless of the result of the latter.”"[^245]

> "Neither does it matter that the civil action reserved in the October 21, 1997 order of the trial court was the civil action ex delicto. To reiterate, an independent civil action arising from contracts, as in the instant case, may be filed separately and prosecuted independently even without any reservation in the criminal action. Under Article 31 of the Civil Code “[w]hen the civil action is based on an obligation not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony, [e.g. culpa contractual] such civil action may proceed independently of the criminal proceedings and regardless of the result of the latter.” Thus, in Vitola, et al. v. Insular Bank of Asia and America, the Court, applying Article 31 of the Civil Code, held that a civil case seeking to recover the value of the goods subject of a Letter of Credit-Trust Receipt is a civil action ex contractu and not ex delicto. As such, it is distinct and independent from the estafa case filed against the offender and may proceed regardless of the result of the criminal proceedings."[^246]

> "Article 31 speaks of a civil action "based on an obligation not arising from the act x x x complained of as a felony." This clearly means that the obligation must arise from an act not constituting a crime. In the instant case, the act purporting to create the obligation is assailed as a crime in itself. That act, which is prohibited by law, is the entering into dredging contracts that are manifestly and grossly disadvantageous to the government. A contract executed against the provisions of prohibitory laws is void. If the dredging contracts are declared illegal, then no valid obligation can arise from such contracts. Consequently, no civil action based on such contracts can proceed independently of the criminal action."[^247]

> "The foregoing rule was adopted from Circular No. 57-97 of this Court. It specifically states that the criminal action for violation of B.P. 22 shall be deemed to include the corresponding civil action. It also requires the complainant to pay in full the filing fees based on the amount of the check involved. Generally, no filing fees are required for criminal cases, but because of the inclusion of the civil action in complaints for violation of B.P. 22, the Rules require the payment of docket fees upon the filing of the complaint. This rule was enacted to help declog court dockets which are filled with B.P. 22 cases as creditors actually use the courts as collectors. Because ordinarily no filing fee is charged in criminal cases for actual damages, the payee uses the intimidating effect of a criminal charge to collect his credit gratis and sometimes, upon being paid, the trial court is not even informed thereof. The inclusion of the civil action in the criminal case is expected to significantly lower the number of cases filed before the courts for collection based on dishonored checks. It is also expected to expedite the disposition of these cases. Instead of instituting two separate cases, one for criminal and another for civil, only a single suit shall be filed and tried. It should be stressed that the policy laid down by the Rules is to discourage the separate filing of the civil action. The Rules even prohibit the reservation of a separate civil action, which means that one can no longer file a separate civil case after the criminal complaint is filed in court. The only instance when separate proceedings are allowed is when the civil action is filed ahead of the criminal case. Even then, the Rules encourage the consolidation of the civil and criminal cases. We have previously observed that a separate civil action for the purpose of recovering the amount of the dishonored checks would only prove to be costly, burdensome and time-consuming for both parties and would further delay the final disposition of the case. This multiplicity of suits must be avoided. Where petitioners' rights may be fully adjudicated in the proceedings before the trial court, resort to a separate action to recover civil liability is clearly unwarranted. In view of this special rule governing actions for violation of B.P. 22, Article 31 of the Civil Code cited by the trial court will not apply to the case at bar."[^248]

> "Article 31 of the Civil Code provides that when the civil action is based on an obligation not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony, such civil action may proceed independently of the criminal proceedings and regardless of the result of the latter."[^249]

> "Indeed, under the present revised Rules, the criminal action for violation of BP Blg. 22 includes the corresponding civil action to recover the amount of the checks. It should be stressed, this policy is intended to discourage the separate filing of the civil action. In fact, the Rules even prohibits the reservation of a separate civil action, i.e., one can no longer file a separate civil case after the criminal complaint is filed in court. The only instance when separate proceedings are allowed is when the civil action is filed ahead of the criminal case. Even then, the Rules encourages the consolidation of the civil and criminal cases. Thus, where petitioner's rights may be fully adjudicated in the proceedings before the court trying the BP Blg. 22 cases, resort to a separate action to recover civil liability is clearly unwarranted on account of res judicata, for failure of petitioner to appeal the civil aspect of the cases. In view of this special rule governing actions for violation of BP Blg. 22, Article 31 of the Civil Code is not applicable."[^250]

> "The CA's reliance on DMPI Employees Credit Association v. Velez to give due course to the civil action of Chan independently and separately of Criminal Case No. 275381 was unwarranted. DMPI Employees, which involved a prosecution for estafa, is not on all fours with this case, which is a prosecution for a violation of BP 22. Although the Court has ruled that the issuance of a bouncing check may result in two separate and distinct crimes of estafa and violation of BP 22, the procedures for the recovery of the civil liabilities arising from these two distinct crimes are different and non-interchangeable. In prosecutions of estafa, the offended party may opt to reserve his right to file a separate civil action, or may institute an independent action based on fraud pursuant to Article 33 of the Civil Code, as DMPI Employees has allowed. In prosecutions of violations of BP 22, however, the Court has adopted a policy to prohibit the reservation or institution of a separate civil action to claim the civil liability arising from the issuance of the bouncing check upon the reasons delineated in Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corporation, supra."[^251]

> "Death of Mayor Comendador during the pendency of the case could have extinguished the civil liability if the same arose directly from the crime committed. However, in this case, the civil liability is based on another source of obligation, the law on human relations. The pertinent articles follow:"[^252]

> "In this case, the action filed by the heirs is primarily for the recovery of damages arising from breach of contract of carriage allegedly committed by G & S. Clearly, it is an independent civil action arising from contract which is separate and distinct from the criminal action for reckless imprudence resulting in homicide filed by the heirs against Padilla by reason of the same incident. Hence, regardless of Padilla's acquittal or conviction in said criminal case, same has no bearing in the resolution of the present case. There was therefore no error on the part of the CA when it resolved this case without regard to the fact that Padilla has already been acquitted by the RTC in the criminal case. Moreover, while the CA quoted some portions of the MTC Decision in said criminal case, we however find that those quoted portions were only meant to belie G & S' claim that the proximate cause of the accident was the negligence of the driver of the delivery van which allegedly hit the Avis taxicab. Even without those quoted portions, the appellate court's ultimate finding that it was Padilla's negligence which was the proximate cause of the mishap would still be the same. This is because the CA has, in fact, already made this declaration in the earlier part of its assailed Decision. The fact that the MTC Decision from which the subject quoted portions were lifted has already been reversed by the RTC is therefore immaterial."[^253]

> "A single act or omission that causes damage to an offended party may give rise to two separate civil liabilities on the part of the offender . (1) civil liability ex delicto, that is, civil liability arising from the criminal offense under Article 100 of the Revised Penal Code, and (2) independent civil liability, that is, civil liability that may be pursued independently of the criminal proceedings. The independent civil liability may be based on “an obligation not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony,” as provided in Article 31 of the Civil Code (such as for breach of contract or for tort). It may also be based on an act or omission that may constitute felony but, nevertheless, treated independently from the criminal action by specific provision of Article 33 of the Civil Code (“in cases of defamation, fraud and physical injuries”)."[^254]

> "As a necessary consequence of this special rule, the civil liabilities arising from the issuance of a worthless check are deemed instituted in a case for violation of B.P. 22; the death of Bernardo did not automatically extinguish the action. The independent civil liability based on contract, which was deemed instituted in the criminal action for B.P. 22, may still be enforced against her estate in the present case. We thus rule on the present action to determine Bumanglag's civil liability."[^255]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Focus: A Five-Step Checklist**
>
> 1. **Name the source of the obligation.** Read the factual allegations of the complaint, not its title or label. In [*Cancio, Jr. v. Isip*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51647) a claim the plaintiff called delictual was really *culpa contractual*. In [*Safeguard Security Agency, Inc. v. Tangco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40427) the allegations showed quasi-delict even though the heirs had reserved a civil action in the homicide case.
> 2. **Screen for the two carve-outs.** Ask whether the contract itself is prosecuted as a crime, as in [*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492). Ask whether a B.P. 22 case on the same checks was filed first, as in [*Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corp. v. Asia Dynamic Electrix Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980).
> 3. **Check the reservation rule for the period.** Under the 2000 rules no reservation is needed for independent civil actions. See [*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368) and [*Supreme Transportation Liner, Inc. v. San Andres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64620). The 1988 amendments applied in [*San Ildefonso Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35753) no longer govern.
> 4. **Read the criminal judgment closely.** An acquittal does not defeat an independent action, as in [*Heirs of Jose Marcial K. Ochoa v. G & S Transport Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52995). It may defeat one when it declares that the negligence did not exist, as in [*Castillo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_48541_1989.html).
> 5. **Guard against double recovery.** Article 2177 bars recovering twice for the same act or omission. See [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074).

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Trap: Estafa Is Not B.P. 22**
>
> The procedures for recovering civil liability in estafa and in B.P. 22 cases are not interchangeable. [*Heirs of Eduardo Simon v. Chan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37623) drew that line. In estafa the offended party may reserve a separate civil action or file an independent fraud action under Article 33. A contractual suit may also run beside the estafa case, as in [*Lim v. Kou Co Ping*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55074).
>
> In B.P. 22 the criminal action is deemed to include the civil action for the face value of the checks. Reservation is barred. A later collection suit fails for *litis pendentia* or forum shopping, even when it names the corporation instead of its signing officers. See [*Hyatt Industrial Manufacturing Corp. v. Asia Dynamic Electrix Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42980) and [*Silangan Textile Manufacturing Corp. v. Demetria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41404). Alleging fraud does not change the result.

> [!TIP]
> **Bar Exam Trap: The Acquitted Driver and the Employer**
>
> Do not assume that the employer escapes because the driver was acquitted. In [*Gula v. Dianala*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/sep1984/gr_l-40308_1984.html) the acquittal rested on reasonable doubt. The quasi-delict suit against the driver and the truck owner went on. The owner's liability under Article 2180 is primary, not subsidiary.
>
> Watch for the opposite case. In [*Mendoza v. Arrieta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/jun1979/gr_l-32599_1979.html) the plaintiff had pressed his claim against the jeep driver as *culpa criminal*. The criminal court declared that the jeep driver could not be held liable. The civil claim against him was extinguished. The claim against the truck owner survived because it rested on quasi-delict.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practical Application: Pleading the Independent Action**
>
> - Plead every element of quasi-delict. [*Heirs of Pedro Tayag, Sr. v. Alcantara*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/jul1980/gr_50959_1980.html) listed fault or negligence, damage, the causal link and the absence of a prior contract. Add the employer's failure to exercise due diligence in selection and supervision.
> - Plead the contract and its breach when the claim is contractual. In [*Vintola v. Insular Bank of Asia and America*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/may1987/gr_l-73271_1987.html) the bank sued on the letter of credit and trust receipt after the estafa acquittal.
> - Prove negligence by preponderance of evidence. In [*Fegarido v. Alcantara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68422) the driver's acquittal did not stop a solidary award against the driver and the operator.
> - Prove the employer's lack of diligence with specifics. In [*Heirs of Catalina P. Mendoza v. ES Trucking and Forwarders*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66172) the employer hired the driver on his license and application alone. It also let him drive a truck that was not registered with the LTFRB.

> [!NOTE]
> **Practical Application: Timing, Forum and Survival**
>
> - An accused may file his own quasi-delict suit while the criminal case is pending. Rule 111 bars counterclaims in the criminal case. See [*Casupanan v. Laroya*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51368).
> - Do not ask the criminal court to award a purely contractual debt after an acquittal. In [*Murao v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57909) the sales commission had to go to a separate civil action.
> - Check whether the debt was paid. In [*Nuguid v. Nicdao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42465) full payment left no civil liability after the acquittal.
> - Consider a joint trial where the same court has jurisdiction over both cases. [*Caños v. Peralta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1982/aug1982/gr_l-38352_1982.html) allowed it under Rule 31, Section 1.
> - Do not try to consolidate a collection suit into a Sandiganbayan case. The Sandiganbayan lacks jurisdiction over the contractor's claim. See [*Republic (DPWH) v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47492).
> - When the accused dies, look for another source of obligation. Under [*People v. Bayotas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/sep1994/gr_102007_1994.html) a claim based on law, contract, quasi-contract or quasi-delict survives and is pursued against the estate or the executor or administrator. A human-relations claim survived in [*Asilo, Jr. v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52987). A loan claim was enforced inside the B.P. 22 case in [*Bernardo v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61354).
> - Keep the prescriptive periods apart. In [*Spouses Santos v. Pizardo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53406) the four-year quasi-delict period had run. The reserved civil liability *ex delicto* could still be enforced.

> [!NOTE]
> **Drafting Reminder for the Bench**
>
> State which civil liability the complaint pursues before ruling on reservation, suspension or *res judicata*. Then trace each award through every level of review. Name the court that affirmed, modified or reversed and state each amount with its payer, payee and interest.
