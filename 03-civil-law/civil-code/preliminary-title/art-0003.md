> ART. 3. Ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. (2)

[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 3 establishes the legal presumption that every person knows the law once it is in force. A party cannot avoid legal duties or escape liability by claiming lack of awareness or unfamiliarity with statutory or regulatory commands. The Supreme Court has nonetheless set a strict boundary around the rule. Article 3 applies only to mandatory and prohibitory laws, not to permissive provisions that merely confer an option or choice of remedies.

**In brief**

1. Confirm that the legal norm invoked is a mandatory or prohibitory law or a judicial decision interpreting it, not a permissive option.
2. Distinguish claimed ignorance of the law from an established mistake of fact.
3. Apply the rule against laypersons, employers, landowners, professionals, lawyers, public officers and judges alike, disallowing pleas of good faith, reliance on private legal advice or inherited practice where compliance is compelled.
4. Trace the specific statutory consequences attached to noncompliance, including conviction for *malum prohibitum* offenses, *in pari delicto* dismissal of civil suits, loss of good-faith possessor status under Article 526 and disciplinary or administrative liability.

The sections below examine criminal and marital applications, standards imposed on judges, lawyers and public officers, civil, property, labor and agrarian rulings, the mandatory-and-prohibitory limit and judicial notice of laws and the statutory origin of the rule. Detailed summaries of decisions cited concisely in the topical discussion appear in Case Discussions, followed by Related Provisions, Key Cases and Suggested Answers.

## Criminal Law, Malum Prohibitum Offenses and Marital Impediments

Civil Code Article 3 operates alongside penal statutes defining marriage crimes and *malum prohibitum* offenses. In [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309),[^2] petitioner married Magdalena on May 20, 2006 and contracted a second marriage with Maricar on September 7, 2013 without dissolving the first marriage or securing a judicial declaration of nullity under Article 40 of the Family Code. He claimed in defense that the second marriage lacked formal requisites under Article 3 of the Family Code because no ceremony occurred and the municipal civil registrar rather than the mayor officiated. The Regional Trial Court of Guimba convicted him of Bigamy under Article 349 of the Revised Penal Code. The Court of Appeals affirmed.[^3]

The Supreme Court granted the petition in part and set aside the Bigamy conviction on reasonable doubt because the prosecution failed to prove all elements of a valid or voidable second marriage under *Pulido*. Instead, it found him guilty beyond reasonable doubt under Article 350 of the Revised Penal Code of knowingly contracting a marriage against the provisions of laws, sentencing him to six months of *arresto mayor* to three years, six months and 21 days of *prision correccional* with preventive detention deducted.[^4] Over and above record evidence showing that he declared himself "single" in the marriage certificate and concealed the prior marriage, the Supreme Court held that Article 3 of the Civil Code creates a presumption that the accused knows Family Code legal impediments, marriage requisites and Article 40, standing against him absent any sufficient evidence to the contrary.[^5]

Even before conviction or defense proof, an accused cannot use a later civil action to halt criminal prosecution for bigamy. In [*Marbella-Bobis v. Bobis*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37897),[^6] the husband contracted a first marriage on October 21, 1985 and a second marriage on January 25, 1996 without an Article 40 decree. Indicted for bigamy on February 25, 1998, he filed a civil nullity suit claiming lack of a marriage license and moved to suspend the criminal trial. Reversing the Quezon City Regional Trial Court order suspending the trial, the Supreme Court directed immediate continuation of the bigamy prosecution. Every person is presumed to know the law, so ignorance of Article 40 of the Family Code cannot be invoked as an excuse or used through a belated civil suit to frustrate prosecution.[^7]

In *malum prohibitum* crimes under special laws, claimed ignorance and good faith fail even where the accused invokes regulatory confusion. In [*People v. Gasacao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41800),[^8] a licensed manning agency's crewing manager collected cash bonds ranging from P10,000.00 to P20,000.00 from five seafarer applicants and failed to deploy or refund them. The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City convicted him of Large Scale Illegal Recruitment in Criminal Case No. Q-00-94240 with life imprisonment, a P500,000.00 fine and indemnities, while acquitting him in a separate information where complainants did not testify. After the Court of Appeals affirmed, the Supreme Court denied his petition. It dismissed as "flimsy and self serving" his claim that he was unaware of the Philippine Overseas Employment Administration prohibition on cash bonds, holding that ignorance of the law excuses no one and good faith is unavailable.[^9] The Supreme Court reached the same result in [*People v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35343)[^10] for unlicensed placement fees and in [*People v. Neri*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1985/dec1985/gr_l-37762_1985.html)[^11] for illegal possession of a prohibited homemade firearm.

## Judicial Duty, Public Officers, Court Personnel and Lawyers

While ordinary citizens are presumed to know the law, judges face a heightened standard because they interpret and enforce it. In [*Benito v. Balindong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48870),[^12] a Regional Trial Court judge in Malabang, Lanao del Sur issued restraining orders, a preliminary injunction and an October 5, 2006 decision annulling a Department of the Interior and Local Government order implementing an Ombudsman nine-month suspension. The Court of Appeals annulled his orders for want of jurisdiction under Sections 14 and 27 of Republic Act No. 6770 and Ombudsman Administrative Order No. 17-03 following *Fabian*. Rejecting the judge's plea of good faith, the Supreme Court held that if ordinary people are presumed to know the law, judges are duty-bound to actually know and understand it. Good faith fails where governing jurisdictional rules are basic and clear, so the Supreme Court fined him P30,000 for gross ignorance of the law and P10,000 for violating the Lawyer's Oath and Code of Professional Responsibility, with a stern warning.[^13]

Earlier, in [*Lantaco, Sr. v. Llamas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/am_1037_1981.html),[^14] a Pasay City judge acquitted a jeepney association officer of four estafa cases on July 31, 1975 for insufficiency of evidence, withheld copies of the decision from complaining drivers and ignored Supreme Court orders. Dismissing the judge with forfeiture of all retirement privileges and prejudice to government or government-corporation reinstatement, the Supreme Court cited both Article 3 and Article 8 of the New Civil Code alongside judicial notice under Rule 129, Section 1. The judge showed gross ignorance of the Social Security Act and of 19-year-old settled Supreme Court doctrine treating boundary-system jeepney drivers as employees.[^15]

Public officers outside the judiciary likewise cannot shift statutory compliance onto subordinates, private legal counsel, new legislation or inherited office practices. In [*Castillo-Co v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64563),[^16] the Governor of Quirino purchased reconditioned heavy equipment despite a Sangguniang Panlalawigan resolution authorizing only brand-new equipment and released a 40% advance payment prohibited by Section 338 of the Local Government Code. The Sandiganbayan convicted her under Section 3(g) of Republic Act No. 3019, imposed an indeterminate prison sentence with perpetual disqualification and ordered P330,490.78 civil indemnity. Affirming *in toto* with a sentence of six years and one month to six years and nine months, the Supreme Court held that the *Arias* doctrine neither covers ignoring an explicit board resolution on an engineer's recommendation nor applies to the legal opinion of a private consultant. Everyone is presumed to know the law, so a mistake of law or reliance on private counsel's opinion cannot justify an illegal act or exculpate an executive officer.[^17]

Administrative and disciplinary rulings uniformly bar ignorance pleas by government officials, civil servants, court personnel and lawyers. Public school and municipal officers could not plead layman unfamiliarity or recent procurement reform in [*Office of the Deputy Ombudsman for Luzon v. Dionisio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63223)[^18] and [*Suyat v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68798).[^19] Agency officials, health personnel and examinees were presumed to know corporate authority, nursing registration and civil service rules in [*Navotas Industrial Corp. v. Guanzon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68038),[^20] [*Civil Service Commission v. Rodriguez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66494)[^21] and [*Catipon v. Japson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60516).[^22] Likewise, inherited custom did not excuse a sheriff in [*Tabao v. Cabcabin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61874),[^23] nor did an erroneous procedural assumption excuse a lawyer in [*In re: Resolution Dated August 14, 2013 of the Court of Appeals v. Mortel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62353).[^24]

## Property, Contracts, Labor and Agrarian Applications

In property disputes, Article 3 bars claimants from using ignorance of Civil Code ownership rules to claim good-faith possession. In [*Jagualing v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/mar1991/gr_94283_1991.html),[^25] occupants of a river island in a non-navigable river claimed ownership by adverse possession for about fifteen years against the riparian owner suing to quiet title. The Regional Trial Court of Misamis Oriental dismissed the complaint on the view that the island was a delta. The Court of Appeals reversed under Articles 463 and 465. Affirming the appellate court, the Supreme Court declined to treat the island as a delta absent proof of legal requisites and applied Articles 3 and 526 of the Civil Code. Because occupants are presumed to know a riparian owner's preferential right under Article 465, their claimed ignorance of that article cannot establish good faith, leaving them subject to thirty years of uninterrupted adverse possession rather than their admitted fifteen years.[^26]

In contract and construction litigation, ignorance of statutory safety standards places both contracting parties *in pari delicto*. In [*Yu v. Heirs of Sia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68423),[^27] a contractor previously convicted for violating Presidential Decree No. 1096 sued a hotel owner for a P448,240.00 unpaid contract balance, while the owner counterclaimed for undersized rooms. The Regional Trial Court of Legazpi City awarded the balance to the contractor, but the Court of Appeals reversed and awarded the owner P1,127,923.86 in renovation costs plus moral, exemplary and fee awards. Reversing the appellate court and dismissing both the complaint and the counterclaim, the Supreme Court held both parties *in pari delicto* under Article 1411 of the Civil Code. Because ignorance of the law excuses no one, the hotel owner was not an innocent layperson, was deemed to know National Building Code minimum standards when approving the building plan, failed to consult her architect and operated the hotel in 2005 without a full occupancy permit.[^28]

Article 3 also defeats a seller's attempt to regain conveyed land under an *in pari delicto* plea, even as an alternative ruling. In [*Acabal v. Acabal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43774),[^29] a widower conveyed 18.15 hectares of unregistered conjugal land to his godson-nephew. After the nephew sold the parcel to a third party, the widower sued three years later claiming he had meant to sign a three-year lease. The Regional Trial Court of Dumaguete City dismissed the complaint, while the Court of Appeals reversed and held the sale simulated. Reversing the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court held that the widower failed to prove deception or a lease and found no violation of agrarian retention limits because only three to four hectares were suited to sugarcane. Even assuming illegality, *in pari delicto* barred relief, Article 1416 did not protect a landowner rather than beneficiary farmers and under Article 3 the widower could not feign ignorance of the law or claim good faith.[^30] Applying Articles 160 and 493 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court declared both sales valid up to the widower's five-ninths (`5/9`) ideal share, noting that a buyer's good faith is irrelevant for unregistered land.[^31]

Agrarian and labor disputes apply the same standard to statutory deadlines and mandatory terms. A landowner's administrator could not plead ignorance of the one-month deadline to select a deceased agricultural lessee's successor in [*Garcia v. Vda. de Caparas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55807),[^32] while the Supreme Court viewed an administrative "honest belief" excuse for unpaid tenant amortizations as lacking legal basis under the ignorance rule in [*Heirs of Lazaro Gallardo v. Soliman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55745).[^33]

## Mandatory and Prohibitory Scope Versus Permissive Rules

The text of Article 3 links ignorance directly to "compliance therewith," which narrows the provision to rules that command or forbid conduct. In [*D.M. Consunji, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52124),[^34] a construction worker fell 14 floors to his death on November 2, 1990. Ten days before the November 25, 1990 police investigation report was dated, his widow filed a claim for death benefits with the State Insurance Fund and received P3,581.85 in accrued pension plus monthly pension benefits of P596.97, later P716.40.[^35] On May 9, 1991, she sued the contractor for damages in the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City, which awarded P644,000.00 in total damages. The Court of Appeals affirmed *in toto*.[^36]

Before the Supreme Court, the contractor invoked Article 3 and Article 8 of the Civil Code to argue that the widow could not plead ignorance of the choice of remedies between Employees' Compensation Commission benefits and a civil tort suit under *Floresca*. The Supreme Court rejected that argument and held that the application of Article 3 is limited to mandatory and prohibitory laws. Because the *Floresca* choice of remedies is neither mandatory nor prohibitory, her ignorance could not be held against her.[^37] The Supreme Court added that the rule on ignorance or mistake of fact nullifying a waiver had been misapplied in *Floresca* and in the widow's case. It also found no proof that she knew the cause of her husband's death when she signed the claim form on November 15, 1990. It remanded the case to the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City to determine whether the trial court award exceeded the Employees' Compensation Commission award and deduct Labor Code payments to prevent double recovery, affirming the Court of Appeals decision in all other respects.[^38]

Where a statute sets mandatory terms or prescriptive rules, however, litigants bear the risk of invoking the wrong law. In [*Intengan v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50280),[^39] bank depositors initiated criminal complaints in 1993 under Republic Act No. 1405 against bank officers who disclosed foreign currency deposit records. After the Department of Justice ordered the informations withdrawn and the Court of Appeals dismissed their certiorari petition, the Supreme Court affirmed and denied the petition. It held that foreign currency deposit confidentiality had been governed since 1977 by Republic Act No. 6426 as amended by Presidential Decree No. 1246, not by Republic Act No. 1405.[^40]

Because filing under the wrong statute did not toll the eight-year prescriptive period under Act No. 3326, the Supreme Court rejected the depositors' plea that the ruling left them without a remedy. Existing laws are matters of mandatory judicial notice and *ignorantia legis non excusat*, so nothing prevented the depositors from filing a complaint charging the correct offense during the appeal.[^41] Similarly, a small-establishment employer who failed to apply for a minimum-wage exemption under Republic Act No. 6727 could not invoke layperson status in [*Pablico v. Cerro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65235),[^42] while barangay officials elected in May 1994 were estopped under Article 3 in [*David v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34756)[^43] from claiming ignorance of the three-year term fixed by Republic Act No. 7160.

## Statutory Origin and Constitutional Vagueness Challenges

The Philippine presumption that everyone knows the law derives from civil law tradition and shapes constitutional review of penal statutes. In [*People v. Siton*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49720),[^44] two accused charged with vagrancy under Article 202, paragraph 2 of the Revised Penal Code before the Municipal Trial Court in Cities in Davao City filed a certiorari petition challenging the statute as vague and violative of equal protection. Branch 11 of the Regional Trial Court of Davao City granted their petition on July 29, 2005 and declared Article 202(2) unconstitutional in reliance on the United States Supreme Court decision in *Papachristou v. City of Jacksonville*.[^45]

Granting the People's petition, reversing the Regional Trial Court and ordering the criminal prosecutions to continue, the Supreme Court rejected the *Papachristou* fair-notice rationale. Under Philippine law, ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith, a principle of Spanish origin adopted to govern legal conduct in this jurisdiction, unlike American law where ignorance of the law is a traditional rule admitting of exceptions.[^46] The Supreme Court further distinguished the specific provisions of the Jacksonville ordinance from Article 202(2) and noted existing constitutional probable-cause safeguards against arbitrary arrests.[^47]

## Case Discussions

### Employer Omission to Seek Wage Exemption

A restobar owner paid eight employees below the prescribed minimum wage and never applied for a small-establishment exemption under Section 4(c) of Republic Act No. 6727.[^48] After the Labor Arbiter dismissed the employees' complaints, the National Labor Relations Commission awarded wage differentials, 13th month pay, reinstatement and separation pay. The Court of Appeals deleted separation pay. On review, the owner claimed that as a mere layman he could not be expected to know the exemption application rules.

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification imposing 12% annual interest on wage differentials until July 1, 2013 or last work and 6% annual interest on all monetary awards from finality. It held that the statutory exemption elements are cumulative, that having omitted to apply he could not shield himself behind layman status because ignorance of the law excuses no one and that the tribunals also found he employed more than ten workers. The decision shows that statutory exemptions requiring prior administrative application cannot be claimed retroactively through pleaded ignorance of labor law.[^49]

### Statutory Barangay Term of Office

Barangay officials elected in May 1994 filed consolidated petitions for prohibition and certiorari seeking to halt the May 1997 barangay elections on the claim that Republic Act No. 6679 granted them a five-year term rather than the three-year term in Section 43(c) of Republic Act No. 7160.[^50]

The Supreme Court denied both petitions as devoid of merit. It held that Republic Act No. 7160, the later statute, fixed a constitutional three-year term, noted that under petitioners' own theory their 1994 election would have been illegal and ruled that elected officials are estopped from claiming a different term. Under Article 3 of the Civil Code, a belated claim of ignorance as to which statute governed their election is unacceptable. The decision shows that public officials cannot invoke ignorance of the statute under which they took office to extend their tenure.[^51]

### Unlicensed Recruitment and Plea for Mercy

Accused-appellant Rodolfo Caures, acting with co-accused without a license, collected P64,000.00 in placement fees from five Taiwan job applicants.[^52] The Regional Trial Court of Manila convicted him of large-scale illegal recruitment under the Labor Code, imposing life imprisonment and a P100,000.00 fine, plus five counts of estafa ordering restitution with 6% annual interest. On appeal, he sought mercy, claiming he had no idea the transaction was illegal and lacked criminal intent.

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal and affirmed the trial court *in toto*. It ruled that his plea for mercy was precluded by the axiom that ignorance of the law excuses no one and that lack of criminal intent is unavailing because large-scale illegal recruitment is *malum prohibitum*, while deceit was separately established for estafa. The decision shows that pleaded unfamiliarity with licensing laws neither excuses *malum prohibitum* guilt nor mitigates statutory penalties.[^53]

### Unauthorized Firearm Permit in Malum Prohibitum

A deputy governor was prosecuted in the City Court of Cagayan de Oro for possessing a homemade .38-caliber "paltik" revolver issued by the provincial governor alongside a carry permit from the Acting Provincial Commander.[^54] The City Court convicted him under Section 878 in relation to Section 2692 of the Revised Administrative Code as amended by Republic Act No. 4, imposing imprisonment of one year and one day, a P1,000.00 fine, confiscation and costs.

The Supreme Court affirmed the judgment with costs. Expressly adopting the trial court's reasoning citing Civil Code Article 3, it held that a deputy governor ought to know that an act *per se* illegal cannot be legalized by a provincial governor or provincial commander. It noted that an official permit could at most mitigate. Good faith and lack of criminal intent are no defense in a *malum prohibitum* offense. The Supreme Court also noted that *Macarandang* had been revoked by *Mapa*. The decision shows that unauthorized executive permits do not legalize prohibited acts or establish good faith under Article 3.[^55]

### School Lease and Unbidded Spending

A public school principal and a teachers' association president leased school grounds to a private drugstore without Department of Education or provincial approval and spent P600,000.00 in advance rent and P680,000.00 in donations without official receipts or public bidding under Republic Act No. 9184.[^56] The Ombudsman upgraded their liability on reconsideration to Grave Misconduct with dismissal, while the Court of Appeals downgraded the offense to Simple Misconduct. Before the administrative bodies, they claimed to be laymen unfamiliar with legal requirements.

The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals and found both respondents guilty of Grave Misconduct, imposing dismissal, cancellation of eligibility, forfeiture of benefits except accrued leave credits and perpetual disqualification. It held that they could not hide behind ignorance or unfamiliarity with governing laws and found that the principal's unrefuted remark about avoiding long Department of Education procedures, paired with their school-benefit defense, proved actual awareness and flagrant disregard. The decision shows that public-benefit motives and claimed unfamiliarity do not reduce Grave Misconduct where rules are deliberately bypassed.[^57]

### Recent Enactment of Procurement Law

Municipal officials of Buguias, Benguet purchased P1,050,000.00 in pesticides without public bidding after Republic Act No. 9184 took effect.[^58] The Ombudsman dismissed all three officials, the Court of Appeals affirmed the treasurer's dismissal and modified the accountant's and agricultural officer's offenses while maintaining dismissal and the officials filed a belated Rule 65 petition. On the merits, they pleaded good faith and excusable negligence based on the recent enactment of Republic Act No. 9184 and pressure from the local chief executive.

The Supreme Court dismissed the Rule 65 petition as an improper and late remedy against a final appellate ruling and affirmed the Court of Appeals. Addressing the merits, it rejected good faith and excusable negligence, quoting [*Office of the Deputy Ombudsman for Luzon v. Dionisio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63223) that public officers cannot hide behind ignorance or unfamiliarity, noting they were enjoined and duty-bound to know the law and finding that the purchase violated even repealed Section 367 limits of Republic Act No. 7160. The decision shows that recent statutory enactment does not excuse noncompliance by public officials, particularly where prior law was also breached.[^59]

### Presumed Knowledge of Corporate Agency Rules

A National Power Corporation committee chair recommended disbursement of P124,436,195.00 and $2,167,701.16 in Ganda Energy receivables to unauthorized third parties lacking any corporate board resolution or secretary's certificate, despite prior notice from creditor Navotas Industrial.[^60] The Ombudsman found him guilty of Grave Misconduct. The Court of Appeals initially affirmed before exonerating him in an Amended Decision.

The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Amended Decision and reinstated the original appellate and Ombudsman findings of Grave Misconduct with accessory penalties of eligibility cancellation, forfeiture of benefits except accrued leave credits and perpetual disqualification because respondent had already left service. It held that a public officer is presumed to know the rules on corporate board authority, agency and payment embodied in law and jurisprudence and that ignorance thereof excuses no one. The decision shows that Article 3's presumption extends to settled jurisprudential rules on corporate representation in public disbursements.[^61]

### Practicing Nursing Without Registration

A public health nurse falsely declared passing the 1988 Nursing Licensure Examination and entered another nurse's Professional Regulation Commission identification number across six Personal Data Sheets between 1989 and 2000 while practicing without valid registration under Republic Act No. 877.[^62] The Civil Service Commission Regional Office dismissed the charge, the Commission Proper found Serious Dishonesty and Grave Misconduct and the Court of Appeals reversed.

The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated the Commission Proper decision imposing dismissal, cancellation of eligibility, forfeiture of retirement benefits except accrued leave credits and perpetual disqualification. Rejecting her uncorroborated explanation involving a supposed fixer as lacking credence, the Supreme Court held that she could not hide behind ignorance or lack of familiarity with Sections 16 and 30 of Republic Act No. 877 governing the nursing profession. The decision shows that regulated professionals and civil servants cannot excuse unlicensed practice through claimed ignorance of licensing statutes.[^63]

### Unverified Civil Service Eligibility Rules

A Social Security System employee lacking 1.5 Military Science units declared college graduation in 1984 to take the October 17, 1993 Career Service Professional Examination, claiming he believed length of government service still substituted for academic deficiencies despite discontinuation in January 1993 under Civil Service Commission Memorandum Circular No. 42, s. 1991.[^64] The Civil Service Commission Cordillera Administrative Region exonerated him of dishonesty and falsification, suspended him for six months and one day for conduct prejudicial and revoked his eligibility. The Court of Appeals denied his petition.

The Supreme Court denied review and affirmed the Court of Appeals. Besides holding that he failed to exhaust administrative remedies before the Commission Proper, it affirmed that he was negligent in failing to verify current examination rules directly with the Commission. Good faith and lack of deliberate intent did not excuse conduct prejudicial to the service because ignorance of the law excuses no one. The decision shows that applicants must verify current administrative circulars rather than relying on superseded rules or co-workers' advice.[^65]

### Inherited Office Practice Versus Statutory Duties

A prosecutor charged a court sheriff with certifying the voluntary surrender of an accused for a P120,000.00 cash bail bond, alleging that no arrest warrant had been issued.[^66] The sheriff invoked a common practice inherited from predecessors and cited orders of other judges authorizing release of bailed accused. The Investigating Judge found simple irregularity and recommended a P5,000.00 fine and warning.

The Supreme Court adopted the factual findings, classified the offense as Simple Misconduct and imposed a P5,000.00 fine deducted from retirement benefits, omitting a stern warning because of his optional retirement. It held that an inherited practice is no defense because ignorance of the law excuses no one and because laws are repealed only by subsequent ones, so violations cannot be excused by disuse, custom or practice. The decision shows how Article 3 works directly alongside Article 7 against unlawful workplace custom.[^67]

### Mistaken Procedural Belief on Appeal Withdrawal

Counsel of record filed a Motion to Withdraw Appeal in the Court of Appeals and thereafter ceased coordinating with the law office whose address he used, ignoring appellate resolutions issued on September 20 and November 11, 2010 directing proof of his client's conformity before denying withdrawal on February 23, 2011.[^68] He waited until March 2014 to update his address and pleaded an honest belief under Rule 50, Section 3 that filing the motion terminated the case.

The Supreme Court found his disobedience willful though not malicious, suspended him from the practice of law for one year for violating Canons 7, 10, 11, 12 and 18 and Rules 18.03 and 18.04 of the Code of Professional Responsibility with a stern warning and directed a second counsel to show cause within ten days. Filing a motion to withdraw an appeal does not automatically terminate an appeal without a court resolution. A lawyer cannot excuse noncompliance through mistaken procedural belief because ignorance of the law excuses no one. The decision shows that counsel remains duty-bound to monitor pending motions until resolved by court order.[^69]

### Statutory Deadline to Select Agricultural Successor

Sisters sued their deceased brother's widow 17 years after the landowner's administrator designated the brother as sole agricultural lessee in 1979, relying on a 1996 affidavit from the administrator recounting the brother's supposed oral admission of an alternate-farming agreement.[^70] The Provincial Agrarian Reform Adjudicator, Department of Agrarian Reform Adjudication Board and Court of Appeals all ruled for the widow.

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It held the administrator's affidavit recounting the decedent's alleged statement inadmissible under the Dead Man's Statute, declined to rule on land conversion and ruled that petitioners slept on their rights for 17 years. The administrator could not claim ignorance of the one-month selection deadline under Section 9 of Republic Act No. 3844, so she was deemed to have chosen the brother. The sisters were held to the same compliance. The decision shows that Article 3 prevents a landowner's representative and the other heirs from unwinding statutory leasehold succession decades later.[^71]

### Honest Belief Excuse in Agrarian Amortization Default

Landowner heirs sued a Presidential Decree No. 27 tenant-beneficiary and his children for unpaid palay amortizations and cancellation of registered emancipation patents.[^72] Agrarian tribunals ordered amortization payments but excused default on the ground that respondents labored under an "honest belief" of absolute ownership and could not understand annotated liens. The Court of Appeals dismissed the heirs' review petition because only four of six co-heirs signed the verification and certification.

The Supreme Court granted the petition, set aside the dismissal and remanded the case to the Court of Appeals. It ruled that four co-heirs sharing a common interest substantially complied with the certification rule and confirmed exclusive Adjudication Board jurisdiction over registered patents. Observing on a tentative basis that the "honest belief" excuse appeared to lack legal ground under Section 2 of Presidential Decree No. 816 because ignorance of the law excuses no one, the Supreme Court left deliberate refusal to pay, patent validity and interest to the Court of Appeals on remand. The decision shows that agrarian tribunals cannot excuse statutory amortization defaults on pleaded misunderstanding of annotated title liens.[^73]

## Key Cases

- [*D.M. Consunji, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52124): Limits Article 3 to mandatory and prohibitory laws and excludes permissive *Floresca* remedy choices. The Supreme Court remanded the case to the trial court to compare its award with the Employees' Compensation Commission award and affirmed the Court of Appeals in all other respects.
- [*Intengan v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50280): Holds existing statutes subject to mandatory judicial notice and bars prescription tolling when filing under the wrong bank secrecy statute. The Supreme Court denied the petition.
- [*People v. Siton*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49720): Rejects a *Papachristou* fair-notice vagueness challenge to vagrancy under the Spanish-derived ignorance rule. The Supreme Court granted the People's petition and reversed the trial court's declaration of unconstitutionality.
- [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309): Applies Article 3 to presume knowledge of Family Code marriage laws in an Article 350 conviction, qualified by absence of sufficient contrary evidence. The Supreme Court set aside the Bigamy conviction and convicted him under Article 350 with an indeterminate sentence of six months of *arresto mayor* to three years, six months and 21 days of *prision correccional*.
- [*Marbella-Bobis v. Bobis*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37897): Refuses to treat ignorance of Family Code Article 40 or a subsequent nullity suit as a prejudicial question stopping bigamy trial. The Supreme Court granted the petition and ordered the trial court to proceed with the bigamy case immediately.
- [*Benito v. Balindong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48870): Contrasts ordinary presumed knowledge with judges' affirmative duty to actually know and understand the law. The Supreme Court fined the judge P30,000 for gross ignorance of the law and P10,000 for violating the Lawyer's Oath and Canons of the Code of Professional Responsibility.
- [*Lantaco, Sr. v. Llamas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/am_1037_1981.html): Dismisses a city judge for gross ignorance of statute and settled Supreme Court doctrine under Articles 3 and 8. The Supreme Court dismissed the judge with forfeiture of retirement privileges.
- [*Castillo-Co v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64563): Holds that *Arias* does not shield reliance on a private lawyer's opinion or defiance of a local legislative board resolution. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Sandiganbayan *in toto*.
- [*Jagualing v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/mar1991/gr_94283_1991.html): Denies good-faith possession under Articles 3 and 526 to river-island occupants claiming ignorance of Article 465. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals.
- [*Yu v. Heirs of Sia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68423): Places a hotel owner *in pari delicto* under Article 1411 because she is charged with knowledge of Building Code room standards. The Supreme Court dismissed the complaint and the counterclaim and set aside the Court of Appeals decision.
- [*Acabal v. Acabal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43774): Rejects a seller's claimed ignorance and holds Article 1416 inapplicable to a landowner conveying conjugal land. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and declared both sales valid only up to the seller's five-ninths share.
- [*People v. Gasacao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41800) and [*People v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35343): Bar unawareness, good faith and mercy pleas in *malum prohibitum* illegal recruitment. The Supreme Court denied the petition in *Gasacao* and affirmed the conviction *in toto* in *Reyes*.
- [*Tabao v. Cabcabin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61874): Combines Article 3 and Article 7 to reject inherited office practice as an excuse for unauthorized acts. The Supreme Court found the sheriff guilty of Simple Misconduct and fined him P5,000.00.

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1: Permissive choice of remedies versus mandatory rule**

A construction worker died on the job. Ten days before any police investigation report existed, his widow signed a claim form and received initial pension benefits under the Labor Code. Several months later, she sued the employer for civil damages. The employer moved to dismiss, citing Civil Code Articles 3 and 8 to argue that she was presumed to know the *Floresca* doctrine allowing a choice between compensation benefits and a civil suit and had waived the civil action. Should the motion to dismiss be granted?

No. Under [*D.M. Consunji, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52124),[^76] the application of Article 3 is limited to mandatory and prohibitory laws. Because the *Floresca* choice of remedies is a permissive rule rather than a mandatory or prohibitory one, Article 3 does not charge the widow with knowledge of that choice or bar her civil action. Moreover, where there is no proof that she knew the cause of death when signing the compensation claim form, no valid waiver exists. To prevent double recovery, however, the trial court must determine whether its award exceeds the Employees' Compensation Commission benefits and deduct Labor Code payments received.

**Question 2: Reliance on private counsel in local procurement**

A provincial governor was authorized by the Sangguniang Panlalawigan to procure a loan solely for brand-new heavy equipment. Acting on a recommendation from the provincial engineer and a legal opinion from a private lawyer acting as consultant, the governor bought reconditioned equipment and authorized a prohibited 40% advance payment. Prosecuted under Section 3(g) of Republic Act No. 3019, she invoked good faith under *Arias* and her lawyer's advice. Is her defense valid?

No. In [*Castillo-Co v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64563),[^77] the Supreme Court held that the *Arias* doctrine neither excuses an executive who ignores an explicit Sangguniang Panlalawigan resolution on a subordinate's recommendation nor shields reliance on a private legal consultant who is not a subordinate public officer of the agency. Because everyone is presumed to know the law, a mistake of law cannot justify an illegal act. Reliance on private counsel's legal opinion likewise will not exculpate the official.

**Question 3: Good-faith possession of a newly formed river island**

Occupants settled on an island formed in a non-navigable river and possessed it openly for about fifteen years. When the riparian owner sued to quiet title, the occupants claimed acquisitive prescription in good faith, asserting they did not know that Civil Code Article 465 gives preferential ownership to riparian owners. Did they acquire the island by ordinary prescription?

No. Under [*Jagualing v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/mar1991/gr_94283_1991.html),[^78] occupants are presumed to have notice of the riparian owner's preferential right recognized by law. Under Articles 3 and 526 of the Civil Code, claimed ignorance of Article 465 is not a valid defense to support good-faith possession. Lacking good faith, the occupants may acquire ownership only through uninterrupted adverse possession for thirty years, so fifteen years of possession is insufficient.

# Quotes

> "The argument has no merit. The application of Article 3 is limited to mandatory and prohibitory laws. This may be deduced from the language of the provision, which, notwithstanding a person's ignorance, does not excuse his or her compliance with the laws. The rule in Floresca allowing private respondent a choice of remedies is neither mandatory nor prohibitory. Accordingly, her ignorance thereof cannot be held against her."[^79]

> "It may well be argued that the foregoing disquisition would leave petitioners with no remedy in law. We point out, however, that the confidentiality of foreign currency deposits mandated by Republic Act No. 6426, as amended by Presidential Decree No. 1246, came into effect as far back as 1977. Hence, ignorance thereof cannot be pretended. On one hand, the existence of laws is a matter of mandatory judicial notice; on the other, ignorantia legis non excusat. Even during the pendency of this appeal, nothing prevented the petitioners from filing a complaint charging the correct offense against private respondents. This was not done, as everyone involved was content to submit the case on the basis of an alleged violation of Republic Act No. 1405 (Bank Secrecy Law), however, incorrectly invoked."[^80]

> "The said underlying principle in Papachristou that the Jacksonville ordinance, or Article 202 (2) in this case, fails to give fair notice of what constitutes forbidden conduct, finds no application here because under our legal system, ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. This principle is of Spanish origin, and we adopted it to govern and limit legal conduct in this jurisdiction. Under American law, ignorance of the law is merely a traditional rule that admits of exceptions."[^81]

> "In any event, over and above the foregoing pieces of evidence, Article 3 of the Civil Code clearly states that "[i]gnorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith." The accused is presumed to know the law, including the provisions of the Family Code on legal impediments to marriage, the essential and formal requisites for its validity, and the requirement of a prior judgment declaring the first marriage void for the purpose of remarriage. This presumption stands against Rommel, absent any sufficient evidence to the contrary."[^82]

> "Ignorance of the existence of Article 40 of the Family Code cannot even be successfully invoked as an excuse. The contracting of a marriage knowing that the requirements of the law have not been complied with or that the marriage is in disregard of a legal impediment is an act penalized by the Revised Penal Code. The legality of a marriage is a matter of law and every person is presumed to know the law. As respondent did not obtain the judicial declaration of nullity when he entered into the second marriage, why should he be allowed to belatedly obtain that judicial declaration in order to delay his criminal prosecution and subsequently defeat it by his own disobedience of the law? If he wants to raise the nullity of the previous marriage, he can do it as a matter of defense when he presents his evidence during the trial proper in the criminal case."[^83]

> "We find as flimsy and self serving appellant's assertion that he was unaware of the prohibition against the collection of bonds or cash deposits from applicants. It is an established dictum that ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. The defense of good faith is neither available."[^84]

> "In regard to appellant’s plea for mercy, further consideration of the matter is precluded by the axiom that ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. In any case, his allegation of lack of criminal intent is unavailing. Illegal recruitment in large scale which is penalized by the Labor Code is malum prohibitum, not malum in se. The fact alone that a person violated the law warrants his conviction."[^85]

> "If ordinary people are presumed to know the law, judges are duty-bound to actually know and understand it. A contrary rule will not only lessen the faith of the people in the courts but will also defeat the fundamental role of the judiciary to render justice and promote the rule of law."[^86]

> "Decisions of the Supreme Court need not be proved as they are matters of judicial notice (Sec. 1, Rule 129, Rev. Rules of Court; V Moran, Rules of Court, 1970 ed., pp. 38-39). Ignorance of the law excuses no one (Art. 3, New Civil Code) and judicial decisions applying or interpreting the law or the Constitution are part of the legal system (Art. 8, New Civil Code)."[^87]

> "Moreover, Gov. Co cannot hide behind the cloak of ignorance or lack of familiarity with the provisions of the law. It is settled in our jurisdiction that ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. Corollarily, a mistake of law cannot be used to justify an illegal act because everyone IS presumed to know the law and the consequences of its violation."[^88]

> "It is settled that ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. As such, when Rosemarie approved the building plan, she is deemed to have done so with the knowledge of the minimum standard requirements under PD 1096."[^89]

> "More. Villaner cannot feign ignorance of the law, nor claim that he acted in good faith, let alone assert that he is less guilty than Leonardo. Under Article 3 of the Civil Code, “ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith.”"[^90]

> "Herein, the petitioner himself admitted that he did not apply for such exemption, thus, it is clear that he cannot claim benefits under the law. The petitioner cannot shield himself from complying with the law by the lone fact that he is just a layman and cannot be expected to know of the law's requirements. Under our legal system, ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. Furthermore, the policy of the Labor Code, under which R.A. No. 6727 is premised, is to include all establishments, except a few specific classes, under the coverage of the law. As the petitioner failed to apply for an exemption, and it is undisputed that the respondents are MPRB's employees and are paid less than the prescribed minimum wage, the petitioner's liability for wage differential cannot be denied."[^91]

> "The sum total of these absurdities in petitioners’ theory is that barangay officials are estopped from asking for any term other than that which they ran for and were elected to, under the law governing their very claim to such offices: namely, RA 7160, the Local Government Code. Petitioners’ belated claim of ignorance as to what law governed their election to office in 1994 is unacceptable because under Art. 3 of the Civil Code, “(i)gnorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith.”"[^92]

> "Thus, respondent cannot excuse himself from complying with the Court of Appeals' July 20, 2010 Notice simply because he "belie[ved] that the case has long been closed and terminated" when he filed the Motion to Withdraw Appeal. Ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance. Respondent could not safely assume that the case had already been closed and terminated until he received the Court of Appeals resolution on the matter."[^93]

# Tips

### Diagnostic Checklist

1. Classify the rule as mandatory, prohibitory or permissive before applying Article 3. If the doctrine offers a choice of remedies like *Floresca* in [*D.M. Consunji, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52124), Article 3 does not penalize unawareness.
2. Separate mistake of fact from mistake of law. Check whether the record shows absence of factual knowledge at signing rather than ignorance of a statutory duty, as shown in [*D.M. Consunji*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52124).
3. Verify both statutes and settled judicial decisions. Under [*Intengan v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50280), [*Lantaco, Sr. v. Llamas*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/am_1037_1981.html) and [*Navotas Industrial Corp. v. Guanzon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68038), courts take judicial notice of statutes and jurisprudence.
4. Check whether the defense rests on any special exception or contrary evidence. In [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), the Supreme Court treated Article 3's presumption of marriage-law knowledge as standing absent sufficient evidence to the contrary.
5. Identify the actor's legal role and heightened duties. Judges, public officials, lawyers and regulated professionals face stricter accountability under [*Benito v. Balindong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48870), [*Castillo-Co v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64563), [*In re: Mortel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62353) and [*Civil Service Commission v. Rodriguez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66494).

### Common Traps

> [!TIP]
> Never assume filing under an analogous or repealed statute preserves your action. In [*Intengan v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50280), filing under Republic Act No. 1405 failed to toll prescription for a Republic Act No. 6426 violation.

> [!NOTE]
> Layperson status, private legal advice, inherited workplace custom and public-benefit motives do not defeat mandatory rules. The Supreme Court rejected those excuses in [*Yu v. Heirs of Sia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68423), [*Pablico v. Cerro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65235), [*Castillo-Co v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64563), [*Tabao v. Cabcabin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61874) and [*Office of the Deputy Ombudsman for Luzon v. Dionisio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63223).

### What Worked and What Failed

Claims succeeded only where Article 3 did not reach the issue or where another statutory failure defeated prosecution. In [*D.M. Consunji, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52124), limiting Article 3 to mandatory and prohibitory laws preserved the widow's civil tort award subject to deduction of Labor Code payments. In [*Genio v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69309), the Bigamy conviction under Article 349 was set aside on reasonable doubt because the prosecution did not prove the elements of a valid or voidable second marriage, although Article 3 still supported guilt under Article 350. In [*Acabal v. Acabal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43774), the buyers defeated annulment because the seller failed to prove deception and held a valid five-ninths (`5/9`) co-ownership share under Articles 160 and 493.

Defenses consistently failed whenever parties invoked ignorance of mandatory statutes or regulations, good faith in *malum prohibitum* offenses, *Papachristou* vagueness against Philippine criminal statutes, private legal opinions against board resolutions, unverified civil service rules, inherited office practices or pending withdrawal motions without a court order, as shown in [*People v. Siton*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49720), [*People v. Gasacao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41800), [*People v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35343), [*People v. Neri*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1985/dec1985/gr_l-37762_1985.html), [*Marbella-Bobis v. Bobis*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37897), [*Suyat v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68798), [*Catipon v. Japson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60516), [*David v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34756), [*Jagualing v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/mar1991/gr_94283_1991.html), [*Garcia v. Vda. de Caparas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55807) and [*Heirs of Lazaro Gallardo v. Soliman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55745).
