> ART. 4. Laws shall have no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided. (3)

[^civil-code]

# Explanation

## Nature, Rationale and General Rule of Prospectivity

Article 4 of the Civil Code establishes the general rule that laws shall have no retroactive effect unless the contrary is provided.[^2] Statutes formulate rules for the future rather than the past, expressed in the maxim *lex de futuro, judex de praeterito* and *lex prospicit, non respicit*.[^3] Retroactive laws tend to be unjust and oppressive because they can unsettle vested rights or disturb the legal effect of prior transactions.[^4]

Retroactivity under Article 4 is a statutory norm in the Civil Code rather than an independent constitutional standard.[^5] Congress may validly provide that a law shall operate retroactively unless doing so impairs a vested right to life, liberty or property protected by due process.[^6]

In [*Commission on Elections v. Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50669), incumbent barangay officials in Caloocan City filed a petition for declaratory relief in the Regional Trial Court before the October 29, 2007 barangay elections to challenge the proviso in Section 2 of Republic Act No. 9164 that reckoned their three-term limit from the 1994 barangay elections.[^7] The trial court agreed with the officials and declared the proviso unconstitutional on the ground that it retroactively counted pre-enactment terms.[^8] Reversing the trial court on appeal by the Commission on Elections, the Supreme Court En Banc held that the challenged proviso merely continued the three-term rule existing under prior statutes and that no one holds a constitutionally protected vested right to an elective public office.[^9]

Non-retroactivity is strictly a question of law. Equitable considerations cannot override Article 4 when a statute supplies the governing rule.[^10] [^11] In [*Philippine Deposit Insurance Corporation v. Stockholders of Intercity Savings and Loan Bank, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51088), the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas petitioned in 1987 for the liquidation of Intercity Savings and Loan Bank, Inc. Creditors received payment of their principal claims in 2002 before Republic Act No. 9302 took effect in 2004.[^12] The Regional Trial Court denied the Philippine Deposit Insurance Corporation's motion to award surplus interest dividends to creditors under Section 12 of Republic Act No. 9302 while the Court of Appeals dismissed the liquidator's ordinary appeal on the ground that the pure legal question belonged under Rule 45.[^13] Relaxing procedural rules to resolve the merits, the Supreme Court affirmed and held that Section 28 of Republic Act No. 9302 made the statute effective 15 days after publication without any retroactivity clause, barring surplus dividends on pre-2004 payments.[^14]

In [*4E Steel Builders Corporation v. Maybank Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69009), a borrower and accommodation mortgagors defaulted on credit-line loans obtained from Maybank Philippines, Inc., a foreign bank, which then purchased five mortgaged lots at a November 21, 2003 foreclosure sale.[^15] The Court of Appeals annulled the foreclosure sale because Republic Act No. 4882 then disqualified foreign banks from bidding or acquiring mortgaged real property at foreclosure. Both sides elevated petitions to the Supreme Court.[^16] Rejecting Maybank's plea to apply Republic Act No. 10641 (enacted in 2014) retroactively on grounds of equity and social justice, the Supreme Court affirmed the annulment because equity applies only in the absence of statutory law and Republic Act No. 10641 lacked a retroactivity clause under Article 4. The Supreme Court denied both petitions and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification. It annulled the foreclosure sale and cancelled the certificate of sale. It ordered Maybank and the borrowers to appoint an independent accountant to compute the outstanding loan. The borrowers must pay that balance with legal interest.[^17]

Taxing statutes are likewise construed prospectively unless their language clearly demands retroactivity.[^18] In [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. Filipinas Compañia de Seguros*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1960/apr1960/gr_l-14880_1960.html), an insurance company acting also as a real estate dealer paid in full on January 4, 1956 the P150.00 annual fixed tax then required by Section 182 of the National Internal Revenue Code.[^19] When Republic Act No. 1612 took effect upon approval on August 24, 1956 and introduced higher graduated rates up to P500.00, the Commissioner of Internal Revenue assessed P350.00 in additional tax for 1956, prompting a petition for review before the Court of Tax Appeals.[^20] The Court of Tax Appeals ordered the Commissioner to stop collecting the additional tax. The Supreme Court En Banc affirmed because applying Republic Act No. 1612 to a 1956 fixed tax already paid in January 1956 would violate Section 21's express effectivity clause and the rule against retroactive tax burdens under Article 4.[^21]

Every case of doubt is resolved against retroactive operation unless legislative intent to the contrary appears expressly or by necessary implication.[^22]

## Statutes, Effectivity Clauses and Administrative Issuances

Where a statute, executive order, administrative rule or local ordinance fixes its effectivity upon approval or after publication without an express retroactivity clause, it governs future transactions only.[^23] [^24] Administrative rules and circulars derive their authority from the statutes they implement and share the same prospective limitation under Article 4.[^25] [^26]

In [*Tetangco, Jr. v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65748), the Commission on Audit disallowed increases in per diems and representation and transportation allowances paid between January 2010 and February 2011 to ex-officio directors of the Philippine International Convention Center, Inc. who were Bangko Sentral officials, citing lack of presidential approval under Executive Order No. 24.[^27] Because Executive Order No. 24 took effect only on March 21, 2011 and lacked any retroactivity provision under Article 4, the Supreme Court En Banc granted certiorari and nullified the disallowances, holding that the order could not deauthorize benefits granted prior to its effectivity.[^28]

In [*Curata v. Philippine Ports Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49359), the Philippine Ports Authority instituted expropriation proceedings on October 14, 1999 in the Regional Trial Court of Batangas City to expand the Batangas Port Zone under Rule 67 of the Rules of Court and Administrative Order No. 50.[^29] After Republic Act No. 8974 took effect on November 26, 2000, requiring an initial deposit equal to 100 percent of Bureau of Internal Revenue zonal value upon filing, the trial court and the Court of Appeals applied Republic Act No. 8974 to pending proceedings and ordered substantially higher deposits and valuations.[^30] Modifying the consolidated rulings on review, the Supreme Court En Banc held that just compensation standards and provisional deposit prerequisites in Republic Act No. 8974 create substantive proprietary rights rather than mere procedure. Under Article 4, the 2000 statute could not apply retroactively to the 1999 expropriation suit. The Supreme Court declared that Rule 67 and not Republic Act No. 8974 governed the case. It annulled the orders that had applied the 2000 statute and reduced just compensation from P5,500.00 to P425.00 per square meter.[^31]

The same rule of statutory prospectivity bars retroactive application across public procurement, housing regulation, mining transfers, forestry classification, insurance renewals, corporate privatization and labor appeal requirements. In [*Abaya v. Ebdane, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41019), the Supreme Court dismissed a Rule 65 challenge to a foreign-assisted Catanduanes highway contract and held that Republic Act No. 9184 (effective January 26, 2003) did not govern bidding whose invitation was published in November and December 2002 under Executive Order No. 40.[^32] In [*William G. Kwong Management, Inc. v. Diamond Homeowners & Residents Association*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65345), the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals in upholding a subdivision's 2006 security policy because Republic Act No. 9904 (2010) affected substantive rights and could not retroactively invalidate prior rules.[^33] In [*Manapat v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44546), the Supreme Court reversed Court of Appeals resolutions in part and ruled that the small-property-owner exemption in Republic Act No. 7279 (1992) operated prospectively under Section 49 and Article 4, leaving 1977 expropriation suits unaffected.[^34]

Additional applications confirm the breadth of prospectivity across regulatory regimes. Administrative issuances cannot retroactively annul recall steps already underway under an earlier Commission on Elections resolution ([*Sanchez v. Commission on Elections*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/jan1991/gr_94459_1991.html)),[^35] defer the start of a veteran's widow pension filed before the rules existed ([*Limoico v. Board of Administrators, Philippine Veterans Affairs Office*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/oct1984/gr_l-40244_1984.html)),[^36] or disallow prior-year allowances under an executive order limited to a later fiscal year ([*Home Development Mutual Fund v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46233)).[^37] Substantive statutes cannot impose presidential approval requirements on pre-existing mining agreements ([*Lepanto Consolidated Mining Co. v. WMC Resources Int'l. Pty. Ltd.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42137)),[^38] reclassify previously patented private property into public forest ([*SAAD Agro-Industries, Inc. v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43670)),[^39] invalidate prior insurance renewals ([*ACME Shoe, Rubber & Plastic Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1985/jan1985/gr_l-56718_1985.html)),[^40] strip the Civil Service Commission of jurisdiction over an employee appeal pending before bank privatization ([*Philippine National Bank v. Tejano, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50526)) [^41] or impose new appeal bonds and immediate execution on already perfected labor appeals ([*Callanta v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/aug1993/gr_105083_1993.html)[^42] and [*Inciong v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/may1990/gr_88943_1990.html)).[^43]

## Vested Rights, Ongoing Situations and Agrarian Laws

A law is not retroactive merely because it applies to persons or transactions in existence when it takes effect, so long as it governs future events and impairs no vested right.[^44] [^45] A vested right is a present fixed interest that has become complete and unconditional, not a mere expectancy founded on the anticipated continuance of existing law.[^46] [^47]

This distinction appears sharply in two military retirement decisions involving Section 27 of Presidential Decree No. 1638, signed on September 10, 1979, which terminates pension benefits upon a retiree's loss of Filipino citizenship. In [*Parreño v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43838), an Armed Forces officer who remained in active service in 1979 and retired only on January 5, 1982 later became a naturalized United States citizen, prompting the military and the Commission on Audit to stop his pension in January 2001.[^48] Affirming the Commission on Audit's denial of his claim, the Supreme Court En Banc held that although Presidential Decree No. 1638 applies prospectively under Article 4, applying it to personnel still in active service in 1979 who retired in 1982 is prospective because military retirement rights vest only upon retirement.[^49]

By contrast, in [*Carolino v. Senga*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60398), Colonel Jeremias Carolino retired in December 1976 under Republic Act No. 340 and received monthly pensions until March 2005, when the Armed Forces terminated his pension for acquiring United States citizenship.[^50] Although the Regional Trial Court granted mandamus to restore his pension, the Court of Appeals reversed on the theory that Presidential Decree No. 1638 repealed Republic Act No. 340.[^51] Setting aside the Court of Appeals' decision and reinstating the trial court's judgment, the Supreme Court held that Presidential Decree No. 1638 operated prospectively under Article 4 and its own Sections 33 and 35. Therefore, it could not divest retirement benefits that had already vested upon Carolino's 1976 retirement.[^52]

Similarly, in [*Council of Teachers and Staff of Colleges and Universities of the Philippines v. Secretary of Education*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64679), faculty groups challenged the K to 12 Law (Republic Act No. 10533) as an unconstitutional retroactive impairment of students' asserted right under Batas Pambansa Blg. 232 to graduate after four years of high school.[^53] Upholding the statute's constitutionality, the Supreme Court En Banc ruled that the program applies prospectively to students still enrolled and future entrants while excluding prior graduates. It added that Batas Pambansa Blg. 232 created no unconditional vested right to a four-year curriculum.[^54] Likewise, in [*Alvarez v. PICOP Resources, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51009), requiring local legislative consultation under Sections 26 and 27 of the 1991 Local Government Code for a timber company's 2002 to 2027 forest management conversion did not violate Article 4 even though the company's original timber license dated back to 1969.[^55] Nor do administrative circulars and inter-agency memoranda violate Article 4 when they merely establish verification procedures to enforce existing import tax liabilities and a seller's express warranty under Article 1546 of the Civil Code ([*Harrison Motors Corporation v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54382)).[^56]

In agrarian reform, the Supreme Court En Banc resolved conflicting appellate rulings in [*Nilo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/apr1984/gr_l-34586_1984.html).[^57] There, landowners filed agrarian ejectment suits before September 10, 1971 based on personal cultivation under Section 36(1) of Republic Act No. 3844, after which Section 7 of Republic Act No. 6389 deleted personal cultivation as a ground for ejectment.[^58] Upholding ejectment in one consolidated appeal and remanding the other for trial on the merits, the Supreme Court En Banc held that despite floor-debate statements by individual legislators, Republic Act No. 6389 contained no express or necessarily implied retroactivity under Article 4 and therefore did not abate pending ejectment actions.[^59] Subsequent divisions uniformly applied [*Nilo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/apr1984/gr_l-34586_1984.html) in [*Balatbat v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jan1992/gr_36378_1992.html)[^60], [*Nepomuceno v. Salazar*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/may1989/gr_37165_1989.html)[^61] and [*Gallardo v. Borromeo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/may1988/gr_l-36007_1988.html),[^62] while later rulings held that Letter of Instructions No. 729 and Presidential Decrees Nos. 27 and 316 operated prospectively only so as not to defeat pre-1988 municipal zoning reclassifications ([*Buklod nang Magbubukid sa Lupaing Ramos, Inc. v. E.M. Ramos and Sons, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37024)[^63] and [*Ong v. Imperial*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61215)),[^64] pre-1972 conversion cases ([*Coloso v. Garilao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42752)) [^65] or a tenant's 1970 statutory redemption judgment ([*Baltazar v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/may1981/gr_40191_1981.html)).[^66]

## Constitutional Contract Impairment and Credit Preference

Article 4 avoids constitutional conflict with the non-impairment clause under Section 10, Article III of the 1987 Constitution by restricting amendatory statutes that alter existing security interests to prospective operation.[^67]

In [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/mar1990/gr_82763_1990.html), the Development Bank of the Philippines held prior real estate mortgages over properties of Lirag Textile Mills, Inc. and foreclosed them before Republic Act No. 6715 amended Article 110 of the Labor Code on March 21, 1989 to expand workers' wage preference to other monetary claims.[^68] The National Labor Relations Commission ordered the bank to remit P6,292,380.00 from the foreclosure proceeds to pay separated employees.[^69] Granting certiorari and setting aside the commission's order, the Supreme Court En Banc ruled that Article 110 creates an ordinary preferred credit rather than a specific lien under Articles 2241, 2242 and 2244 of the Civil Code and requires *in rem* insolvency proceedings. Even if amended Article 110 were read as an absolute preference, Article 4 mandates prospective application so as not to wipe out pre-existing mortgage credits in violation of the non-impairment clause.[^70]

The Supreme Court applied this same ruling in its June 27, 1990 decision in [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/jun1990/gr_86932_1990.html)[^71], its January 19, 1994 decision in [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/jan1994/gr_86227_1994.html)[^72] and En Banc in [*Hautea v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/feb1994/gr_96149_1994.html).[^73] These rulings protected prior bank mortgages and pre-1989 foreclosures against retroactive application of Republic Act No. 6715. Conversely, where valid police-power regulation protecting helpless subdivision buyers does not unconstitutionally destroy security without public justification, necessary legislative intent may displace non-impairment objections, as seen in [*Philippine National Bank v. Office of the President*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33441).[^74]

## Exceptions to Non-Retroactivity Under Article 4

Article 4 explicitly recognizes legislative power to give statutes retroactive operation when "the contrary is provided," and settled jurisprudence recognizes narrow companion exceptions where retroactivity is necessarily implied or where the law is curative, remedial or procedural, creates new rights without impairing vested rights or is a penal statute favorable to an accused who is not a habitual criminal.[^75] [^76] [^77]

### Express Retroactivity and Necessary Implication

When the rule-making authority or legislature expressly directs retroactive application to all pending cases, that directive governs even if its effect is unfavorable to the respondent.[^78]

In [*Usama v. Tomarong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68944), the widow of a police officer killed in a 2016 shooting filed an administrative complaint against a Regional Trial Court judge who had granted P200,000.00 bail without hearing to murder suspects before an Information was filed and who retired on April 1, 2019.[^79] Applying the Revised Rule 140 of the Rules of Court (A.M. No. 21-08-09-SC, effective April 4, 2022), the Supreme Court En Banc cited Section 24 of Revised Rule 140, which directs retroactive application to all pending and future administrative cases involving judiciary personnel even when prejudicial to the employee. The explanatory note to that section states that the Supreme Court effectively abandons *Dela Rama v. De Leon*. The Supreme Court fined the retired judge P220,000.00.[^80]

Even without an explicit retroactivity clause, retroactive effect may arise by clear and necessary implication from the preamble, structure and remedial provisions of a statute, though courts apply this exception with strict restraint.[^81] [^82] Thus, in [*Philippine National Bank v. Office of the President*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33441), installment lot buyers who had already bought lots and built homes before Marikina Village, Inc. mortgaged the subdivision to Philippine National Bank on December 18, 1975 challenged the bank's attempt to disregard their installment contracts after foreclosure.[^83] Both the Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board and the Office of the President applied Presidential Decree No. 957 (effective July 12, 1976) to restrict the bank to collecting only remaining amortizations.[^84] Affirming, the Supreme Court held that although Presidential Decree No. 957 contains no general retroactivity clause, its preamble and Sections 20, 21 and 23 unmistakably imply legislative intent to cover antecedent 1975 subdivision mortgages as a social-justice police-power measure.[^85]

Yet later decisions strictly cabined this implication to its statutory context. In [*Dueñas v. Santos Subdivision Homeowners Association*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46915), the Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and held that Presidential Decree No. 957 and Presidential Decree No. 1216 (October 14, 1977) contained no express retroactivity provision under Article 4 and could not retroactively compel open-space dedication for subdivision plans already approved in 1966 where neither non-development nor unpaid amortizations existed.[^86] Likewise, in [*People's Industrial and Commercial Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35279), the Supreme Court affirmed the trial court and the Court of Appeals in ruling that neither Presidential Decrees Nos. 957 and 1344 nor Republic Act No. 6552 applied retroactively to revive 1961 contracts to sell that had already been automatically cancelled in October 1971 under contractual default clauses.[^87] Meanwhile, in [*Puzon v. Abellera*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jan1989/gr_75082_1989.html), the Supreme Court held that Presidential Decree No. 1271 (December 22, 1977) necessarily retroacts upon statutory compliance to validate pre-July 31, 1973 Baguio townsite titles from their original issue dates, subjecting the validated land to 1971 to 1977 realty taxes even while voiding a premature October 10, 1977 auction for lack of notice.[^88]

### Curative, Remedial, Procedural and New-Rights Statutes

Curative statutes, rules regulating procedure and statutes creating new rights without impairing vested rights generally apply retroactively to pending actions as exceptions to Article 4.[^89] [^90]

In [*Republic v. Pasig Rizal Co., Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68138), Pasig Rizal Co., Inc. applied in 2010 for original registration of a 944-square-meter parcel in Pasig City possessed by its predecessors since 1956.[^91] The Regional Trial Court confirmed title on December 1, 2011 and the Court of Appeals affirmed on February 25, 2014, after which the Republic petitioned the Supreme Court under Rule 45.[^92] While the appeal was pending, Republic Act No. 11573 took effect on September 1, 2021, shortening the required possession period under Section 14(1) of Presidential Decree No. 1529 from possession since June 12, 1945 to 20 years and revising proof of land classification in Section 7.[^93] Affirming the corporation's uncontroverted possession since 1956 and remanding to the Court of Appeals for Section 7 land-classification evidence, the Supreme Court En Banc held that although Republic Act No. 11573 lacks a retroactivity clause, it applies retroactively to all applications pending before trial courts or the Court of Appeals on September 1, 2021 because it is curative and creates new rights without impairing vested rights.[^94]

On the same principle, procedural and jurisdictional allocations generally govern pending cases. In [*Villaseñor v. Ombudsman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57009), Administrative Order No. 17 amending the Ombudsman's Rules of Procedure to make dismissal and suspension orders immediately executory pending appeal took effect on September 7, 2003, after an investigating panel's June 17, 2003 joint decision on the Manor Hotel fire but before the Ombudsman approved and enforced it.[^95] Affirming the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court held that procedural rules regulating execution apply to pending administrative cases and infringe no vested right in public office or mode of procedure.[^96] In [*Rajah Humabon Hotel, Inc. v. Trajano*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_100222_1993.html), employees filed wage claims exceeding P5,000.00 each before a Regional Director on March 14, 1989 prior to Republic Act No. 6715, which soon shifted jurisdiction over money claims above P5,000.00 to Labor Arbiters.[^97] Setting aside the Department of Labor Undersecretary's orders, the Supreme Court held that a subsequent statute reallocating subject-matter jurisdiction operates retroactively like a curative law on pending proceedings and directed referral to the proper Labor Arbiter.[^98]

### Favorable Penal Laws and Constitutional Safeguards

Consistent with Article 22 of the Revised Penal Code, penal statutes apply retroactively insofar as they favor a person guilty of a felony who is not a habitual criminal.[^99] Courts likewise apply favorable amendatory penal statutes retroactively to reduce criminal liability for non-excluded offenses.[^100]

In [*Agote v. Lorenzo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48728), the Regional Trial Court of Manila convicted petitioner in May 1999 of both illegal possession of a firearm under Presidential Decree No. 1866 and election gun-ban violation committed on April 27, 1996, invoking Article 4 of the Civil Code to refuse retroactive application of Republic Act No. 8294 (effective July 6, 1997).[^101] After the Court of Appeals dismissed his improper certiorari petition on jurisdictional and timeliness grounds, the Supreme Court En Banc suspended technical rules to address the substantive injustice.[^102] Reversing in part and dismissing the illegal-possession charge, the Supreme Court held that Republic Act No. 8294, being favorable to the accused, operated retroactively so that no separate offense of simple illegal firearm possession could be prosecuted when another crime (the gun-ban violation) was committed at the same time.[^103]

However, constitutional Bill of Rights guarantees are not "penal laws" within the meaning of Article 22 of the Revised Penal Code and do not acquire automatic retroactivity under that exception.[^104] In [*Filoteo, Jr. v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34163), a police investigator executed an uncounseled custodial waiver and confession on May 30, 1982, prior to the 1983 *Morales v. Enrile* guidelines and the 1987 Constitution. He was convicted by the Sandiganbayan in 1987.[^105] Rejecting his invocation of Article 22 of the Revised Penal Code to apply Section 12(1), Article III of the 1987 Constitution retroactively, the Supreme Court En Banc distinguished charters of individual liberties from penal laws prescribing state punishment and upheld the admissibility of the voluntary 1982 waiver (while modifying the offense from brigandage to robbery).[^106]

## Judicial Decisions Under Article 8 in Relation to Article 4

Article 8 of the Civil Code provides that judicial decisions applying or interpreting the laws or the Constitution form part of the legal system of the Philippines.[^107] Reading Article 8 alongside Article 4 yields a two-part doctrine: judicial construction generally retroacts to the statute's enactment because it merely declares contemporaneous legislative intent, whereas a ruling that overrules a prior settled doctrine or official administrative interpretation operates prospectively only.[^108] [^109]

### General Rule: Statutory Construction Retroacts Absent Overruling

When a Supreme Court decision construes a statute for the first time without overturning an earlier binding precedent or a competent official circular, its interpretation forms part of the statute from its original passage and applies to prior transactions and pending cases.[^110] [^111]

In [*San Miguel Corporation v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68899), the Bureau of Internal Revenue assessed San Miguel Corporation in May 2014 for 2009 deficiency Documentary Stamp Tax on intercompany advances based on *Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. Filinvest Development Corp.* (2011), which held that instructional letters and vouchers evidencing advances constitute taxable loan agreements under the Tax Code.[^112] Both the Court of Tax Appeals Division and En Banc upheld the basic tax assessment while ordering refunds of interest and compromise penalty.[^113] Denying San Miguel's petition and partially granting the Commissioner's petition (ordering refund only of the P50,000.00 compromise penalty while denying interest refund), the Supreme Court held that *Filinvest* established the original legislative intent of the Tax Code without overturning any binding precedent, noting that *APC Group* was a non-binding Minute Resolution and that private BIR rulings issued to other taxpayers could not confer good-faith protection on San Miguel.[^114]

Likewise, in [*Philippine International Trading Corporation v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63590), the Supreme Court En Banc held that its June 22, 2010 ruling restricting Executive Order No. 756 retirement gratuities to the reorganization period retroacted to the executive order's 1981 enactment because it overturned no prior Supreme Court ruling and long-continued illegal disbursements create no vested right.[^115] In [*Republic v. Remman Enterprises, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56544), the Supreme Court reversed the lower courts and applied the 2008 *T.A.N. Properties* evidentiary rule requiring a certified copy of the original land classification to a 2001 land registration application.[^116] In [*Ramos v. National Commission on Indigenous Peoples*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66673), the Supreme Court held that *Unduran* construing Section 66 of the Indigenous Peoples' Rights Act applied retroactively to nullify a 2010 National Commission on Indigenous Peoples injunction because an earlier conflicting remark in *City Government of Baguio v. Masweng* was mere *obiter dictum*.[^117] Finally, in [*Chavez v. Public Estates Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47293), the Supreme Court En Banc denied Amari's plea for prospective application of its 2002 decision annulling the Manila Bay reclaimed-land transfer, holding that the 2002 ruling merely reiterated constitutional prohibitions in force since 1973 and the Spanish Law of Waters while Amari acted without good faith.[^118]

### Prospective Overruling of Settled Doctrine and Administrative Reliance

When the Supreme Court overrules a settled judicial doctrine or when a citizen acts in good faith upon an official administrative interpretation later reversed, the new doctrine must be applied prospectively under Articles 4 and 8 to avoid unfairness and impairment of accrued rights or criminal notice.[^119] [^120]

In [*Co v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/oct1993/gr_100776_1993.html), petitioner issued a postdated guarantee check on September 1, 1983, relying on Ministry of Justice Circular No. 4 (December 15, 1981), which expressly excluded guarantee checks from prosecution under Batas Pambansa Blg. 22.[^121] Although the Ministry reversed that circular on August 8, 1984 (prospectively) and the Supreme Court later held in *Que v. People* (September 21, 1987) that guarantee checks are covered by Batas Pambansa Blg. 22, both the Regional Trial Court of Pasay City and the Court of Appeals convicted petitioner retroactively under *Que*.[^122] Reversing the conviction and dismissing the criminal case, the Supreme Court En Banc held under Articles 4 and 8 that prospectivity governs judicial doctrines and reversals of official Justice Secretary opinions alike, especially in criminal prosecutions where conduct must be reasonably foreseeable.[^123]

The Supreme Court enforced the same safeguard across criminal procedure, public procurement of property rights and appellate modes. In [*Columbia Pictures, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34021), a search warrant issued on December 14, 1987 under then-prevailing *Burgos* standards could not be quashed by applying the August 19, 1988 *20th Century Fox* master-tape requirement retroactively.[^124] In custodial-investigation cases, the April 26, 1983 *Morales-Galit* requirement of counsel during waiver operated prospectively from promulgation: it did not invalidate 1982 voluntary waivers in [*Santos v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51584)[^125] and [*Filoteo, Jr. v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34163),[^126] yet invalidated an uncounseled October 3, 1985 confession taken after April 26, 1983 in [*People v. Binamira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35103).[^127] In [*People v. Martinado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/oct1992/gr_92020_1992.html), a new procedural doctrine deeming escape before judgment a waiver of appeal was applied prospectively so as not to foreclose a 1989 appeal.[^128] In [*Chavez v. National Housing Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44182), the 2002 [*Chavez v. Public Estates Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47293) decision could not retroactively undo 1996 Smokey Mountain reclaimed-land titles acquired for value.[^129]

Even changes in judicial review channels operate prospectively when retroactive enforcement would destroy pending appeals taken in good faith under conflicting precedent or prior rules. In [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. De Leon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48605), the Supreme Court En Banc resolved conflicting Court of Appeals decisions by confirming that appeals from Special Agrarian Courts must proceed by petition for review under Section 60 of Republic Act No. 6657 rather than ordinary appeal, yet granted reconsideration in part to make that ruling prospective so over 60 pending appeals filed via ordinary appeal would not be dismissed on a technicality.[^130] Nearly two decades later, in [*Global Medical Center of Laguna, Inc. v. Ross Systems International, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67423), the Supreme Court En Banc carved Construction Industry Arbitration Commission arbitral awards out of Rule 43 appeals (directing pure law questions to Rule 45 before the Supreme Court and arbitral-integrity challenges to Rule 65 before the Court of Appeals) and abandoned prior precedents, but expressly declared its ruling prospective so pending Rule 43 appeals before the Court of Appeals remained valid.[^131] In [*Dela Calzada v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69315), the Supreme Court En Banc set aside a 2021 Commission on Audit Proper resolution that had *motu proprio* reinstated disallowed employee refund liability by retroactively applying 2019 and 2020 precedents (*Chozas* and *Madera*) to undo an unchallenged 2018 exoneration, holding that prospective overruling and finality of judgment foreclosed such reversal.[^132]

### Prospective Abandonment of the Condonation Doctrine

The landmark sequence abandoning the condonation doctrine illustrates how the Supreme Court defines and enforces the exact cut-off date of a prospective overruling under Articles 4 and 8.[^133] [^134]

In [*Carpio Morales v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377), the Ombudsman challenged temporary restraining and injunction orders issued by the Court of Appeals halting the preventive suspension of Makati City Mayor Jejomar Erwin S. Binay, Jr. based on the 1959 *Pascual* condonation doctrine.[^135] Finding no constitutional or statutory basis for condonation and holding it incompatible with public accountability under the 1987 Constitution, the Supreme Court En Banc abandoned the condonation doctrine, yet explicitly declared the abandonment **prospective** in effect under Articles 4 and 8 so as to respect prior reliance.[^136]

Subsequent decisions clarified what event triggered prospective abandonment:
1. In [*Office of the Ombudsman v. Vergara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63642), the Second Division applied condonation to an official against whom a complaint was filed in 2005 and who was re-elected in 2010 after a gap in terms, emphasizing that the case had been instituted before [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377).[^137]
2. In [*Dator v. Carpio-Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64667), the First Division denied condonation where the complaint was instituted on May 2, 2016 after [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377), while reducing the suspension for good faith.[^138]
3. In [*Herrera v. Mago*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66094), the First Division noted under *Crebello v. Ombudsman* that [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377) attained finality on **April 12, 2016**. It denied condonation even though the complaint had been filed on January 9, 2015 because the official was re-elected only on May 9, 2016.[^139]
4. Finally, in [*Madreo v. Bayron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66828)[^140] and [*Gaudan v. Degamo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67228)[^141], the Supreme Court En Banc settled the reckoning standard. Re-election (whether in a regular or recall election) is the event that triggers and vests the defense under the old condonation doctrine.[^142] Therefore, condonation continues to cover administrative offenses of officials whose re-elections occurred **before April 12, 2016** (even if the Ombudsman complaint was filed or decided later), whereas re-elections on or after April 12, 2016 no longer condone prior misconduct.[^143] [^144] On that basis, En Banc in [*Gaudan v. Degamo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67228) declared Ombudsman Office Circular No. 17, s. 2016 (which had denied condonation to all cases still pending as of April 12, 2016 regardless of re-election date) **null and void**.[^145]

## Pre-1950 Acts and Civil Code Transitional Provisions

Where alleged wrongful acts occurred in 1949 before the Civil Code of the Philippines took effect, Article 4 operates alongside Article 2257 of the Civil Code.[^146]

In [*Strebel v. Figueras*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1954/dec1954/gr_l-4722_1954.html), a gasoline station lessee sued labor and prosecution officials for opinions and criminal charges allegedly issued in 1949, seeking moral damages despite alleging no physical injuries.[^147] Affirming the dismissal ordered by the Court of First Instance of Manila for failure to state a cause of action, the Supreme Court En Banc held that although Article 2219 of the Civil Code allows moral damages for malicious prosecution, Articles 4 and 2257 forbid applying new civil sanctions retroactively to acts committed in 1949 before the Code's effectivity.[^148]

Note that although Bar problems frequently pair Family Code [Article 256](../../family-code/title-12-final-provisions/art-256.md) (retroactive effect of the Family Code unless vested rights are prejudiced) with Civil Code Article 4, none of the decisions cited in this article apply Family Code Article 256 alongside Civil Code Article 4.

## Case Discussions

These discussions give the facts and the disposition of cases that the sections above cite for a single point.

### [*Sanchez v. Commission on Elections*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/jan1991/gr_94459_1991.html): Recall Rules Not Retroactive

In [*Sanchez v. Commission on Elections*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/jan1991/gr_94459_1991.html), defeated mayoral candidate Emmanuel R. Balanon and Manuel D. Cañete filed notices of recall on February 5, 1990 against Butuan City Mayor Guillermo R. Sanchez and Vice-Mayor Carlito T. Tan. The City Election Registrar scheduled signing dates under existing COMELEC Resolution No. 1612.[^149] On May 23, 1990, the Commission on Elections promulgated Resolution No. 2272 (effective June 2, 1990, seven days after May 26 publication) and then issued Minute Resolution No. 90-0590 on June 27, 1990 nullifying the signing process under Resolution No. 1612 and resetting new dates.[^150] Granting prohibition on direct resort, the Supreme Court En Banc voided Minute Resolution No. 90-0590 for grave abuse of discretion, holding that Resolution No. 2272 had no retroactivity clause under Article 4 and could not nullify recall proceedings already started in February 1990.[^151] The case shows that an administrative agency cannot apply new election rules retroactively to abort validly commenced recall proceedings.[^152]

### [*Limoico v. Board of Administrators, Philippine Veterans Affairs Office*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/oct1984/gr_l-40244_1984.html): Start Date of a Widow's Pension

In [*Limoico v. Board of Administrators, Philippine Veterans Affairs Office*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/oct1984/gr_l-40244_1984.html), a World War II veteran's widow filed her pension application on April 23, 1958 under Republic Act No. 65, which the Philippine Veterans Affairs Office approved on May 24, 1972 while paying only from the 1972 approval date under its 1960 Revised Rules.[^153] The Court of First Instance of Manila ordered monthly payments starting from the April 23, 1958 filing date (increased under Republic Act No. 4117 from its effectivity). The Court of Appeals certified the pure question of law.[^154] Affirming, the Supreme Court held that administrative rules promulgated in 1960 under Republic Act No. 2664, like statutes under Article 4, have no retroactive effect unless provided and cannot postpone a valid 1958 pension entitlement.[^155] The case shows that veterans' pension rules operate prospectively and pension legislation favors claimants whose eligibility predated restrictive regulations.[^156]

### [*Home Development Mutual Fund v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46233): Prior-Year Allowances

In [*Home Development Mutual Fund v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46233), the Home Development Mutual Fund Board passed Resolution No. 1313 on June 2, 1997 granting 1996 amelioration allowances to personnel detailed by a private manpower contractor, DBP Service Corporation, which the Commission on Audit disallowed in a 2002 decision and 2003 resolution citing Administrative Order No. 365 (effective October 10, 1997) and Civil Service Omnibus Rules.[^157] On certiorari, the Supreme Court En Banc agreed with the Home Development Mutual Fund that Administrative Order No. 365 lacked a retroactivity clause under Article 4 and governed only fiscal year 1997 rather than 1996, yet affirmed the disallowance because the Omnibus Rules covered only government employees while excusing refund due to good faith.[^158] The case shows that invoking Article 4 against an inapplicable 1997 executive order does not validate a disbursement that already lacked authority under pre-existing rules.[^159]

### [*William G. Kwong Management, Inc. v. Diamond Homeowners & Residents Association*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65345): Subdivision Security Policy

In [*William G. Kwong Management, Inc. v. Diamond Homeowners & Residents Association*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65345), Diamond Homeowners & Residents Association adopted a 2006 "No Sticker, No ID, No Entry" policy to curb crime on subdivision roads in Angeles City, which commercial property owner William G. Kwong Management, Inc. challenged before the Office of the President.[^160] After the Office of the President invalidated the policy in 2010, the Court of Appeals set aside that decision and upheld the policy.[^161] Affirming the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court held that Republic Act No. 9904 (2010 Magna Carta for Homeowners), which imposed consultation and approval conditions for road access restrictions, affects substantive rights and lacks a retroactivity clause under Article 4, so it could not retroactively invalidate the 2006 policy.[^162] The case shows that homeowner policies must be tested under the laws and police-power standards in force when adopted.[^163]

### [*Abaya v. Ebdane, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41019): Procurement Law Effectivity

In [*Abaya v. Ebdane, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41019), taxpayers and petitioners filed a Rule 65 petition to annul a 2004 Department of Public Works and Highways resolution awarding a Japan Bank for International Cooperation-funded road package in Catanduanes to China Road & Bridge Corporation whose bid exceeded the approved budget ceiling.[^164] Petitioners argued that Section 31 of Republic Act No. 9184 (effective January 26, 2003) barred the award, even though the Invitation to Prequalify and to Bid had been published on November 22, November 29 and December 5, 2002 under Executive Order No. 40.[^165] Dismissing the petition, the Supreme Court held that under Article 4 (and consistent with Section 77 of IRR-A), Republic Act No. 9184 contained no retroactivity provision and could not govern procurement already initiated under Executive Order No. 40, which allowed international lender guidelines to prevail.[^166] The case shows that the governing procurement law attaches when the bid invitation is published prior to the new statute's effectivity.[^167]

### [*Manapat v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44546): Small-Property-Owner Exemption

In [*Manapat v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44546), the National Housing Authority instituted expropriation suits in 1977 in the Regional Trial Court against small lot owners in Grace Park Subdivision under Presidential Decree No. 1072.[^168] On appeal in 1994 resolutions, the Court of Appeals exempted several lots of 300 square meters or less by applying Republic Act No. 7279 (the 1992 Urban Development and Housing Act).[^169] Reversing those resolutions on that issue, the Supreme Court held that Republic Act No. 7279 took effect only upon publication under Section 49 and expressed no legislative intent to retroact under Article 4 to expropriation suits filed in 1977.[^170] The case shows that subsequent statutory exemptions for small property owners do not retroactively defeat expropriation actions commenced long before their enactment.[^171]

### [*Lepanto Consolidated Mining Co. v. WMC Resources Int'l. Pty. Ltd.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42137): Mining Agreements and Presidential Approval

In [*Lepanto Consolidated Mining Co. v. WMC Resources Int'l. Pty. Ltd.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42137), WMC Resources executed a Financial and Technical Assistance Agreement on March 22, 1995 (prior to the April 14, 1995 effectivity of Republic Act No. 7942, the Philippine Mining Act of 1995), which required only the Environment Secretary's consent for transfers to third parties.[^172] Competing buyer Lepanto Consolidated Mining Co. challenged the Environment Secretary's 2001 approval of a transfer to Sagittarius Mines, Inc., contending before the Office of the President and the Court of Appeals that Section 40 of Republic Act No. 7942 required prior presidential approval.[^173] Affirming dismissal of the challenge, the Supreme Court held that Republic Act No. 7942 lacked express or implied retroactivity under Article 4 and that retroactively imposing presidential consent on a March 1995 agreement would unconstitutionally impair contract obligations.[^174] The case shows that new statutory restrictions on contract assignment cannot retroactively burden agreements perfected before the statute's effectivity.[^175]

### [*SAAD Agro-Industries, Inc. v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43670): Patented Land and Forest Reclassification

In [*SAAD Agro-Industries, Inc. v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43670), Socorro Orcullo obtained a free patent and Original Certificate of Title in 1971 over a 12.8477-hectare lot in Sibonga, Cebu later sold to SAAD Agro-Industries, Inc.[^176] In 1995, the Republic sued for reversion claiming the parcel was unclassified public forest under Section 13 of Presidential Decree No. 705 (promulgated May 19, 1975) and an uncertified 1980 land classification map photocopy. The Regional Trial Court dismissed the complaint before the Court of Appeals reversed.[^177] Reinstating the trial court's dismissal, the Supreme Court held that Presidential Decree No. 705 contained no retroactivity clause under Article 4 and could not retroactively classify land already validly segregated from the public domain by a 1971 patent and title.[^178] The case shows that later forestry decrees cannot unsettle prior valid Torrens titles issued under existing land laws.[^179]

### [*ACME Shoe, Rubber & Plastic Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1985/jan1985/gr_l-56718_1985.html): Insurance Renewals

In [*ACME Shoe, Rubber & Plastic Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1985/jan1985/gr_l-56718_1985.html), an insurer renewed a fire insurance policy for 1963 to 1964 on May 15, 1963 before Republic Act No. 3540 (implementing mandatory premium prepayment) took effect on October 1, 1963, then applied a January 8, 1964 premium payment to that 1963 to 1964 coverage while cancelling the 1964 to 1965 renewal upon lapse of a 90-day promissory note prior to an October 13, 1964 fire.[^180] The Court of First Instance allowed recovery on the policy, but the Court of Appeals reversed.[^181] Affirming the appellate court, the Supreme Court held under Article 4 that Republic Act No. 3540 did not retroactively invalidate the May 15, 1963 renewal, so applying the January 1964 payment to that policy year was proper and left the 1964 to 1965 policy cancelled at the time of the fire.[^182] The case shows that statutory conditions on contract validity govern policies issued after the statute takes effect.[^183]

### [*Philippine National Bank v. Tejano, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50526): Bank Privatization and Pending Appeal

In [*Philippine National Bank v. Tejano, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50526), Philippine National Bank dismissed branch vice-president Cayetano Tejano, Jr. in 1995. He perfected an appeal to the Civil Service Commission on September 21, 1995 before the bank's privatization on May 27, 1996 under Executive Order No. 80.[^184] The Civil Service Commission later dismissed his appeal in 1998 for supposed loss of jurisdiction upon privatization, which the Court of Appeals reversed and remanded.[^185] Affirming the remand, the Supreme Court En Banc held under Article 4 that Section 6 of Executive Order No. 80 contained no indication of retroactive transfer and therefore removed the bank from Civil Service Commission coverage prospectively only, preserving appellate jurisdiction already acquired in 1995.[^186] The case shows that a supervening privatization charter does not divest an administrative tribunal of pending disciplinary appeals absent express retroactivity.[^187]

### [*Callanta v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/aug1993/gr_105083_1993.html): Appeal Bonds in Labor Cases

In [*Callanta v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/aug1993/gr_105083_1993.html), a Labor Arbiter ordered petitioner's reinstatement on February 16, 1989. His employer appealed to the National Labor Relations Commission on March 20, 1989 (paying the appeal fee without posting a bond), one day before Republic Act No. 6715 took effect on March 21, 1989.[^188] The National Labor Relations Commission reversed on the merits and found voluntary resignation.[^189] Dismissing the worker's certiorari petition, the Supreme Court held under Article 4 that Republic Act No. 6715's new cash-bond and immediate-reinstatement rules had no retroactivity clause and did not invalidate an appeal already perfected on March 20, 1989 under prior rules.[^190] The case shows that new appeal prerequisites do not unsettle appeals perfected even one day before the amendatory law took effect.[^191]

### [*Inciong v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/may1990/gr_88943_1990.html): Immediate Execution of Labor Awards

In [*Inciong v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/may1990/gr_88943_1990.html), dismissed workers obtained a December 15, 1988 Labor Arbiter reinstatement decision which the employer appealed on January 6, 1989 without a supersedeas bond. The National Labor Relations Commission denied immediate execution pending appeal.[^192] Dismissing the workers' certiorari petition, the Supreme Court held under Article 4 that Republic Act No. 6715 (effective March 21, 1989) making reinstatement orders immediately executory contained no retroactivity provision and could not govern a 1988 decision already appealed before its effectivity.[^193] The case confirms that execution pending appeal introduced by Republic Act No. 6715 operates prospectively.[^194]

### [*Alvarez v. PICOP Resources, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51009): Forest Conversion and Local Consultation

In [*Alvarez v. PICOP Resources, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51009), PICOP Resources, Inc. filed a mandamus petition in 2002 in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City to compel conversion of its timber license (tracing back to a 1969 warranty document) into a 2002 to 2027 Integrated Forest Management Agreement without obtaining prior local legislative consultation under Sections 26 and 27 of the 1991 Local Government Code.[^195] Denying PICOP's motion for reconsideration of its prior adverse ruling, the Supreme Court En Banc rejected PICOP's Article 4 objection, noting that the company was applying for a new 25-year agreement for 2002 to 2027 subject to the 1987 Constitution and current statutory requirements.[^196] The case shows that applying current statutory prerequisites to a new prospective resource-utilization term is not retroactive application.[^197]

### [*Harrison Motors Corporation v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54382): Import Tax Verification Circulars

In [*Harrison Motors Corporation v. Navarro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54382), a buyer purchased two assembled trucks in June 1987 on the seller's express representation that all taxes and customs duties on imported parts had been paid, then paid P32,943.00 to free the vehicles when seized under 1987 and 1988 Bureau of Customs, Bureau of Internal Revenue and Land Transportation Office circulars and memoranda of agreement.[^198] Both the Regional Trial Court of Makati and the Court of Appeals ordered the seller to reimburse the buyer.[^199] Affirming reimbursement, the Supreme Court explained that while administrative regulations are generally prospective under Article 4, the issuances imposed no new tax but merely enforced pre-existing tax liabilities at importation and the seller's express warranty under Article 1546.[^200] The case shows that administrative enforcement procedures for pre-existing statutory obligations do not create prohibited retroactive liability.[^201]

### [*Balatbat v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jan1992/gr_36378_1992.html): Agrarian Ejectment Pending Before 1971

In [*Balatbat v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/jan1992/gr_36378_1992.html), a new landowner sued in the Court of Agrarian Relations on June 15, 1970 to eject an agricultural lessee for personal cultivation under Section 36(1) of Republic Act No. 3844. Both the agrarian court and the Court of Appeals ordered ejectment.[^202] Dismissing the tenant's petition for review, the Supreme Court applied [*Nilo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/apr1984/gr_l-34586_1984.html) and Article 4 to hold that Section 7 of Republic Act No. 6389 (effective September 10, 1971) lacked express or implied retroactivity despite Senate floor statements and did not abate pending personal-cultivation ejectment suits.[^203] The case shows that legislative floor debates alone cannot supply the retroactivity required to overcome Article 4.[^204]

### [*Nepomuceno v. Salazar*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/may1989/gr_37165_1989.html): Agrarian Ejectment Remanded for Trial

In [*Nepomuceno v. Salazar*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/may1989/gr_37165_1989.html), landowners filed agrarian complaints in July 1970 alleging both personal cultivation and non-agricultural conversion, after which the Court of Agrarian Relations partially dismissed the personal-cultivation ground on January 15, 1972 under Republic Act No. 6389 and the Court of Appeals dismissed the appeal as interlocutory.[^205] Setting aside both lower rulings and remanding, the Supreme Court held that the partial dismissal was appealable and substantively erroneous under Article 4 because Republic Act No. 6389's effectivity clause gave Section 7 no retroactive effect over 1970 complaints.[^206] The case shows that trial courts may not dismiss pending agrarian causes of action based on subsequent statutory repeals lacking retroactive effect.[^207]

### [*Gallardo v. Borromeo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/may1988/gr_l-36007_1988.html): Personal Cultivation and Leasehold

In [*Gallardo v. Borromeo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/may1988/gr_l-36007_1988.html), a retired letter carrier sued on December 7, 1963 to eject his tenant for personal cultivation, which the Court of Agrarian Relations dismissed in 1971 and the Court of Appeals affirmed in 1972 by applying Section 7 of Republic Act No. 6389 retroactively.[^208] Reversing the lower courts, the Supreme Court followed [*Nilo*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/apr1984/gr_l-34586_1984.html) and Article 4, permitting the 65-year-old retiree to terminate the tenancy under Section 36 of Republic Act No. 3844 in force when he sued in 1963.[^209] The case shows that appellate courts cannot invoke a post-suit statutory amendment to defeat a pending complaint filed under prior law.[^210]

### [*Buklod nang Magbubukid sa Lupaing Ramos, Inc. v. E.M. Ramos and Sons, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37024): Zoning Before Agrarian Decrees

In [*Buklod nang Magbubukid sa Lupaing Ramos, Inc. v. E.M. Ramos and Sons, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37024), E.M. Ramos and Sons, Inc. secured Municipal Ordinance No. 29-A on July 9, 1972 approving residential subdivision development of 372 hectares in Dasmariñas, Cavite, yet the Department of Agrarian Reform and the Office of the President later subjected the land to agrarian reform coverage before the Court of Appeals annulled those orders in 1997.[^211] Affirming the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court held under Article 4 that Letter of Instructions No. 729 (1978) and the 1981 Human Settlements Regulatory Commission Charter conferred no retroactive review authority over 1972 local zoning classifications, leaving the land validly reclassified before June 15, 1988.[^212] The case shows that subsequent national zoning review agencies cannot retroactively invalidate earlier lawful municipal reclassifications.[^213]

### [*Ong v. Imperial*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61215): Zoning Reclassification and Conversion

In [*Ong v. Imperial*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61215), landowners protested an August 14, 1994 agrarian coverage notice over a 40.5645-hectare parcel in Daet, Camarines Norte reclassified before June 15, 1988, winning exemption from the Office of the President in 2005 before the Court of Appeals reversed in 2010.[^214] Setting aside the Court of Appeals and reinstating the Office of the President's exemption order, the Supreme Court reiterated [*Buklod*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37024) and Article 4 to hold that 1978 and 1981 review directives could not operate retroactively over prior local reclassifications.[^215] The case confirms that valid pre-1988 non-agricultural reclassification permanently places land outside the Comprehensive Agrarian Reform Program.[^216]

### [*Coloso v. Garilao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42752): Pre-1972 Land Conversion

In [*Coloso v. Garilao*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42752), landowners obtained a final February 8, 1972 agrarian court judgment on a September 8, 1969 complaint authorizing subdivision conversion of 26.5 tenanted hectares, yet the Department of Agrarian Reform Secretary disregarded the judgment in 1996 citing Republic Act No. 6389 and Presidential Decree No. 27.[^217] Partly granting the landowners' Rule 65 petition, the Supreme Court held under Article 4 that neither Republic Act No. 6389 (1971) nor Presidential Decree No. 27 (1972) acted retroactively to divest the agrarian court's jurisdiction over the 1969 complaint as to the 26.5 hectares (while upholding agrarian coverage over the remaining 273.5 hectares left undeveloped for over 21 years).[^218] The case shows that final judgments under earlier statutes remain immune from subsequent non-retroactive agrarian decrees.[^219]

### [*Baltazar v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/may1981/gr_40191_1981.html): Tenant Redemption Judgment

In [*Baltazar v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/may1981/gr_40191_1981.html), a tenant consigned the purchase price in 1970 to exercise his statutory redemption right under Section 12 of Republic Act No. 3844 against a 1969 buyer seeking subdivision conversion and won an August 20, 1970 agrarian court judgment.[^220] In 1974, the Court of Appeals dismissed the buyer's ejectment suit yet denied redemption by applying Presidential Decrees Nos. 27 (1972) and 316 (1973) retroactively.[^221] Setting aside the appellate decision and affirming the 1970 agrarian judgment, the Supreme Court held under Article 4 that Presidential Decrees Nos. 27 and 316 operated prospectively only and could not extinguish a tenant's accrued redemption right from 1970.[^222] The case shows that even remedial social legislation does not retroactively erase accrued statutory rights of redemption.[^223]

### [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/jun1990/gr_86932_1990.html) (G.R. No. 86932, June 27, 1990): Foreclosed Mortgage and Worker Claims

In [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/jun1990/gr_86932_1990.html), Development Bank of the Philippines held a 1983 mortgage over Philippine Smelters Corporation assets and foreclosed in February and March 1987, after which workers filed labor money claims while involuntary insolvency proceedings were also pending. The Labor Arbiter and National Labor Relations Commission ordered the bank to pay the workers.[^224] Annulling the commission's decision, the Supreme Court followed [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/mar1990/gr_82763_1990.html) and Article 4 to hold that Republic Act No. 6715 amending Labor Code Article 110 applies prospectively only so as not to impair the bank's earlier 1983 mortgage credit, leaving workers to pursue claims in the pending insolvency proceeding.[^225] The case reinforces prospective protection of prior mortgage liens over subsequent labor-preference amendments.[^226]

### [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/jan1994/gr_86227_1994.html) (G.R. No. 86227, January 19, 1994): Pre-1989 Foreclosure and Worker Claims

In [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/jan1994/gr_86227_1994.html), Development Bank of the Philippines foreclosed Atlas Textile assets in March 1985. The Labor Arbiter and National Labor Relations Commission held the bank liable on October 1985 employee claims under Article 110 of the Labor Code.[^227] Reversing the labor tribunals on certiorari, the Supreme Court held that Republic Act No. 6715 (effective March 21, 1989) operates prospectively under Article 4 and cannot retroactively subordinate a pre-1989 mortgage foreclosure outside *in rem* insolvency distribution proceedings.[^228] The case underscores that expanded worker preference under Republic Act No. 6715 neither creates a lien nor retroacts against earlier mortgages.[^229]

### [*Hautea v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/feb1994/gr_96149_1994.html): Retirement Pay and Bank Foreclosure

In [*Hautea v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/feb1994/gr_96149_1994.html), Philippine National Bank foreclosed Calinog-Lambunao Sugar Mill assets on December 2, 1986 and transferred them to the Asset Privatization Trust, after which a retired employee won a default 1987 Labor Arbiter award and levied on the foreclosed properties before the National Labor Relations Commission annulled the execution in 1990.[^230] Dismissing the employee's certiorari petition, the Supreme Court En Banc applied [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. National Labor Relations Commission*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1990/mar1990/gr_82763_1990.html) and Article 4 to rule that Republic Act No. 6715 (effective March 21, 1989) cannot retroactively reach PNB's 1986 mortgage credit and foreclosure.[^231] The case confirms that prospective operation of Article 110 protects foreclosing mortgagees and their government transferees against pre-1989 labor execution levies.[^232]

### [*Dueñas v. Santos Subdivision Homeowners Association*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46915): Open Space and Approved Plans

In [*Dueñas v. Santos Subdivision Homeowners Association*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46915), a homeowners' association sought in 1997 to compel the daughter of the late developer of Santos Subdivision (whose plans were approved in 1966) to provide an open space under Section 31 of Presidential Decree No. 957 as amended by Presidential Decree No. 1216 (October 14, 1977). The Housing and Land Use Regulatory Board dismissed the petition before the Court of Appeals reversed citing *Eugenio*.[^233] Setting aside the appellate ruling and reinstating dismissal, the Supreme Court held that the association lacked juridical capacity and that Presidential Decrees Nos. 957 and 1216 lacked express retroactivity under Article 4, distinguishing *Eugenio* because neither non-development nor withheld amortizations were alleged.[^234] The case defines the narrow boundary of *Eugenio* and bars retroactive open-space imposition on pre-decree approved subdivisions.[^235]

### [*People's Industrial and Commercial Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35279): Contracts to Sell Cancelled in 1971

In [*People's Industrial and Commercial Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35279), a subdivision developer automatically cancelled six 1961 contracts to sell in October 1971 under contractual default clauses and sued in 1984 to recover possession. Both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals ruled for the developer.[^236] Affirming, the Supreme Court held that Presidential Decrees Nos. 957 (1976) and 1344 (1978) and Republic Act No. 6552 (August 26, 1972) contain no express retroactivity clause under Article 4 and could not govern contracts already automatically cancelled in October 1971.[^237] The case shows that buyer-protection housing decrees do not retroactively undo prior contractual cancellations perfected before their enactment.[^238]

### [*Puzon v. Abellera*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jan1989/gr_75082_1989.html): Townsite Titles and Realty Taxes

In [*Puzon v. Abellera*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/jan1989/gr_75082_1989.html), Baguio land held under a Republic Act No. 931 title was auctioned for 1971 to 1977 tax delinquencies on October 10, 1977 shortly before Presidential Decree No. 1271 took effect on December 22, 1977 validating qualifying pre-July 31, 1973 titles. Both the land registration court and the Intermediate Appellate Court annulled the auction and held no taxes were due.[^239] Modifying the lower rulings, the Supreme Court held under Article 4's necessary-implication exception that Presidential Decree No. 1271 retroactively validated title from original issue upon statutory compliance, rendering the owner liable for 1971 to 1977 taxes, even while voiding the October 1977 tax sale as premature and lacking notice.[^240] The case shows how a curative decree's recitals can imply retroactive validation with corresponding retroactive fiscal liability.[^241]

### [*Villaseñor v. Ombudsman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57009): Immediately Executory Ombudsman Orders

In [*Villaseñor v. Ombudsman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57009), two Quezon City electrical inspectors charged over the August 18, 2001 Manor Hotel fire challenged an August 23, 2006 Ombudsman order immediately implementing their 2003 dismissal and suspension penalties while a motion for reconsideration and an appeal remained pending, arguing that Administrative Order No. 17 (effective September 7, 2003) violated Article 4. The Court of Appeals dismissed their certiorari petition.[^242] Affirming, the Supreme Court held that rules regulating procedure and execution pending appeal are an exception to Article 4 and apply retroactively to pending administrative cases because no person holds a vested right in procedure or public office (while directing resolution of the unresolved motion).[^243] The case shows that procedural amendments governing immediate administrative execution apply immediately to pending cases.[^244]

### [*Rajah Humabon Hotel, Inc. v. Trajano*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_100222_1993.html): Money Claims and Jurisdiction

In [*Rajah Humabon Hotel, Inc. v. Trajano*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/sep1993/gr_100222_1993.html), 25 hotel employees filed wage and incentive-leave claims exceeding P5,000.00 each before the Department of Labor Regional Director on March 14, 1989 prior to Republic Act No. 6715. The Regional Director and Undersecretary assumed jurisdiction and granted the claims.[^245] Granting certiorari and setting aside the administrative orders, the Supreme Court held that although subsequent laws do not ordinarily govern pending suits under Article 4, a subsequent law vesting subject-matter jurisdiction in another forum operates retroactively like a curative law on pending cases, requiring referral to the Labor Arbiter.[^246] The case shows that statutory reallocations of adjudicatory jurisdiction apply immediately to unresolved actions.[^247]

### [*Philippine International Trading Corporation v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63590): Retirement Gratuity Ruling

In [*Philippine International Trading Corporation v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63590), the Philippine International Trading Corporation assailed a 2013 Commission on Audit decision upholding disallowances in its 2010 Annual Audit Report of post-reorganization retirement gratuities under Section 6 of Executive Order No. 756, arguing that a 2010 Supreme Court decision construing those gratuities as temporary should apply prospectively only.[^248] Dismissing certiorari, the Supreme Court En Banc held under Articles 4 and 8 that its 2010 decision overturned no prior Supreme Court ruling or competent administrative interpretation, so the construction retroacted to Executive Order No. 756's issuance and illegal payments conferred no vested right.[^249] The case shows that judicial interpretation of an executive issuance retroacts when it neither reverses prior precedent nor upsets valid official rulings.[^250]

### [*Republic v. Remman Enterprises, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56544): Land Classification Evidence

In [*Republic v. Remman Enterprises, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56544), Remman Enterprises applied in 2001 to confirm title over two Taguig lots using local Department of Environment and Natural Resources certifications. Both the Regional Trial Court (2007) and the Court of Appeals (2011) granted registration even after *Republic v. T.A.N. Properties, Inc.* (June 26, 2008) required a certified copy of the Secretary-approved classification.[^251] Reversing and denying registration, the Supreme Court rejected Remman's Article 4 objection and held that judicial interpretation forms part of the Property Registration Decree from passage (coupled with insufficient proof of possession since 1945).[^252] The case shows that doctrinal clarifications of existing statutory proof requirements govern pending appeals absent an overruling of prior precedent.[^253]

### [*Ramos v. National Commission on Indigenous Peoples*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66673): Injunction Power of the NCIP

In [*Ramos v. National Commission on Indigenous Peoples*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66673), successors of 1957 public-land awardees sought certiorari against a February 18, 2010 National Commission on Indigenous Peoples decision enjoining execution of an agrarian board judgment in favor of minor indigenous claimants where the opposing parties did not belong to the same indigenous community.[^254] Partly granting certiorari and dismissing the injunction complaint for lack of jurisdiction, the Supreme Court held under Articles 4 and 8 that *Unduran*'s reading of Section 66 of the Indigenous Peoples' Rights Act applied retroactively to the pending dispute because an earlier remark in *City Government of Baguio v. Masweng* was mere *obiter dictum*, not a reversed binding doctrine.[^255] The case shows that repudiating non-binding *obiter dictum* does not trigger prospective-only overruling.[^256]

### [*Chavez v. Public Estates Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47293): Reclaimed Land and Prospective Effect

In [*Chavez v. Public Estates Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47293), after the Supreme Court En Banc on July 9, 2002 nullified the Amended Joint Venture Agreement transferring reclaimed and submerged Manila Bay lands to Amari Coastal Bay Development Corporation, Amari sought reconsideration pleading prospective application under *De Agbayani*, *Benzonan* and Article 4.[^257] Denying reconsideration with finality, the Supreme Court En Banc held that prospective overruling and the operative fact doctrine did not apply because the 2002 ruling merely reiterated the constitutional ban on corporate acquisition of public alienable land existing since 1973 and the inalienability of submerged bay areas, while Amari did not contract in good faith.[^258] The case shows that prospective protection is unavailable where a decision applies settled constitutional prohibitions rather than reversing prior doctrine.[^259]

### [*Columbia Pictures, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34021): Search Warrant Standards

In [*Columbia Pictures, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34021), a Makati Regional Trial Court issued Search Warrant No. 87-053 on December 14, 1987 in an anti-piracy investigation upon examination of witnesses under existing *Burgos* probable-cause standards, then quashed the warrant in November 1988 by retroactively applying *20th Century Fox* (promulgated August 19, 1988) requiring presentation of master tapes, which the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^260] Reversing and reinstating the warrant, the Supreme Court En Banc held under Articles 4 and 8 that a new evidentiary doctrine cannot retroactively invalidate search warrants validly issued eight months earlier under existing jurisprudence.[^261] The case shows that new judicial standards for probable cause operate prospectively to preserve fair play.[^262]

### [*Santos v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51584): Custodial Waivers Before 1983

In [*Santos v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51584), Central Bank employees gave uncounseled extrajudicial confessions on February 13, February 17 and March 22, 1982 and were tried before the Sandiganbayan for estafa through falsification of public documents.[^263] Modifying the Sandiganbayan's judgment (acquitting two petitioners for reasonable doubt and adjusting two others' sentences), the Supreme Court En Banc held under Articles 4 and 8 that the April 26, 1983 *Morales-Galit* rule requiring counsel's presence during waiver and Section 12(1), Article III of the 1987 Constitution do not apply retroactively to voluntary 1982 confessions.[^264] The case shows that judge-made custodial safeguards apply prospectively from the promulgation date of the landmark decision announcing them.[^265]

### [*People v. Binamira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35103): Uncounseled Confession After 1983

In [*People v. Binamira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35103), accused Armando Binamira executed an uncounseled extrajudicial confession on October 3, 1985 without being effectively informed of or afforded counsel of his own choice. The Makati Regional Trial Court convicted him of robbery with homicide in 1989.[^266] Reversing and acquitting on reasonable doubt, the Supreme Court rejected the prosecution's reliance on prospectivity before the 1987 Constitution, holding under Articles 4 and 8 that *Morales* (April 26, 1983) and *Galit* (March 20, 1985) had already established the judge-made rule prospectively before Binamira's October 1985 interrogation.[^267] The case pairs with [*Santos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51584) and [*Filoteo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34163) to pinpoint April 26, 1983 as the exact prospective boundary for custodial counsel waivers.[^268]

### [*People v. Martinado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/oct1992/gr_92020_1992.html): Escape as Waiver of Appeal

In [*People v. Martinado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/oct1992/gr_92020_1992.html), Eliseo Martinado escaped jail on August 6, 1988 prior to the February 22, 1989 promulgation of his robbery with homicide conviction, filed an appeal through counsel in March 1989 and was recaptured on April 10, 1989.[^269] Before deciding his appeal, the Supreme Court issued *People v. Mapalao*, holding that an accused who fails to appear at promulgation without justifiable cause waives the right to appeal unless returning within 15 days.[^270] Citing Articles 4 and 8, the Supreme Court held that applying *Mapalao*'s new prejudicial rule retroactively would unfairly defeat Martinado's 1989 appeal, so it gave due course to his appeal and modified his conviction to homicide.[^271] The case shows that new procedural doctrines that extinguish remedies operate prospectively when prejudice to prior appellants would result.[^272]

### [*Chavez v. National Housing Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44182): Smokey Mountain Titles

In [*Chavez v. National Housing Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44182), petitioner sought prohibition and mandamus in 2004 to nullify the 1993 Smokey Mountain reclamation joint venture and reclaimed-land titles completed and sold to third-party corporations by August 1996, citing the July 9, 2002 [*Chavez v. Public Estates Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47293) decision.[^273] Denying prohibition (while granting mandamus for document access), the Supreme Court En Banc held under Articles 4 and 8 and *Benzonan* that [*Chavez v. Public Estates Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47293) could not apply retroactively to impair vested rights and Torrens titles already transferred under the Smokey Mountain project prior to 2002.[^274] The case shows how prospective judicial application and the operative fact doctrine protect vested property rights built under earlier law.[^275]

### [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. De Leon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48605): Mode of Appeal from Agrarian Courts

In [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. De Leon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48605), Land Bank appealed a 1997 Special Agrarian Court just-compensation ruling by ordinary appeal under Rule 41 rather than petition for review under Section 60 of Republic Act No. 6657. The Court of Appeals dismissed the appeal before the Supreme Court initially affirmed on September 10, 2002.[^276] On motion for reconsideration noting over 60 similar pending ordinary appeals filed amid conflicting Court of Appeals rulings, the Supreme Court En Banc partially granted reconsideration under Articles 4 and 8 and ruled that the petition-for-review requirement affects the substantive right to appeal and therefore applies prospectively only after finality of the Resolution.[^277] The case shows that novel judicial resolutions settling conflicting appellate modes operate prospectively to protect pending appeals filed in good faith.[^278]

### [*Global Medical Center of Laguna, Inc. v. Ross Systems International, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67423): Appeals from Arbitral Awards

In [*Global Medical Center of Laguna, Inc. v. Ross Systems International, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67423), parties to a hospital construction contract appealed a Construction Industry Arbitration Commission arbitral award to the Court of Appeals under Rule 43, after which both elevated the dispute under Rule 45.[^279] Modifying the appellate math error while upholding party autonomy, the Supreme Court En Banc abandoned prior cases allowing Rule 43 appeals from CIAC awards and directed future appeals on pure law directly to the Supreme Court under Rule 45 and integrity or constitutional challenges to the Court of Appeals under Rule 65, yet made that doctrinal reversal strictly prospective under Articles 4 and 8 so pending Rule 43 appeals remained intact.[^280] The case shows that sweeping jurisdictional-mode overrulings protect litigants with pending appeals filed under the abandoned rule.[^281]

### [*Dela Calzada v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69315): Audit Disallowance and Finality

In [*Dela Calzada v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69315), National Economic and Development Authority Caraga employees were absolved from refunding P882,759.07 in disallowed incentives by an unchallenged 2018 decision of the Commission on Audit Proper, only to have that liability unilaterally reinstated in a December 22, 2021 resolution when officers moved for partial reconsideration, based on 2019 and 2020 precedents (*Chozas* and *Madera*) narrowing good-faith immunity.[^282] Granting certiorari, the Supreme Court En Banc set aside the reinstatement under Articles 4 and 8, holding that supervening jurisprudence operates prospectively and cannot disturb a final exoneration or deny due process.[^283] The case shows that prospective overruling reinforces finality of judgment and procedural due process against retroactive reinstatement of liability.[^284]

### [*Office of the Ombudsman v. Vergara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63642): Condonation and Complaint Date

In [*Office of the Ombudsman v. Vergara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63642), a June 21, 2005 complaint charged Cabanatuan City Mayor Julius Cesar Vergara for dumpsite neglect. After a gap between terms he was re-elected in 2010, leading the Court of Appeals to hold the Ombudsman's penalty barred by condonation.[^285] Affirming, the Second Division applied [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377)'s prospective abandonment under Articles 4 and 8 and held that condonation still applied to cases instituted earlier, even across an election gap, so long as the same electorate re-elected the official.[^286] The case shows an intermediate step in applying prospective condonation, later refined by [*Madreo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66828) and [*Gaudan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67228) to focus strictly on the date of re-election.[^287]

### [*Dator v. Carpio-Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64667): Complaint Filed After Carpio Morales

In [*Dator v. Carpio-Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64667), a complaint filed on May 2, 2016 accused Lucban Mayor Celso Olivier Dator of misconduct for hiring his sister under a 2014 job order, after which the Ombudsman found simple misconduct and the Court of Appeals denied injunctive relief.[^288] Partly granting Dator's petition to reduce his suspension to one month and one day based on good-faith reliance on prior municipal practice, the First Division held under Articles 4 and 8 that he could not invoke condonation because his case was instituted on May 2, 2016 after [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377).[^289] The case shows the institution-date reasoning before the Supreme Court En Banc unified the test around re-election dates.[^290]

### [*Herrera v. Mago*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66094): Re-election After April 12, 2016

In [*Herrera v. Mago*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66094), a complaint filed on January 9, 2015 accused Vinzons Vice-Mayor Radames Herrera of unilaterally approving RATA differentials to former councilors, after which the Ombudsman ordered his dismissal and the Court of Appeals affirmed on October 24, 2016.[^291] Denying certiorari, the First Division cited *Crebello*'s April 12, 2016 finality date for [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377) under Articles 4 and 8. It denied condonation despite the 2015 complaint because Herrera was re-elected only on May 9, 2016.[^292] The case bridges [*Vergara*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63642) and [*Madreo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66828) by pinpointing April 12, 2016 as the cut-off date for re-election.[^293]

### [*Madreo v. Bayron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66828): Recall Re-election and the Cut-off

In [*Madreo v. Bayron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66828), an administrative complaint filed on November 22, 2013 charged Puerto Princesa Mayor Lucilo Bayron for July 1, 2013 acts. Bayron won a recall election on May 8, 2015 and was re-elected again in May 2016 before the Ombudsman dismissed him in November 2016 and the Court of Appeals reversed.[^294] Affirming the Court of Appeals, which had set aside the dismissal from service, the Supreme Court En Banc clarified under Articles 4 and 8 that re-election (including recall re-election) before April 12, 2016 vests the condonation defense regardless of when the complaint was pending, though Bayron's subsequent May 2016 re-election did not condone misconduct.[^295] The case settled that the date of re-election decides whether the condonation defense survives the April 12, 2016 cut-off.[^296]

### [*Gaudan v. Degamo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67228): Circular No. 17 Declared Void

In [*Gaudan v. Degamo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67228), Negros Oriental Governor Roel Degamo assumed office by succession after his 2010 board election and was re-elected Governor in May 2013 by the same electorate, facing a 2013 complaint decided by the Ombudsman in January and May 2016 before the Court of Appeals enjoined and reversed dismissal on condonation.[^297] Denying consolidated petitions, the Supreme Court En Banc applied [*Madreo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66828) under Articles 4 and 8, affirmed condonation based on Degamo's May 2013 re-election and formally declared Ombudsman Office Circular No. 17, s. 2016 null and void for trying to withhold condonation in cases still pending on April 12, 2016.[^298] The case shows that an administrative circular cannot narrow the prospective cut-off fixed by the Supreme Court En Banc.[^299]

## Suggested Answers

### 1. Prospective Administrative Regulations and Pre-Existing Expropriation Suits

**Question:** In 2020, a government agency filed an expropriation complaint and deposited provisional compensation equal to the property's tax assessment under Rule 67. In 2022, Congress enacted a statute effective 15 days after publication requiring government infrastructure projects to deposit 100 percent of zonal value upfront, without any retroactivity clause. The landowners move to compel the agency to pay the higher deposit under the 2022 law and invoke equity. Should the trial court grant the motion?

**Answer:** No, the trial court should deny the motion. Under Article 4 of the Civil Code, laws shall have no retroactive effect unless the contrary is provided. Every doubt is resolved in favor of prospective operation. The Supreme Court En Banc held in [*Curata v. Philippine Ports Authority*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49359) that statutory rules governing provisional deposits and standards of just compensation create substantive proprietary rights rather than mere procedural remedies, so an amendatory expropriation statute lacking an express retroactivity clause cannot apply retroactively to pending expropriation actions. Moreover, as ruled in [*4E Steel Builders Corporation v. Maybank Philippines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69009), equity applies only in the absence of statutory law and cannot supply retroactivity against Article 4. As held in [*Philippine Deposit Insurance Corporation v. Stockholders of Intercity Savings and Loan Bank, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51088), a 15-day post-publication effectivity clause reinforces legislative intent for strictly prospective application.

### 2. Reversal of Supreme Court Doctrine and Official Administrative Reliance

**Question:** In 2019, a public officer committed an administrative infraction during his 2016 to 2019 term, was re-elected to the same post in May 2022 and had an Ombudsman complaint filed against him in 2023. Relying on [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377), he invokes good faith and argues that a 2020 Supreme Court decision clarifying a statutory penalty should not apply to him. Meanwhile, in a separate criminal case, an accused issued an instrument in 2018 relying on an official Ministry of Justice circular stating such act was non-criminal before a 2021 Supreme Court ruling held the act punishable. Resolve both defenses.

**Answer:** The public officer's condonation defense fails, whereas the accused's reliance defense succeeds. First, the Supreme Court En Banc abandoned the condonation doctrine prospectively in [*Carpio Morales v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377). In [*Madreo v. Bayron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66828) and [*Gaudan v. Degamo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67228), the Supreme Court En Banc noted that [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377) attained finality on April 12, 2016 and clarified that the reckoning trigger is the date of re-election. Because the officer's re-election occurred in May 2022 after April 12, 2016, condonation no longer applies. Furthermore, unless the 2020 decision overruled an earlier binding precedent on the statutory penalty, judicial interpretation forms part of the statute from enactment under Article 8, as held in [*San Miguel Corporation v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68899) and [*Philippine International Trading Corporation v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63590). Second, in the criminal prosecution, Articles 4 and 8 require prospective application of a judicial doctrine that overturns an official executive interpretation on which a citizen reasonably relied prior to the reversal, just as the Supreme Court En Banc dismissed the criminal charge in [*Co v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/oct1993/gr_100776_1993.html).

### 3. Curative and Favorable Penal Exceptions vs. Contractual Cancellations

**Question:** During the pendency of an application for land title confirmation filed in 2019, Congress passes a statute in 2021 shortening the required period of possession of alienable public land to 20 years and simplifying proof of classification, without an express retroactivity clause. In the same year, Congress also reduces the penalty for an offense pending trial against an accused who is not a habitual criminal. Compare how Article 4 applies to both statutes. Contrast a subdivision developer who automatically cancelled defaulted installment contracts to sell in 1971 prior to buyer-protection housing decrees enacted later without express retroactivity.

**Answer:** Both the 2021 land-confirmation statute and the 2021 penal reduction apply retroactively to the pending proceedings. The later buyer-protection decrees do not apply retroactively to revive already cancelled contracts. Under [*Republic v. Pasig Rizal Co., Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68138), statutes that are curative and create new rights without impairing vested rights (such as shortening public-land possession requirements to 20 years under Republic Act No. 11573) apply retroactively as an exception to Article 4 to applications still pending at the trial or appellate stage. Similarly, as illustrated in [*Filoteo, Jr. v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34163) (discussing Article 22 of the Revised Penal Code) and applied to favorable statutory amendments in [*Agote v. Lorenzo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48728), a favorable penal statute operates retroactively to benefit an accused despite Article 4. By contrast, as the Supreme Court held in [*People's Industrial and Commercial Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35279), housing statutes lacking an express retroactivity clause under Article 4 cannot retroactively unsettle installment contracts that had already been validly cancelled under default clauses before the decrees took effect.

# Quotes

> "Retroactivity of laws is a matter of civil law, not of a constitutional law, as its governing law is the Civil Code, not the Constitution. Article 4 of the Civil Code provides that laws shall have no retroactive effect unless the contrary is provided. The application of the Civil Code is of course self-explanatory - laws enacted by Congress may permissibly provide that they shall have retroactive effect. The Civil Code established a statutory norm, not a constitutional standard."[^300]

> "Statutes are prospective and not retroactive in their operation, they being the formulation of rules for the future, not the past. Hence, the legal maxim lex de futuro, judex de praeterito — the law provides for the future, the judge for the past, which is articulated in Article 4 of the Civil Code: "Laws shall have no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided." The reason for the rule is the tendency of retroactive legislation to be unjust and oppressive on account of its liability to unsettle vested rights or disturb the legal effect of prior transactions."[^301]

> "Statutes are prospective and not retroactive in their operation, laws being the formulation of rules for the future, not the past. Hence, the legal maxim lex de futuro, judex de praeterito—the law provides for the future, the judge for the past—which is articulated in Art. 4 of the Civil Code thusly: "Laws shall have no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided." The legislative intent as to the retroactive application of a law is made manifest either by the express terms of the statute or by necessary implication. The reason for the rule is the tendency of retroactive legislation to be unjust and oppressive on account of its liability to unsettle vested rights or disturb the legal effect of prior transactions."[^302]

> "It is a rule of statutory construction that all statutes are to be construed as having only a prospective operation unless the purpose and intention of the Legislature to give them a retrospective effect is expressly declared or is necessarily implied from the language used. In every case of doubt, the doubt must be solved against the retrospective effect. The cases supporting this rule are almost without number."[^303]

> "It is a well-entrenched rule that doctrines of equity apply only in the absence of a statutory law. "Equity, which has been aptly described as 'justice outside legality,' should be applied only in the absence of, and never against, statutory law." In this regard, Article 4 of the Civil Code expressly states that laws shall have no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided. Notably, R.A. No. 10641 does not contain a retroactivity clause. Hence, it can only be applied prospectively."[^304]

> "Section 24 of Revised Rule 140 and its explanatory note are clear that all of the provisions of the Revised Rule 140 shall apply retroactively even if their application would be prejudicial to the employee. Thus, the Revised Rule 140 shall be "uniformly applicable to all cases, regardless of when the infractions are committed." This rule is only subject to the qualification that the retroactive effect of Revised Rule 140 shall be "without prejudice to the internal rules of the Committee on Ethics and Ethical Standards of the Supreme Court insofar as complaints against Members of the Supreme Court are concerned.""[^305]

> "To be sure, the curative nature of RA 11573 can easily be discerned from its declared purpose, that is, "to simplify, update and harmonize similar and related provisions of land laws in order to simplify and remove ambiguity in its interpretation and implementation." Moreover, by shortening the period of adverse possession required for confirmation of title to twenty (20) years prior to filing (as opposed to possession since June 12, 1945 or earlier), the amendment implemented through Section 6 of RA 11573 effectively created a new right in favor of those who have been in possession of alienable and disposable land for the shortened period provided. The retroactive application of this shortened period does not impair vested rights, as RA 11573 simply operates to confirm the title of applicants whose ownership already existed prior to its enactment."[^306]

> "Article 4 of the Civil Code does indeed provide that laws shall have no retroactive effect. Rules regulating the procedure of courts, however, are retroactive in nature, and are, thus, applicable to actions pending and unresolved at the time of their passage. As a general rule, no vested right may attach to or arise from procedural laws and rules, hence, retroactive application does not violate any right of a person adversely affected."[^307]

> "Guided by the foregoing, the Court cannot but set aside petitioner's conviction in Criminal Case No. 96-149820 for illegal possession of firearm since another crime was committed at the same time, i.e., violation of COMELEC Resolution No. 2826 or the Gun Ban."[^308]

> "Petitioner’s contention that Article III, Section 12 of the 1987 Constitution should be given retroactive effect for being favorable to him as an accused, cannot be sustained. While Article 22 of the Revised Penal Code provides that "(p)enal laws shall have a retroactive effect insofar as they favor the person guilty of a felony who is not a habitual criminal," what is being construed here is a constitutional provision specifically contained in the Bill of Rights which is obviously not a penal statute. A bill of rights is a declaration and enumeration of the individual rights and privileges which the Constitution is designed to protect against violations by the government, or by individuals or groups of individual. It is a charter of liberties for the individual and a limitation upon the power of the state. Penal laws, on the other hand, strictly and properly are those imposing punishment for an offense committed against the state which the executive of the state has the power to pardon. In other words, a penal law denotes punishment imposed and enforced by the state for a crime or offense against its law."[^309]

> "6. Even if Article 110 and its Implementing Rule, as amended, should be interpreted to mean "absolute preference," the same should be given only prospective effect in line with the cardinal rule that laws shall have no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided (Article 4, Civil Code). Thereby, any infringement on the constitutional guarantee on non-impairment of the obligation of contracts (Section 10, Article III, 1987 Constitution) is also avoided. In point of fact, DBP's mortgage credit antedated by several years the amendatory law, RA No. 6715. To give Article 110 retroactive effect would be to wipe out the mortgage in DBP's favor and expose it to a risk which it sought to protect itself against by requiring a collateral in the form of real property."[^310]

> "It is consequently clear that a judicial interpretation becomes a part of the law as of the date that law was originally passed, subject only to the qualification that when a doctrine of this Court is overruled and a different view is adopted, and more so when there is a reversal thereof, the new doctrine should be applied prospectively and should not apply to parties who relied on the old doctrine and acted in good faith. To hold otherwise would be to deprive the law of its quality of fairness and justice then, if there is no recognition of what had transpired prior to such adjudication."[^311]

> "For appeals from CIAC arbitral awards that have already been filed and are currently pending before the CA under Rule 43, the prior availability of the appeal on matters of fact and law thereon applies. This is only proper since the parties resorted to this mode of review as it was the existing procedural rules at the time of filing, prior to the instant amendment."[^312]

> "Based on these considerations, the Court deems it proper to declare the Ombudsman's Office Circular No. 17 dated May 11, 2016 null and void, pursuant to the above-discussed ruling in Madreo. As it stands, the condonation doctrine is still considered as "good law" in all administrative cases involving public officials whose reelections occurred before April 12, 2016, regardless of the dates of filing of the administrative cases against them or the status of said cases when the Carpio Morales ruling attained finality."[^313]

> "Thus, it was arbitrary on the part of the COA Proper to reinstate petitioners' liability to apply the new precedent—good faith is no longer recognized as a justification to excuse recipients from liability—when, at the time the previous ruling was issued and became final, prevailing jurisprudence says otherwise. To stress, Chozas v. Commission on Audit and all the other cases cited in that case, which was the basis of the COA Proper in reinstating petitioners' liability was promulgated in 2019 or after the COA Proper's previous ruling in 2018 had become final with respect to petitioners' liability. Too, it may not come amiss to note that it was only in Madera, promulgated in 2020, when the Court En Banc clarified the inapplicability of the good faith rule in excusing a recipient from liability in a disallowance. Verily, we cannot countenance a deviation from the time-honored doctrine of immutability of judgments only to violate the equally-recognized principle of prospective overruling."[^314]

# Tips

- Verify the **governing statute and effectivity clause first**: if an act, tax payment, contract or appeal perfection occurred before a statute's approval or post-publication effectivity date, the older rule governs unless an Article 4 exception applies.
- Resolve **any statutory ambiguity against retroactivity**: under [*Nilo v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/apr1984/gr_l-34586_1984.html), every case of doubt favors prospective operation. Floor statements by individual legislators cannot substitute for express or necessarily implied statutory intent.
- Classify the **nature of the amendment carefully**: substantive standards affecting proprietary deposits, exemptions or appeal rights apply prospectively, whereas purely procedural or curative enactments creating new rights without impairing vested rights generally govern pending actions.
- Check whether **Article 4 exceptions** apply cleanly. Express retroactivity clauses (even if prejudicial in judicial discipline), clear legislative social-justice intent and favorable penal laws for non-habitual offenders overcome prospectivity, whereas new civil sanctions do not reach pre-effectivity acts under [*Strebel v. Figueras*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1954/dec1954/gr_l-4722_1954.html).
- Separate **constitutional limits from statutory rules**: Article 4 itself is only a civil statutory norm, so Congress may give a law retroactive effect unless doing so divests vested rights or unconstitutionally impairs existing contracts (including pre-existing recorded mortgages).
- Distinguish **first-time judicial interpretation** from **doctrinal overruling**: under Article 8, judicial construction retroacts to the law's passage if it overturns no binding precedent (and *obiter dicta* or Minute Resolutions do not count as settled precedent), whereas overruling a settled doctrine or Justice Secretary circular applies prospectively.
- Use **exact doctrinal cut-off dates**: under [*Carpio Morales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61377) as applied in [*Madreo v. Bayron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66828), check whether the public official's **re-election** (not merely complaint filing) occurred before April 12, 2016. For custodial counsel waivers addressed in [*Santos v. Sandiganbayan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51584) and [*People v. Binamira*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35103), check whether the confession followed April 26, 1983.
