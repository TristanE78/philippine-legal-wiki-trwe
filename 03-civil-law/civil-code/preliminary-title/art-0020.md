> ART. 20. Every person who, contrary to law, willfully or negligently causes damage to another, shall indemnify the latter for the same.[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 20 of the Civil Code establishes the general civil sanction for all provisions of law that do not provide their own specific civil liability. Whenever a person violates an existing statute, administrative regulation or legal duty, whether willfully or negligently and thereby causes damage to another, Article 20 obliges the wrongdoer to indemnify the injured party.

- **General sanction for violations of law.** Article 20 was incorporated by the Code Commission to afford relief to anyone who suffers damage because another person has violated a legal provision that does not specify its own civil indemnity.[^2] [^3]
- **Applies to both willful and negligent unlawful acts.** Unlike Articles 19 and 21, which require an intentional act or bad faith, the text of Article 20 does not distinguish between willful and negligent conduct so long as the act is contrary to law.[^4] [^5]
- **Requires an existing legal duty and breach.** Liability under Article 20 attaches only when an existing statute, regulation or legal norm imposes a duty on the actor and the actor's breach of that duty causes injury.[^6] [^7]

This commentary examines the statutory function of Article 20 as a general sanction for violations of law, its boundary with Articles 19, 21 and 2176, its operation in statutory and regulatory breaches, its role in contract pre-termination, self-help and malicious prosecution, the defense of good faith and *damnum absque injuria* and the rules on individualized responsibility and forum jurisdiction.

## Scope, Requisites and General Sanction for Violations of Law

Article 20 furnishes the general civil sanction for violations of statutes and legal rules that do not declare their own civil remedy.[^8] [^9] In [*Garcia, Jr. v. Salvador*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41343), the Supreme Court explained that the Code Commission incorporated Article 20 to provide relief to a person who suffers damage whenever another commits an act in violation of some legal provision.[^10] In [*Banal v. Tadeo, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/dec1987/gr_l-78911_1987.html), the Supreme Court applied Article 20 to hold that an offender who issues a worthless check in violation of Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 incurs civil liability to indemnify the payee for the damage caused, even though Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 is a special penal statute that does not expressly provide for civil indemnity.[^11] Because damage or injury to another is the foundation of the civil action under Article 20, the offended payee is entitled to indemnity and may intervene through a private prosecutor in the civil aspect of the criminal case.[^12]

To sustain a claim for damages under Article 20 as a standalone sanction for a statutory breach, the claimant must establish that the defendant violated an existing provision of law or regulation through fault or negligence and that the violation caused injury to the claimant.[^13] [^14] Where a complaint alleges ultimate facts showing that the defendant violated the plaintiff's statutory right to exclude others from her personal property under Article 429 of the Civil Code, as in [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367), the complaint states a cause of action for damages under Article 20 even if the pleading does not cite Article 20 or Article 429 by number.[^15]

## Boundary With Articles 19, 21 and 2176

Articles 19, 20 and 21 form an interlocking framework under the Civil Code chapter on human relations, but each provision covers a distinct sphere of wrongful conduct.[^16] [^17] Article 19 lays down a rule of conduct requiring every person to act with justice, give everyone his due and observe honesty and good faith, without specifying a direct remedy for its breach.[^18] [^19] Articles 20 and 21 supply the remedies when that standard is transgressed.[^20] [^21]

In [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367), the Supreme Court drew a structural boundary between Articles 19 and 20: Article 19 governs damage caused when no positive law is violated because the act is done within the scope of the defendant's rights or duties, whereas Article 20 furnishes the general sanction when the act itself violates a provision of law.[^22] When a plaintiff invokes Article 20 as the sanction for an abuse of rights under Article 19, the plaintiff must establish the elements of abuse of rights, particularly bad faith.[^23] [^24]

In [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html), the Supreme Court noted that Articles 19 and 21 share the element of intent while Article 20 does not distinguish between willful and negligent acts.[^25] In [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826) (quoting *Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*), the Supreme Court distinguished Article 20 from Article 21 by the illegality of the act and the mental state required.[^26] Article 20 concerns violations of existing law and allows recovery whether the unlawful act was committed willfully or negligently.[^27] [^28] By contrast, Article 21 concerns injuries caused by acts that are not proscribed by positive law but are contrary to morals, good customs or public policy and it applies only when the act is willful.[^29] [^30] In [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826) (quoting *Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*), the Supreme Court noted that "willful" refers to the intention to do the act and the desire to achieve the injurious outcome, while "negligence" refers to a situation where the act was consciously done without intending the injurious result.[^31]

Article 20 also operates alongside the general law on negligence under Articles 1170, 1173 and 2176.[^32] [^33] In [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Co.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921), the Supreme Court stated that the Civil Code makes liability for negligence clear under Article 2176 and Article 20 in relation to the definition of negligence in Article 1173.[^34] However, in [*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519), the Supreme Court pointed out that while Article 2180 establishes vicarious liability for quasi-delicts committed under Article 2176, there is no corresponding provision imposing vicarious liability on a labor union for acts governed by Articles 19 and 20.[^35] Similarly, in [*Caltex (Philippines), Inc. v. Sulpicio Lines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38239), the Supreme Court rejected the reliance of the Court of Appeals on Articles 20 and 2176 against a voyage charterer on whom the law imposed no duty to inspect the carrier's vessel.[^36]

## Statutory and Regulatory Violations: Health, Banking, Agency, Education and Corporate Governance

Because Article 20 penalizes acts done "contrary to law", the Supreme Court has applied it across diverse regulatory regimes whenever a breach of statutory or administrative duties inflicts harm.

- **Public health and clinical laboratory regulations.** In [*Garcia, Jr. v. Salvador*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41343), a clinical laboratory owner who was a medical technologist ran a Hepatitis B test on a trainee without a pathologist's supervision and released a false reactive result under the rubber-stamp signature of a pathologist who had not authorized the release.[^37] The Supreme Court held that violating the statutory and regulatory safeguards under Republic Act No. 4688, Section 29(b) of Republic Act No. 5527 and Department of Health Administrative Order No. 49-B (1988) constituted gross negligence and a breach of duty actionable under Article 20, justifying moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees after the trainee lost her job.[^38]
- **Banking diligence and premature loan certifications.** In [*Comsavings Bank v. Spouses Capistrano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56165), an originating bank had a housing borrower sign a Certificate of House Acceptance/Completion before construction of the house had started.[^39] The Supreme Court held the bank jointly and severally liable with the builder under Articles 20 and 1170 because a bank must exercise the highest degree of diligence. It added that Article 2219 allows moral damages for acts referred to in Article 20.[^40]
- **Agency limits in bank-brokered mortgage redemption insurance.** In [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_109937_1994.html), a mortgagee bank acting as an insurance agent required a 76-year-old borrower to apply for Mortgage Redemption Insurance and deducted the premium from his loan proceeds despite knowing that the policy had a 60-year maximum acceptance age.[^41] The Supreme Court held that when an agent exceeds the limits of its authority without giving the third party sufficient notice under Article 1897 (cited in the decision text as Article 1987), the resulting deception brings Articles 19, 20 and 21 into play and renders the bank liable to refund the premium and pay moral damages and attorney's fees, although it did not extinguish the principal loan because no insurance contract was perfected and the claim that the borrower would have obtained other insurance was speculative.[^42] The Supreme Court followed that reasoning in [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826). It held a lender bank liable as an agent that acted beyond its authority. The bank had deducted a premium from a business loan without disclosing that the insurance covered only consumer loans. The Supreme Court upheld the moral damages, attorney's fees and premium reimbursement.[^43]
- **Educational institutions and graduation notices.** In [*University of the East v. Jader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59575), a university listed a fourth-year law student as a tentative candidate for graduation and allowed him to attend the investiture ceremonies without promptly informing him that he had received a failing grade in a removal examination, preventing him from taking the bar examinations.[^44] The Supreme Court held that conscious indifference to the rights of others, even without intent to injure, amounts to negligence that renders the school liable for actual damages and attorney's fees under Articles 19 and 20, while deleting moral damages because the senior law student was also negligent in failing to verify his own removal grade.[^45]
- **Corporate by-laws and delinquency notices.** In [*Calatagan Golf Club, Inc. v. Clemente, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49070), a golf club mailed its third and final delinquency demand to a member's post office box despite knowing from two prior returned notices that the post office box had already been closed and then auctioned the member's share without contacting him at his residential address or telephone numbers on file.[^46] The Supreme Court held that the club's bad-faith breach of the notice mandate in its own By-Laws brought Articles 19, 20 and 21 into operation and justified restoring the share and awarding moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees.[^47]

## Contract Pre-Termination, Self-Help, Utility Disconnection and Malicious Prosecution

Article 20 operates in tandem with Article 19 when a party invokes a void contractual power, takes the law into his own hands, exercises a property or administrative prerogative oppressively, or initiates groundless criminal proceedings in bad faith.

- **Pre-termination under a void unilateral clause.** In [*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343), a basketball team owner pre-terminated a head coach's two-year employment contract by invoking a stipulation that permitted the owner to terminate the contract at any time if, in its sole opinion, the coach failed to exhibit sufficient skill.[^48] The Supreme Court declared the stipulation void for violating the mutuality of contracts under Article 1308 and held that because the pre-termination rested on an illegal ground contrary to law and the owner negligently failed to provide a valid legal basis, the owner abused its right and was liable under Article 19 in relation to Article 20 for actual damages representing the unpaid stipulated salary and for attorney's fees.[^49]
- **Post-expiration commercial bad faith.** Even when a contract expires by its own terms, pre-termination and post-termination conduct surrounding the non-renewal can still give rise to a tort cause of action. In [*Tocoms Philippines, Inc. v. Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66168), the Supreme Court held that an outgoing distributor's complaint stated a cause of action for damages under Articles 19, 20 and 21 and survived a motion to dismiss. The distributor alleged that the manufacturer gave no sufficient notice of non-renewal and demanded a buy-back of its stock at 40 and 60 percent below price. It also alleged that the manufacturer sold products to another company at lower prices.[^50]
- **Fraudulent self-help and unauthorized account debits.** In [*Sandejas v. Spouses Ignacio, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46434), two sisters who believed their brother had cheated one of them of three million pesos took a signed blank check that the brother had left for a condominium lease renewal, filled it out for three million pesos and encashed it through an impostor at a bank that waived identification and endorsement requirements.[^51] The Supreme Court held the sisters and the bank solidarily liable for restitution, moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Articles 20, 21, 2208, 2219(10) and 2229 because fraudulently encashing the check violated the law as well as the public policy against putting the law into one's own hands, while the bank's gross negligence amounted to a willful injury.[^52] Similarly, in [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367), a former employer's unilateral instruction to a bank to debit a resigned employee's personal checking account without her consent was held actionable as an invasion of property rights under Article 20 in relation to Article 429.[^53]
- **Abusive utility disconnection, broken family commitments and official retaliation.** In [*Ardiente v. Spouses Pastorfide*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55955), a home seller who had left the water connection in her name after the sale asked the water district to cut off the buyers' water supply without prior notice. The supply stayed cut for more than nine months. The Supreme Court held her liable for moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Article 19 with Articles 20 and 21 and under Article 2219.[^54] In [*Navarro-Banaria v. Banaria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66456), a second wife and guardian who confirmed her infirm 90-year-old husband's attendance at a birthday celebration organized by his family and then took him out of town without informing them was held liable for actual, moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees. The Supreme Court found a violation of Article 19 and awarded the damages under Article 21.[^55] In [*Villanueva v. Rosqueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53481), a Customs Commissioner who let a replacement exercise a Deputy Commissioner's powers while a preliminary injunction was in force, withheld her salary and left her out of the centennial memorabilia was held liable for moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Articles 19 and 20.[^56]
- **Malicious prosecution.** In [*Marsman & Co. v. Ligo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61047) and [*Sosmeña v. Bonafe*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66223), the Supreme Court identified Articles 19, 20, 21, 26, 29, 32, 33, 35, 2217 and 2219(8) of the Civil Code as the statutory basis for a civil action for damages for malicious prosecution.[^57] [^58] Recovery requires proof that the defendant instigated the proceeding, that it ended in acquittal or dismissal, that the defendant acted without probable cause and that the defendant was impelled by legal malice or a sinister design to vex and humiliate.[^59] [^60] In [*Marsman & Co. v. Ligo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61047), the Supreme Court found no probable cause where the employer had a supervisor arrested at the National Bureau of Investigation on an unverified telephone tip and presented him at a press conference. It found legal malice in the hostility of the warehouse manager toward the union.[^61] In [*Sosmeña v. Bonafe*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66223), the Supreme Court held that malicious prosecution encompasses not only criminal trials ending in acquittal but also preliminary investigations ending in dismissal where an employer deliberately initiated fabricated malicious mischief and theft complaints out of ill will.[^62]

## Presumption of Good Faith, *Damnum Absque Injuria* and Failure of Proof

Liability under Article 20, whether invoked alone or in relation to Article 19, fails when the defendant acted without breach of a legal duty or in the honest exercise of a right in good faith, resulting in *damnum absque injuria* (damage without legal injury).

- **Good-faith resort to judicial processes.** Because the law presumes good faith and does not penalize the right to litigate, filing a civil or criminal action with probable cause and without malice does not trigger liability under Articles 19, 20 or 21.[^63] [^64] In [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html), the Supreme Court absolved an unpaid supplier that filed a Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 complaint against the president of the buyer company. His namesake son ran the drawer company at the same address. The supplier honestly believed that the father issued the check. The father did nothing to clear up the mistaken identity. The Supreme Court ruled that an honest mistake of identity without malice produces *damnum absque injuria*.[^65] Likewise, in [*Sandejas v. Spouses Ignacio, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46434), the Supreme Court denied damages to two claimants who were impleaded in good faith, holding that the anxiety usually caused to a party haled into court is *damnum absque injuria* in the absence of malice.[^66]
- **Contracting a second marriage under an honest belief in a foreign divorce.** In [*Mercado v. Ongpin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67004), the Supreme Court held that the mere act of contracting a second marriage while a first marriage subsists is not, by itself, a ground for moral damages under Article 19 in relation to Article 20 or Article 21.[^67] Where the second spouse did not prove bad faith because the husband believed in good faith that his first wife had already become an American citizen when she obtained a Nevada divorce, the awards of moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees were deleted.[^68]
- **Extrajudicial repossession under an express lease clause.** In [*CJH Development Corp. v. Aniceto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66462), the Supreme Court held that a lessor who demolished a restaurant structure and stored the lessee's personal properties at its warehouse after the lease expired, after the trial court denied a preliminary injunction and after multiple notices to vacate, acted in good faith pursuant to express lease stipulations transferring ownership of permanent improvements and authorizing entry and inventory, so that any damage sustained by the lessee was *damnum absque injuria* and not compensable under Articles 19, 20 and 21. It deleted the award of P2,183,625.00 for the lessee's personal properties and ordered any remaining items turned over to the lessee.[^69]
- **Administrative discretion of public officials.** In [*Diaz v. Encanto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61630), the Supreme Court held that bad faith is the core of a claim under Article 19 and that public officers are not liable for moral and exemplary damages for acts within their discretion without proof of bad faith.[^70] Because university officials acted within their authority in recommending that a professor's sabbatical leave be without pay and withholding her salaries when she failed to report for work, they were absolved of moral and exemplary damages. The Supreme Court ordered the university to pay the salaries withheld from July to October 1988 and the later withheld salaries once she submitted the required documents, with 6% interest.[^71]
- **Absence of statutory duty on a voyage charterer or pure sponsor.** In [*Caltex (Philippines), Inc. v. Sulpicio Lines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38239), the Supreme Court absolved a petroleum shipper that voyage-chartered a motor tanker from liability under Articles 20 and 2176 for a maritime collision because the duty to ensure seaworthiness and valid licenses rests on the common carrier and not on a voyage charterer.[^72] Similarly, in [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Co.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921), the Supreme Court held the race organizer liable for gross negligence under Articles 20, 1173 and 2176 for staging a junior marathon on an unblocked highway, but absolved the corporate sponsor whose role was limited to paying a sponsorship fee without control over the race route or safety measures.[^73]

## Labor Strikes, Individualized Responsibility and Forum Jurisdiction

The interaction between Article 20 of the Civil Code and the Labor Code governs both who may be held civilly liable for unlawful strike activities and which tribunal has jurisdiction over damage claims between employers and workers.

- **Individualized liability for illegal acts during a strike.** In [*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519), the Supreme Court ruled that while Article 279(a), paragraph 3 of the Labor Code prescribes termination from employment as the labor penalty for illegal acts during a strike, it does not bar an employer from recovering civil damages under Articles 19 and 20 of the Civil Code for extraordinary losses caused by unlawful strike acts committed in bad faith.[^74] However, because neither the Labor Code nor Articles 19 and 20 adopt vicarious or collective union liability, civil liability rests exclusively on the individual union officers or members whose direct participation and bad faith are proven by substantial evidence and union funds cannot be held liable.[^75] The Supreme Court also held that the Labor Arbiter has jurisdiction over an employer's counterclaim for strike damages.[^76]
- **Jurisdiction of regular civil courts versus labor tribunals.** Whether a complaint invoking Articles 19, 20 and 21 belongs in the regular civil courts or before the Labor Arbiter under Article 224 (formerly Article 217) of the Labor Code depends on whether the claim has a reasonable causal connection with the employer-employee relationship.[^77] In [*Philippine Commercial International Bank v. Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61424), a bank teller sued her employer for damages in the Regional Trial Court after the bank singled her out and deducted from her salary and bonuses to cover an impostor's withdrawal before completing its investigation.[^78] The Supreme Court held that although framing a complaint under Articles 19, 20 and 21 does not by itself remove a labor dispute from the Labor Arbiter, the Regional Trial Court had jurisdiction because the teller's claim rested on Article 19 in relation to Article 21 and had no reasonable connection to a Labor Code claim.[^79] In [*Social Security System v. Ubaña*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61157), the Supreme Court likewise upheld the Regional Trial Court's jurisdiction over an abuse-of-rights complaint under Articles 19 and 20 filed by a service contractor worker against a government-owned corporation for salary differentials and damages, because no employer-employee relationship existed between the contractor's worker and the principal corporation.[^80]

## Case Discussions

### Individualized Liability for Unlawful Acts During a Strike

CASA was the sole bargaining representative of the rank-and-file employees of Central Azucarera, a sugar milling company in Passi City, Iloilo. A bargaining deadlock led to a strike from March 29 to April 12, 2017. On April 5, 2017, union officers and members used motorcycles, amakan and palay to barricade the road junction to Gate 1 of the sugar central. CASA filed an unfair labor practice complaint on August 16, 2017. Central Azucarera sought damages and attorney's fees by counterclaim. The Labor Arbiter dismissed the complaint on April 10, 2018 and denied the counterclaim for lack of merit. The National Labor Relations Commission affirmed in toto on June 29, 2018 and denied reconsideration on September 17, 2018. The Court of Appeals agreed with it that the labor tribunals had no jurisdiction over the counterclaim. It also held on May 28, 2021 that the PHP 64,759,254.72 in actual damages was not proven.[^81]

The Supreme Court En Banc denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification. It held that labor arbiters, not regular courts, have jurisdiction over the counterclaim. It ruled that Articles 19 and 20 let an employer recover damages for illegal acts committed in bad faith during a strike. The employer must prove by substantial evidence the illegal acts, the bad faith and the resulting injury. It was undisputed that the barricade of Gate 1 was illegal. Liability falls only on the officers or members who took part in it and not on the union or its funds. No provision like Article 2180 extends vicarious liability to acts governed by Articles 19 and 20. Central Azucarera did not establish individual participation or bad faith. The counterclaim for actual, moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees therefore failed.[^82]

### Unauthorized Mortgage Redemption Insurance Deduction on a Business Loan

Maria Josefina Miranda and the spouses Robert Glenn and Marjorie Fox obtained a P3,000,000.00 credit accommodation from Land Bank in June 1998. They drew three loans of P850,000.00, P1,150,000.00 and P400,000.00. Land Bank released a net P2,390,699.00 and deducted P5,700.82 from the first loan as a Mortgage Redemption Insurance premium. Robert Glenn Fox died on August 20, 1998. Miranda believed that the insurance paid the loans and stopped paying. No MRI application was ever submitted. Land Bank foreclosed the mortgage and bought the property at the auction on March 31, 2000 for P5,115,904.97. Miranda sued on March 19, 2001. The Regional Trial Court of Mariveles, Bataan, Branch 4 denied her main prayer on June 21, 2010. It ordered Land Bank to pay P150,000.00 as moral damages, P5,700.82 as reimbursement and P100,000.00 as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed in toto on February 24, 2015 and denied reconsideration on September 28, 2015.[^83]

The Supreme Court held that no MRI contract was perfected. The loan was not extinguished and the foreclosure was proper. The loan was for a future business undertaking while the MRI covered only consumer loans. Land Bank acted as an agent and did not disclose that it lacked authority. It deducted the premium and gave the impression that the MRI would cover the loan. It therefore acted in excess of its authority. The Supreme Court recalled that an earlier decision on a similar scheme rested on Articles 19, 20 and 21 together with Article 1897. It also set out the difference between Articles 20 and 21. The award of damages stood. The Court of Appeals was affirmed with modification. The monetary awards earn 6% interest from finality.[^84]

### Presumption of Good Faith in a Subsequent Void Marriage

Rene Ongpin married Alma Mantaring on February 5, 1972. Mantaring later obtained a divorce decree in Nevada. Believing he was free to marry, Ongpin married Mary Elizabeth Mercado in Princeton, New Jersey, on April 21, 1989. They separated on March 16, 2000. Ongpin obtained a judicial declaration of nullity of his marriage to Mantaring on November 25, 2003. He then filed a petition on January 8, 2006 in Bacoor, Cavite, to declare his marriage to Mercado void. The Regional Trial Court declared it void on November 12, 2009. It also ordered Ongpin to pay P250,000.00 as moral damages, P100,000.00 as exemplary damages and P150,000.00 as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals deleted these awards in CA-G.R. CV No. 98320 and denied reconsideration on May 22, 2013.[^85]

The Supreme Court denied Mercado's petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It held that the Regional Trial Court erred in treating the mere contracting of a second marriage as a ground for damages under Article 19 in relation to Articles 20 or 21. Bad faith or deliberate intent to do a wrong must be proven. Mercado did not prove that Ongpin knew his divorce was invalid or that he concealed it from her. She also showed no injury in law, because she knew of an anomaly in his first marriage as early as 1992. Without moral damages there could be no exemplary damages or attorney's fees. Her motion to dismiss her own appeal, filed on December 19, 2019, was not granted.[^86]

### Bad-Faith Breach of Commitment to Bring Infirm Patriarch to Family Celebration

Pascasio Banaria was turning 90. His family prepared a birthday party for February 22, 2004. The respondents were his brother, sister, sons, daughters, granddaughters and grandsons. His wife and guardian Adelaida was reminded of the event as early as February 2003 and confirmed his attendance. She contributed P5,000.00. About 200 guests attended. Adelaida instead took him to Tarlac and arrived on the afternoon of February 21, 2004. The couple was back in Manila on February 23. The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 216 awarded actual damages of P61,200.00, P3,000.00 and P3,275.00 and US$3,619.00 on May 23, 2011. It also gave P60,000.00 as moral damages to each plaintiff, P50,000.00 as exemplary damages and P60,000.00 as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals deleted the US$3,619.00 on October 15, 2014. It set moral damages at P300,000.00 in a lump sum, exemplary damages at P30,000.00 and attorney's fees at P50,000.00. It denied reconsideration on April 14, 2015.[^87]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It found that Adelaida intentionally failed to bring Pascasio to the celebration and so violated Article 19. She did not tell the respondents of the change in plans. It awarded damages pursuant to Article 21. Article 20 appears in the decision only as the remedy for a violation of law and was not the basis of the award.[^88]

### Extrajudicial Repossession and Demolition Under an Express Lease Stipulation

Corazon Aniceto owned El Rancho Cafe and Restaurant, which stood on Camp John Hay in Baguio City. She leased the site from CJH Development under contracts that ran until May 17, 2007. The lease made her permanent improvements the property of CJH Development at the end of the term. Her monthly rentals were paid up to February 28, 2008. On January 30, 2008, CJH Development told her to vacate by March 1, 2008. She sued to stop the closure. The trial court issued a 72-hour restraining order on March 4, 2008 and a status quo order on March 6, 2008. It later denied the writ of preliminary injunction. El Rancho was demolished on May 1, 2008. Her case became a suit for damages. The Regional Trial Court of Baguio City, Branch 61 ruled for her on December 11, 2013. It found bad faith under Articles 19, 20 and 21 and awarded actual damages of P2,183,625.00. The Court of Appeals reversed that judgment on July 27, 2015 but still ordered CJH Development to pay P2,183,625.00 for her personal properties. It denied reconsideration on March 8, 2016.[^89]

The Supreme Court denied Aniceto's petition and granted that of CJH Development. It held that a lease stipulation letting the lessor retake possession without court action is a resolutory condition and is valid. Articles 19, 20 and 21 give relief only when a right is exercised in bad faith and with the sole intent to injure. CJH Development was not shown to have acted in bad faith or with malice. It exercised its right as owner of the land. Its lawyers only advised their client to protect its interests. The Supreme Court also deleted the P2,183,625.00 award. CJH Development is not liable for personal properties that were lost or destroyed without its fault, because Aniceto refused to remove them. Any properties still in storage are to be turned over to her.[^90]

### Malicious Prosecution Based on Fabricated Complaints Dismissed at Preliminary Investigation

Menandro Sosmeña was the managing director of Expo Logistics Philippines, a local partner of a Singapore tent supplier. Benigno Bonafe, Jimmy Escobar, Joel Gomez and Hector Pangilinan worked for him. A foreign partner asked Benigno to spy on Sosmeña. The relationship soured. Benigno resigned in September 2001 and Jimmy and Joel followed in October 2001. On February 4, 2002, Sosmeña filed criminal complaints in Pasay City for malicious mischief over cut air conditioner cables. He also charged Benigno with taking P29,000.00 and Jimmy and Joel with theft. The prosecutor dismissed the complaints on May 10, 2002 for insufficient evidence and found that a grudge motivated them. The four sued for malicious prosecution. The Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch 22 found a violation of Article 19 on April 22, 2014. It awarded P200,000.00 as moral damages, P50,000.00 as exemplary damages and P25,000.00 as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed on June 30, 2017.[^91]

The Supreme Court held that malicious prosecution has four elements. A preliminary investigation counts as a proceeding for this purpose. Its dismissal satisfied the first two elements. Ill will, delay and contrived evidence showed that Sosmeña lacked probable cause and acted with malice. The Supreme Court reduced the awards. It denied the petition and affirmed with modification. Sosmeña must pay each respondent P30,000.00 as moral damages, P20,000.00 as exemplary damages and P10,000.00 as attorney's fees. The awards earn 6% interest from finality.[^92]

### Cause of Action for Bad-Faith Conduct Surrounding Non-Renewal of Distributorship

Tocoms was a distributor of Philips domestic appliances under a Distribution Agreement dated July 20, 2011. It alleged that on January 2, 2013 it learned that Philips would not renew the agreement. It also alleged that the notice to the trade was too short. It said Philips demanded a buy-back of its inventory at 40% less for phased out models and 60% less for Class B products. It claimed P20,000,000.00 in actual damages under Articles 19, 20 and 21. Tocoms sued on February 4, 2013 in the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City, Branch 266, against Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc. and others. The trial court denied the motion to dismiss on May 30, 2013 and reconsideration on July 1, 2013. The Court of Appeals reversed on March 13, 2014 in CA-G.R. SP No. 130873. It held that the complaint stated no cause of action because the agreement was non-exclusive and had expired. It denied reconsideration on August 29, 2014.[^93]

The Supreme Court granted the petition and reinstated the case. It held that a motion to dismiss for failure to state a cause of action is resolved within the four corners of the complaint and its annexes. Abuse of rights under Article 19 requires bad faith. Failure to discharge the duties in Article 19 is compensable under Article 20 if the act is contrary to law and under Article 21 if it is contrary to morals, good customs or public policy. If the allegations are hypothetically admitted, they show bad faith in violation of Article 19 and punishable under Article 21. Whether Philips acted with malice remained to be tried. The Supreme Court ordered the trial court to try the case with utmost dispatch.[^94]

### Negligence of Marathon Organizer Versus Non-Liability of Financial Sponsor

Intergames organized a marathon on June 15, 1980. Cosmos paid a P55,000.00 sponsorship fee. Rommel Abrogar, then 18, was bumped by a jeepney on Don Mariano Marcos Avenue while he ran. He died. The petitioners sued for damages in the Court of First Instance of Rizal on October 28, 1980. The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 83 gave judgment on May 10, 1991. It awarded P28,061.63 as actual damages, P100,000.00 as moral damages and P50,000.00 as exemplary damages. It added attorney's fees of P17,806.16. The Court of Appeals reversed it on March 10, 2004.[^95]

The Supreme Court found that Intergames was negligent and that its negligence was the proximate cause of the death. It cited liability for negligence under Article 2176 and Article 20 together with Article 1173. Assumption of risk and the waiver did not bar recovery. Gross negligence supported exemplary damages. Cosmos was only a sponsor. Its role was too remote from the death. The Supreme Court partly affirmed the Court of Appeals by absolving Cosmos. It reversed as to Intergames and reinstated the trial court judgment with modifications. It awarded P113,484.52 for loss of earning capacity. It imposed 6% interest on the actual, moral and exemplary damages and the earning capacity from May 10, 1991 until full payment. The total then earns 6% from finality. Attorney's fees are 10% of the total award.[^96]

### Good-Faith Exercise of Administrative Discretion on Sabbatical Leave

Elizabeth Diaz had served the University of the Philippines since 1963 and was an associate professor in the College of Mass Communication. On May 3, 1988, she applied for a one-year sabbatical leave with pay. The dean recommended denial. The chancellor recommended leave without pay instead. The university required a Report for Duty form. Diaz refused to submit it. Her salaries were withheld. The Ombudsman dismissed her complaint against the university officials on May 4, 1989. The Supreme Court dismissed her petition in G.R. No. 89207 on August 31, 1989. She sued the university and its officials for damages on July 18, 1989 in the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City, Branch 71. On April 17, 1996, it awarded P133,665.50 in unpaid salaries, P300,000.00 as moral damages, P60,000.00 as exemplary damages and P50,000.00 as attorney's fees. It later absolved Dean Georgina Encanto. The Court of Appeals reversed on April 28, 2005. It ordered the university to pay P21,879.64 in salaries and deleted the damages.[^97]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification. It noted that the complaint rested on Articles 19 and 20. Abuse of right requires a legal right or duty, bad faith in exercising it and the sole intent to injure. Sabbatical leave is a privilege and not a right. The Ombudsman, the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court itself had found no bad faith in the denial. The delay in resolving her application was also not shown to be in bad faith. The university must pay the salaries that were withheld. These cover July 1 to October 31, 1988, when her name was dropped from the class schedule without her knowledge. The later periods that are due once she submits the required documents. The interest is 6% a year.[^98]

### Civil Court Jurisdiction Over Arbitrary Salary Deductions Framed Under Articles 19, 20 and 21

Josephine Gomez was a teller at the Domestic Airport Branch of PCIB. In January 1985, she accepted for deposit two genuine Bank of New Zealand drafts, one of them payable to Servants C/C.R. Harrington. She first asked her supervisor, who said they were acceptable. An impostor withdrew P45,000.00 and P5,600.00. The bank paid P50,600.00 to the real depositor. PCIB found her grossly negligent. The trial court found that it never answered her request for the basis of that finding. PCIB deducted P200.00 from her salary and 50% of her bonuses and profit sharing from January 15, 1986. She sued for damages in the Regional Trial Court of Makati City on February 10, 1986. On May 25, 1999, Branch 145 awarded actual damages with 12% interest, moral damages and P50,000.00 as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed in toto on May 23, 2011 and denied reconsideration on December 7, 2011.[^99]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals in toto. It held that the regular court had jurisdiction. Gomez's claim rested on a quasi-delict under Article 19 in relation to Article 21. It had no reasonable causal connection with the claims that Article 224 of the Labor Code gives to labor arbiters. Article 19 states the abuse of right principle. Articles 20 and 21 supply the sanctions. PCIB deducted from her salary while the investigation was pending. It refused to give a factual or legal basis for its finding. It did not penalize the other employees who took part in the transaction. The awards of moral damages and attorney's fees were proper.[^100]

### Regular Civil Court Jurisdiction Over Contractor Worker's Abuse-of-Rights Claim

Debbie Ubaña applied to the Social Security System in July 1995 and was referred to DBP Service Corporation for transitory employment. From May 1996 she served at the SSS Daet branch under service contracts. She was paid P171.00 a day at first. As a processor she received P229.00 a day or P5,038.00 a month, while a regular SSS processor received P18,622.00 a month. She resigned on August 26, 2002. On December 26, 2002, she sued DBP Service Corporation, the SSS and the SSS retirees association in the Regional Trial Court of Daet, Camarines Norte, Branch 39. She asked for P572,682.67 in actual damages, P300,000.00 as moral damages and exemplary damages. The trial court dismissed the case for lack of jurisdiction on October 1, 2003. It reversed itself on March 6, 2007 and reinstated the case against the SSS. The Court of Appeals denied the SSS petition for certiorari on July 29, 2011 and denied reconsideration on January 10, 2012.[^101]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It held that Ubaña was never an SSS employee, since she worked for independent contractors. Both sides agreed that there was no employer-employee relation between her and the SSS. The labor arbiter's jurisdiction under Article 217 of the Labor Code requires one. She could therefore sue under Articles 19 and 20 in the regular court to recover the proper salary. The Supreme Court observed that it was unfair for the SSS to pay her far less than a regular processor for the same work. It remanded the case to the Regional Trial Court for continued proceedings. It did not rule on the merits of her damages claim.[^102]

### Malicious Prosecution and Public Humiliation of Union-Organizing Supervisor

Artemio Ligo was the warehouse supervisor of Marsman. He was in charge of destroying bad order and expired drugs. In February 1993, warehouse manager Quirino Iledan said he got a call from a certain Isabelito Miguel about the sale of such drugs behind a church in Parañaque. Marsman asked the NBI to investigate on March 15, 1993. The NBI arrested several persons on May 7, 1993 but not Ligo. On May 8, 1993, Iledan asked Ligo to go with him to the NBI on the pretext of visiting a colleague. Ligo was arrested and detained for at least 10 days. He and others were presented to the media at a press conference. He was charged with violating Republic Act No. 3720. The Metropolitan Trial Court of Taguig City acquitted him on October 12, 1994. Marsman dismissed him for negligence and breach of trust. The Regional Trial Court of Las Piñas City, Branch 275 gave judgment for him on February 28, 2005. It awarded P3,000,000.00 as moral damages, P500,000.00 as exemplary damages and 25% of the total as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed in toto.[^103]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals in toto. It named Articles 19, 20, 21, 26, 29, 32, 33, 35, 2217 and 2219(8) as the statutory basis of an action for malicious prosecution. The four elements were present. Marsman instigated the prosecution and the acquittal came for lack of evidence. The tip came from a person who was never presented in court. Marsman did not verify the tip before it set the process in motion. Iledan's hostility toward union members showed malice. Marsman and Iledan acted with evident bad faith and in gross disregard of Ligo's rights, so damages were proper. The Supreme Court sent a copy of the decision to the Department of Environment and Natural Resources to look into how Marsman disposed of its expired drugs.[^104]

### Bank's Gross Negligence in Premature Housing Loan Certification

The Capistranos contracted with GCB Builders on May 28, 1992 to build a house for P265,000.00. They assigned the proceeds of a P300,000.00 loan from the National Home Mortgage Finance Corporation to pay for it. Comsavings Bank, the originating bank, granted an interim loan of P260,000.00 and paid GCB Builders P265,000.00 in four releases. The house remained unfinished. GCB Builders asked for another P25,000.00 to complete it. The Capistranos sued on July 12, 1993. The Regional Trial Court of Makati City, Branch 135 ruled for them on April 25, 2003. It ordered the completion of the house and awarded P25,000.00 as actual damages, P200,000.00 as moral damages, P100,000.00 as exemplary damages, P30,000.00 as attorney's fees and P4,500.00 in rentals. The Court of Appeals decided on November 30, 2005. It absolved the National Home Mortgage Finance Corporation and reduced moral damages to P100,000.00 and exemplary damages to P50,000.00.[^105]

The Supreme Court held that the liability of Comsavings Bank rested on Articles 20 and 1170 and not on the warranties in its purchase of loan agreement. A bank must observe the highest degree of diligence in its transactions. The bank showed gross negligence. It made the Capistranos sign the completion certificate before construction began. It gained P17,306.83 from deductions. It also accepted unsigned pictures of a different house. Article 2219 allows moral damages for the acts referred to in Article 20. The actual damages of P25,000.00 became temperate damages under Article 2224. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals with modifications. It fixed P25,000.00 as temperate damages and P30,000.00 as attorney's fees. All damages earn 6% interest from finality.[^106]

### Disconnection of Buyer's Water Supply Without Prior Notice

Joyce Ardiente and her husband owned a housing unit at Emily Homes, Balulang, Cagayan de Oro City. On June 2, 1994, she sold her rights in it to Ma. Theresa Pastorfide for P70,000.00. The agreement made the water and power bills the buyer's account from June 1, 1994. The water connection stayed in Ardiente's name. On March 12, 1999, the Cagayan de Oro Water District cut it off without notice. It told Pastorfide that she owed three months of bills and that Ardiente had asked for the disconnection. Pastorfide paid on March 15, 1999. She sued Ardiente, the water district and its manager Gaspar Gonzalez on April 14, 1999. Her water was restored only after a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction on December 14, 1999. The Regional Trial Court of Cagayan de Oro City, Branch 24 held them solidarily liable on August 15, 2001. The Court of Appeals affirmed on August 28, 2003. It cut moral and exemplary damages to P100,000.00 each and attorney's fees to P25,000.00. It denied reconsideration on December 17, 2003.[^107]

The Supreme Court denied Ardiente's petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It held that she could ask Pastorfide to transfer the account but could not cause the disconnection. She asked for it without warning. Her intent to harm was evident. Article 19 gives no remedy. An action for damages lies under Article 20 or Article 21. The water district also failed to give prior notice and neglected to reconnect after Pastorfide paid. Ardiente, the district and Gonzalez were solidarily liable. Moral damages rest on Article 2219 in connection with Articles 20 and 21. Exemplary damages were proper because the Pastorfides lacked water for more than nine months.[^108]

### Official Retaliation and Humiliation After Subordinate Obtains a Temporary Restraining Order

Emma Rosqueta was Deputy Commissioner at the Bureau of Customs. She tendered a courtesy resignation on January 23, 2001 and withdrew it on June 5, 2001. President Arroyo appointed Gil Valera to her post on July 13, 2001. Rosqueta sued Commissioner Titus Villanueva and others in the Regional Trial Court of Manila. The court issued a restraining order on August 27, 2001 and a writ of preliminary injunction on August 28, 2001. On November 22, 2001, Villanueva still issued a memorandum letting Valera exercise the powers of the office. During the Bureau's centennial in February 2002, the Panorama magazine, the souvenir program and the billboard left out Rosqueta. She sued Villanueva for damages on February 28, 2002 in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City. It dismissed her complaint. The Court of Appeals reversed it. It awarded P500,000.00 as moral damages, P200,000.00 as exemplary damages and P100,000.00 as attorney's fees and litigation expenses.[^109]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification. It held that a person must act in good faith in exercising a right or duty under Article 19. Articles 20 and 21 grant indemnity for the injury that such an abuse causes. Villanueva could not rely on the advice of the Solicitor General, because a court order must be obeyed. Ignoring the injunction showed bad faith and intent to spite Rosqueta. The Supreme Court cited a rule that refusing to obey an order enjoining an otherwise lawful act is an abuse of right. Her later appointment to another post did not erase the injury. The Supreme Court reduced the awards to P200,000.00 as moral damages, P50,000.00 as exemplary damages and P50,000.00 as attorney's fees and litigation expenses.[^110]

### Foreclosure of Club Share by Mailing Final Notice to a Known Closed Postal Box

Sixto Clemente, Jr. bought one share of Calatagan Golf Club. The club issued his certificate on May 2, 1990 for P120,000.00. His monthly dues were P400.00. He paid in March and December 1991 and then stopped. The club sent demand letters on September 21 and October 22, 1992 to the post office box in his application. Both came back marked closed. Its board authorized foreclosure on December 1, 1992. The club sent a final letter on December 7, 1992 to the same box. It sold his share at auction on January 15, 1993 for P64,000.00. Clemente learned of the sale in November 1997 and filed a claim with the Securities and Exchange Commission. The Commission dismissed it on November 15, 2000 as prescribed. The Court of Appeals reversed on June 1, 2004. It restored his share and awarded P400,000.00 in damages less P5,200.00 in unpaid dues.[^111]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. The six-month period in Section 69 of the Corporation Code does not apply to unpaid membership dues. The club did not follow its own by-laws and did not show due diligence. It knew that the earlier letters had been returned. The bad faith of the club brought Articles 19, 20 and 21 into operation. Actual damages were warranted. The Supreme Court agreed with the awards of P200,000.00 as moral damages, P100,000.00 as exemplary damages and P100,000.00 as attorney's fees. It added that Article 32 squarely entitled Clemente to damages.[^112]

### Fraudulent Encashment of Blank Check as Self-Help and Bank's Gross Negligence

Arturo Ignacio, Jr. was the elder brother of Alice Sandejas and Rosita Cusi. He left a signed UCPB check for a condominium lease renewal with the payee's name filled in but the date and amount blank. It was to be given to his nephew Benjamin Espiritu. The check came into Alice's hands. She believed Arturo had cheated Rosita of P3,000,000.00. She and Rosita filled in March 17, 1995 and three million pesos. A driver posed as the payee and they deposited the check in a joint account at Security Bank and Trust Company. They then withdrew the money. Arturo and his wife Evelyn first sued the bank, its manager and cashier and Benjamin on June 18, 1995. They impleaded Alice, Rosita and Patricia Sandejas on November 7, 1995. The Regional Trial Court of Pasig City, Branch 158 ruled for the spouses on December 18, 1998. The Court of Appeals affirmed with modification on August 27, 2002. It deleted the awards in favor of Benjamin.[^113]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It held that Alice and Rosita should have gone to court and not taken the law into their own hands. The rule of pari delicto did not apply. Article 20 and Article 21 supported moral damages. Encashing the check was a violation of law and of the policy that no one may take the law into his own hands. The bank's negligence was so gross that it amounted to willful injury. Exemplary damages and attorney's fees were also proper. Patricia and Benjamin were not entitled to moral damages. Filing a case in good faith does not give rise to them. The damage is damnum absque injuria.[^114]

### Erroneous Hepatitis B Result Released by Unsupervised Clinical Laboratory

Ranida Salvador started work as a trainee in the accounting department of Limay Bulk Handling Terminal, Inc. on October 1, 1993. As a requirement, she underwent a Hepatitis B test at the Community Diagnostic Center. The medical technologist, Orlando Garcia, Jr., conducted the test. The center issued a reactive result on October 22, 1993. The company terminated her. Her father Ramon suffered a heart attack when he heard the news. Later tests came back negative. The company rehired her. Ranida and Ramon sued on July 25, 1994 and named the pathologist, Dr. Castro, in an amended complaint on September 26, 1994. Castro said he rarely went to the center and that the result bore only his rubber stamp. The trial court dismissed the case on September 1, 1997. The Court of Appeals reversed on February 27, 2004. It cleared Castro and held Garcia liable for gross negligence. It denied reconsideration on June 16, 2005.[^115]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It stated the elements of negligence as a duty, a breach, an injury and a proximate cause. Violating a statutory duty is negligence. Garcia broke the duties that Republic Act No. 4688, Republic Act No. 5527 and the Department of Health rules placed on those who run a clinical laboratory. The Supreme Court quoted Article 20 on the duty to indemnify one who is damaged by an act contrary to law. Garcia must pay the respondents P50,000.00 as moral damages, P50,000.00 as exemplary damages and P25,000.00 as attorney's fees.[^116]

### Unilateral Employer Directive to Debit Resigned Employee's Checking Account

Trazo opened a payroll current account with China Banking Corporation. A subsidy of P7,000.00 was credited to it on December 29, 1997. She resigned on December 31, 1997 effective January 1, 1998. Romeo dela Rosa, an assistant vice-president of her former employer PBCOM, wrote a senior officer of the bank on January 5 to debit the P7,000.00. The bank debited it the same day. Her checks to Bliss Development Corp and House of Sara Lee were dishonored. She sued PBCOM, dela Rosa, the bank and the officer. The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 79 dismissed the case on October 7, 1998. It dismissed the claim against the bank and the officer for improper venue and the claim against PBCOM and dela Rosa for lack of cause of action. The Court of Appeals reversed on March 25, 2004. It reinstated the complaint and gave her 10 days to amend it. It denied reconsideration on September 23, 2004.[^117]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It held that the complaint did not state abuse of rights under Article 19. Its allegations did show an act contrary to law under Article 20. Article 19 applies where no law is violated and the act falls within the defendant's rights or duties. Article 20 applies where the act itself violates a legal provision. The complaint alleged a violation of her property rights, which Article 429 protects. A complaint need not cite article numbers. Only ultimate facts are required. The arguments of the petitioners were defenses to be raised at trial.[^118]

### Pre-Termination of Coaching Contract Under a Void Sole-Opinion Clause

GF Equity hired Arturo Valenzona as head coach of the Alaska team in the Philippine Basketball Association for two years from January 1, 1988 to December 31, 1989. His pay was P35,000.00 a month plus a service vehicle. Paragraph 3 let the company terminate the contract if, in its sole opinion, he failed to show enough skill. His lawyer warned him of the clause, but he signed. Alaska placed third in the Open and All-Filipino Conferences in 1988. GF Equity ended the contract by a letter dated September 26, 1988. Valenzona demanded payment on July 30, 1994 and sued on September 26, 1994 in the Regional Trial Court of Manila. It dismissed the complaint on June 28, 1997. The Court of Appeals reversed on October 14, 2002. It found an abuse of right under Article 19 and awarded 15 months of unearned income, P20,000.00 as moral damages, P20,000.00 as exemplary damages and 10% as attorney's fees.[^119]

The Supreme Court set aside the Court of Appeals decision. It declared the clause void for lack of mutuality under Article 1308. GF Equity could still end the contract, but it needed a legal basis and offered none. Its act was not willful because it relied on the clause. The pre-termination rested on an illegal ground and was contrary to law. GF Equity negligently failed to give a legal basis for it. It abused the right of Valenzona and was liable under Article 19 in relation to Article 20. The defense of laches failed because the action was filed within the 10-year period in Article 1144. The Supreme Court deleted the moral and exemplary damages because GF Equity did not act in bad faith. It ordered GF Equity to pay P525,000.00 as actual damages and P60,000.00 as attorney's fees.[^120]

### Failure of Law School to Promptly Inform Graduating Student of Failing Grade

Romeo Jader was a law student of the University of the East from 1984 to 1988. He got an incomplete grade in Practice Court I and took the removal examination on March 28, 1988. On May 30, 1988, Professor Carlos Ortega submitted a grade of 5. A tentative list of candidates for graduation carried a note on his deficiency. He attended the investiture on April 16, 1988 and took leave from work from April 20 to September 30, 1988. He enrolled in a pre-bar class and learned of his failing grade. He could not take the 1988 bar examinations. He sued for damages. The Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch IX ruled for him. It awarded P35,470.00 with legal interest, P5,000.00 as attorney's fees and costs. The Court of Appeals affirmed and added P50,000.00 as moral damages.[^121]

The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification. It held that the school could not show good faith in giving him the belated information. Abuse of right under Article 19 requires the absence of good faith. The Supreme Court cited Articles 19 and 20 on the principle of good dealings. A school has a duty to inform its students of their academic status. It cannot blame the professors. Its failure to inform him promptly misled him into thinking he had met all the requirements. The school was negligent and liable for actual damages. The Supreme Court deleted the moral damages because a senior law student should have verified his status. UE must pay P35,470.00 with 6% interest a year from the filing of the complaint, P5,000.00 as attorney's fees and costs.[^122]

### Non-Liability of Voyage Charterer for Unseaworthiness of Chartered Vessel

The passenger vessel MV Doña Paz collided with the tanker MT Vector on December 20, 1987 at about 10:30 p.m. near Dumali Point. The tanker had left Limay on December 19, 1987 under a voyage charter to Caltex. Teresita and Sotera Cañezal sued Sulpicio on February 13, 1989 in the Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch 8. Sulpicio filed a third-party complaint. On September 15, 1992, the trial court ordered Sulpicio to pay P1,241,287.44 and dismissed the third-party complaint. The Court of Appeals decided on April 15, 1997. It awarded P100,000.00 for the deaths of Sebastian and Corazon Cañezal, P306,480.00 for his unearned income, P300,000.00 as moral damages and P50,000.00 as attorney's fees. It held Vector and Caltex equally liable. It relied on Articles 20 and 2176.[^123]

The Supreme Court granted the petition and set aside the Court of Appeals insofar as it held Caltex liable. A voyage charterer is free from liability to third persons. The charterer has no duty to make sure that the vessel meets the legal requirements, because that duty rests on the common carrier. Caltex owed only ordinary diligence like any shipper. It had reason to believe that the tanker could carry cargo. The Coast Guard also allowed the vessel to sail. The Court of Appeals decision stood as to Sulpicio's payment. Vector Shipping and Francisco Soriano must reimburse Sulpicio. There were no costs.[^124]

### Bank Acting as Insurance Agent Accepting Over-Age Borrower's Insurance Premium

Juan Dans was 76 years old when he applied with the Development Bank of the Philippines for a loan. The bank advised him to get mortgage redemption insurance. The loan was cut to P300,000.00, approved on August 4, 1987 and released on August 11, 1987. The bank deducted a P1,476.00 premium. He submitted the application and health statement on August 15, 1987 and died on September 3, 1987. The insurance pool declared him ineligible because he was over 60. The bank offered to refund the premium and an ex gratia amount of P30,000.00. His widow refused. The family sued on February 10, 1989. The Regional Trial Court of Basilan, Branch I granted summary judgment on March 10, 1990. It awarded P139,500.00, declared the loan settled and awarded P10,000.00 as attorney's fees and P10,000.00 as costs. The Court of Appeals affirmed on September 7, 1992 and denied reconsideration on April 20, 1993.[^125]

The Supreme Court modified the Court of Appeals decision. It found that no insurance contract was perfected. The bank acted as lender and as agent of the pool and exceeded its authority. It did not disclose the limits of its authority. The decision prints Article 1897 as Article 1987. The Supreme Court held that such non-disclosure implies deception, so Articles 19, 20 and 21 come into play. The bank was not liable for the whole policy because it was speculative that Dans would have found other cover. He died on the 19th day after applying and the 23rd day after the release. The bank must reimburse P1,476.00 with legal interest from the filing of the complaint. It must also pay P50,000.00 as moral damages, P10,000.00 as attorney's fees and costs.[^126]

### Honest Mistake of Identity in Bouncing Check Complaint as *Damnum Absque Injuria*

Albenson Enterprises delivered mild steel plates to Guaranteed Industries from September to November 1980. It received a bad check for P2,575.00, number 136361, drawn on the account of E.L. Woodworks. Albenson traced the name Eugenio Baltao to Guaranteed Industries and demanded payment from Eugenio S. Baltao, who denied issuing the check. Albenson filed a complaint for violating Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 on February 14, 1983. An information followed on September 5, 1983. Baltao had a namesake, his son Eugenio Baltao III, who ran E.L. Woodworks in the same building as Guaranteed Industries. Fiscal Castro exonerated Baltao on January 30, 1984. Baltao then sued Albenson and its officers for damages. The trial court awarded P133,350.00 as actual damages, P1,000,000.00 as moral damages, P200,000.00 as exemplary damages and P100,000.00 as attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals decided CA-G.R. CV No. 14948 on May 13, 1989. It cut moral damages to P500,000.00 and attorney's fees to P50,000.00.[^127]

The Supreme Court granted the petition and reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision. It stated the elements of Articles 19 and 21 and noted that Article 20 does not distinguish between willful and negligent acts. It found no abuse of right. Albenson honestly believed that the check was issued by Baltao. Baltao did nothing to clear up the mistaken identity. There was no proof of actual damages. Any damage was damnum absque injuria. No basis existed for exemplary damages or attorney's fees. Costs were charged against Baltao.[^128]

### Civil Indemnity Under Article 20 and Private Prosecutor Intervention in Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 Cases

Rosario Claudio faced criminal cases for violating Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 105, including Criminal Cases Q-40909 to Q-40913. Judge Tomas Tadeo, Jr. presided over the branch. On January 8, 1987, the court rejected the appearance of Atty. Nicolito Bustos as private prosecutor for the offended party, Charmina Banal. It reasoned that the statute does not provide for civil liability and that the offense is against public order. It denied reconsideration on March 31, 1987. Banal filed a petition for certiorari and mandamus.[^129]

The Supreme Court granted the petition. It noted that Article 100 of the Revised Penal Code makes every person criminally liable also civilly liable. It quoted Article 20 and held that indemnification may be had regardless of whether a special law so provides. A holding to the contrary would leave the payee empty-handed. It also helps to avoid a multiplicity of suits. The Supreme Court ordered the trial court to allow a private prosecutor to intervene for Banal. The decision was immediately executory.[^130]

Banaria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66456) (2020) -- Where a second wife and guardian confirmed her infirm husband's attendance at his 90th birthday celebration organized by his family and then took him to Tarlac without notifying them and the Court of Appeals modified the trial court's judgment by deleting US$3,619.00 travel expenses and awarding P61,200.00, P3,000.00 and P3,275.00 actual damages, Php300,000.00 moral damages, Php30,000.00 exemplary damages and Php50,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that her intentional bad-faith breach of commitment violated Article 19 and warranted damages under Article 21 and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^146]
- [*CJH Development Corp. v. Aniceto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66462) (2020) -- Where a lessor demolished a restaurant structure after lease expiration and stored the lessee's personal properties pursuant to express lease clauses and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's P1,000,000.00 moral, P500,000.00 exemplary and P200,000.00 attorney's fees awards while retaining P2,183,625.00 actual damages less undamaged properties returned, the Supreme Court held that extrajudicial repossession under a valid contract clause in good faith creates no liability under Articles 19, 20 and 21, deleted the P2,183,625.00 actual damages award and ordered remaining stored properties, if any, turned over to the lessee.[^147]
- [*Sosmeña v. Bonafe*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66223) (2020) -- Where a company managing director filed fabricated malicious mischief and theft complaints against four resigned employees out of personal ill will and the prosecutor dismissed the charges at preliminary investigation, after which the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded P200,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P25,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that malicious prosecution under Articles 19, 20, 21 and 2219(8) extends to preliminary investigations and affirmed with modification awarding each respondent P30,000.00 moral damages, P20,000.00 exemplary damages and P10,000.00 attorney's fees with 6% annual interest from finality.[^148]
- [*Tocoms Philippines, Inc. v. Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66168) (2020) -- Where an outgoing appliance distributor sued a foreign manufacturer for damages under Articles 19, 20 and 21 for giving no sufficient notice of non-renewal and demanding a buy-back of its stock at 40 and 60 percent below price and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court by dismissing the complaint for lack of cause of action, the Supreme Court held that allegations of bad-faith conduct surrounding contract expiration state a tort cause of action under Articles 19, 20 and 21 and reinstated the trial court's orders.[^149]
- [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Co.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921) (2017) -- Where an 18-year-old runner died after being hit by a jeepney on an unblocked highway during a junior marathon and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's solidary award of P28,061.63 actual damages, P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P17,806.16 attorney's fees against the organizer and sponsor, the Supreme Court held the organizer liable for gross negligence under Articles 20, 1173 and 2176 while absolving the mere financial sponsor and reinstated the awards against the organizer plus P113,484.52 for loss of earning capacity and 6% annual interest from May 10, 1991.[^150]
- [*Diaz v. Encanto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61630) (2016) -- Where university officials recommended that a professor's sabbatical leave be without pay and withheld her salaries when she did not report for duty and the Court of Appeals deleted the trial court's awards of P300,000.00 moral damages, P60,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees and reduced unpaid salaries from P133,665.50 to P21,879.64, the Supreme Court held that public officers are not liable for moral or exemplary damages without proof of bad faith and affirmed with modification ordering the university to pay the withheld salaries with 6% annual interest.[^151]
- [*Philippine Commercial International Bank v. Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61424) (2015) -- Where a bank singled out a teller and deducted from her salary and bonuses before finishing its investigation of a P50,600.00 impostor withdrawal and the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded P5,006.00 actual damages with 12% interest, moral damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that an employee's damage suit challenging oppressive salary deductions without seeking Labor Code reliefs is a civil tort under Article 19 in relation to Article 21 within regular court jurisdiction and affirmed the judgment in toto.[^152]
- [*Social Security System v. Ubaña*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61157) (2015) -- Where a worker deployed by service contractors sued the Social Security System in the Regional Trial Court under Articles 19 and 20 for P572,682.67 in salary differentials, P300,000.00 moral damages, P20,000.00 attorney's fees and P1,000.00 appearance fees and the Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court's reinstatement of the complaint, the Supreme Court held that regular civil courts have jurisdiction where no employer-employee relationship exists between the contractor's worker and the government corporation and ordered the trial court to proceed with dispatch.[^153]
- [*Marsman & Co. v. Ligo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61047) (2015) -- Where a warehouse manager asked a supervisor to come to the National Bureau of Investigation on a pretext and had him arrested on an unverified tip and presented at a press conference and he was acquitted for lack of evidence and the trial court and Court of Appeals awarded P3,000,000.00 moral damages, P500,000.00 exemplary damages and 25% attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that the employer acted without probable cause and with legal malice and affirmed the awards in toto under Articles 19, 20, 21, 2217 and 2219(8).[^154]
- [*Comsavings Bank v. Spouses Capistrano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56165) (2013) -- Where an originating bank had a borrower sign a certificate of house completion before construction had started and the Court of Appeals modified the trial court's judgment by holding the bank and builder solidarily liable for P25,000.00 actual damages, P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P30,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held the bank solidarily liable for gross negligence under Articles 20 and 1170 and affirmed with modification replacing actual damages with P25,000.00 temperate damages plus 6% annual interest from finality.[^155]
- [*Ardiente v. Spouses Pastorfide*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55955) (2013) -- Where a home seller caused the water district to disconnect the buyers' water supply without prior notice nearly five years after the sale and the Court of Appeals reduced the trial court's solidary awards from P200,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees to P100,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages and P25,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that cutting off the water connection without warning was an actionable abuse of rights under Article 19 with Articles 20 and 21 and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^156]
- [*Villanueva v. Rosqueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53481) (2010) -- Where a Customs Commissioner let a replacement exercise a Deputy Commissioner's functions while a preliminary injunction was in force, withheld her salary and left her out of centennial memorabilia and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal by awarding P500,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and P100,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that ignoring the injunction showed bad faith that made him liable under Articles 19 and 20 and affirmed with modification reducing the awards against petitioner Titus B. Villanueva to P200,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses.[^157]
- [*Calatagan Golf Club, Inc. v. Clemente, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49070) (2009) -- Where a golf club mailed its final delinquency notice to a member's post office box that it knew from two prior returned notices was already closed and auctioned his share for P64,000.00 over P5,200.00 unpaid dues and the Court of Appeals reversed the Securities and Exchange Commission's dismissal by ordering a new stock certificate and P200,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages and P100,000.00 attorney's fees less P5,200.00, the Supreme Court held that the club's bad-faith violation of its By-Laws triggered Articles 19, 20 and 21 and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^158]
- [*Sandejas v. Spouses Ignacio, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46434) (2007) -- Where two sisters filled in their brother's signed blank check for P3,000,000.00 and encashed it through an impostor at a bank that waived identification and endorsement and the Court of Appeals modified the trial court's judgment by holding the sisters, the bank and its officers solidarily liable for P3,000,000.00 with legal interest, P200,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees while deleting the awards to Benjamin, the Supreme Court held that fraudulent self-help and gross banking negligence violate Articles 20 and 21 and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^159]
- [*Garcia, Jr. v. Salvador*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41343) (2007) -- Where a medical technologist operating a clinical laboratory without a pathologist's supervision released a false reactive Hepatitis B result that caused a trainee to lose her job and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal by awarding P50,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P25,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that violating clinical laboratory statutes and health regulations breaches a duty actionable under Article 20 and affirmed the Court of Appeals in toto.[^160]
- [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367) (2006) -- Where a bank directed another bank to debit P7,000.00 from a resigned manager's personal checking account without her consent, causing her checks to bounce and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal for lack of cause of action, the Supreme Court held that the complaint stated a cause of action for an act contrary to law under Article 20 in relation to Article 429 rather than abuse of rights under Article 19 and affirmed the reinstatement of the complaint.[^161]
- [*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343) (2005) -- Where a basketball team owner pre-terminated a coach's two-year contract under a clause allowing termination at the owner's sole opinion and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal by awarding actual, moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees, the Supreme Court declared the clause void under Article 1308, held the pre-termination actionable under Article 19 in relation to Article 20 and set aside the Court of Appeals decision to award P525,000.00 actual damages and P60,000.00 attorney's fees.[^162]
- [*University of the East v. Jader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59575) (2000) -- Where a university included a senior law student on the tentative graduation list and allowed him to join the graduation rites without promptly informing him of a failing grade of 5 in a removal examination and the Court of Appeals added P50,000.00 moral damages to the trial court's award of P35,470.00 actual damages and P5,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held the school liable for negligence under Articles 19 and 20 for actual damages and attorney's fees with 6% annual interest from filing of the complaint but deleted moral damages because the student, as a senior law student, should have verified his own grade.[^163]
- [*Caltex (Philippines), Inc. v. Sulpicio Lines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38239) (1999) -- Where a passenger vessel collided with a motor tanker voyage-chartered by a petroleum shipper and the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court's dismissal of the third-party complaint by holding the voyage charterer and tanker owner equally liable under Articles 20 and 2176 to reimburse the passenger carrier, the Supreme Court held that a voyage charterer has no legal duty to inspect a common carrier's vessel or licenses and set aside the Court of Appeals decision insofar as it held the charterer liable.[^164]
- [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_109937_1994.html) (1994) -- Where a mortgagee bank acting as an insurance agent required a 76-year-old borrower to apply for Mortgage Redemption Insurance and deducted a P1,476.00 premium despite a 60-year age limit and the trial court and Court of Appeals declared the P300,000.00 loan settled and ordered reimbursement of P139,500.00 paid under protest plus P10,000.00 attorney's fees and P10,000.00 costs, the Supreme Court held that exceeding agency limits under Article 1897 triggered damages under Articles 19, 20 and 21 without extinguishing the loan and modified the judgment to award a P1,476.00 premium refund with legal interest, P50,000.00 moral damages and P10,000.00 attorney's fees.[^165]
- [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html) (1993) -- Where an unpaid supplier filed a Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 complaint against the president of the buyer company (whose namesake son ran the drawer company at the same address) and the Court of Appeals modified the trial court's awards to P133,350.00 actual damages, P500,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees, the Supreme Court held that filing a criminal complaint in good faith with probable cause and without malice produces *damnum absque injuria* under Articles 19, 20 and 21 and reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision.[^166]
- [*Banal v. Tadeo, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/dec1987/gr_l-78911_1987.html) (1987) -- Where a Regional Trial Court judge barred a private prosecutor from appearing for the offended payee in five Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 prosecutions on the ground that the statute punishes an offense against public order without providing civil indemnity, the Supreme Court held that Article 20 of the Civil Code requires every person who causes damage contrary to law to indemnify the injured party regardless of whether the special penal statute expressly says so and ordered the trial court to allow the private prosecutor to intervene.[^167]

## Suggested Answers

### Problem 1: Statutory Breach by an Unsupervised Clinical Laboratory

A pre-employment applicant underwent a Hepatitis B blood test at a free-standing clinical laboratory owned and run by a registered medical technologist. Without the supervision or authorization of a licensed pathologist, the medical technologist conducted the examination and released a report bearing the pathologist's rubber-stamp signature that falsely declared the applicant reactive for Hepatitis B. Relying on the report, the employer terminated the applicant, who suffered severe mental anguish before confirmatory tests at a hospital proved non-reactive. When the applicant sued the laboratory owner for moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Article 20 of the Civil Code, the owner argued that he had no intent to injure the applicant and that Republic Act No. 4688 and Republic Act No. 5527 are regulatory statutes that do not expressly grant a private civil action for damages. Is the laboratory owner liable under Article 20?

**Suggested Answer:** Yes, the laboratory owner is liable for damages under Article 20 of the Civil Code.[^168] Under Article 20, every person who, contrary to law, willfully or negligently causes damage to another shall indemnify the latter for the same.[^169] In [*Garcia, Jr. v. Salvador*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41343), the Supreme Court held that the Code Commission incorporated Article 20 to provide the general legal basis for awarding damages whenever a person suffers injury because another has violated a legal provision or implementing public-safety regulation.[^170] Operating a clinical laboratory and releasing diagnostic test results without the direct supervision and directive of a licensed pathologist violates Republic Act No. 4688, Section 29(b) of Republic Act No. 5527 and Department of Health Administrative Order No. 49-B (1988) and because Article 20 covers negligent as well as willful statutory violations, the laboratory owner's gross negligence renders him liable for moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees even without intent to injure.[^171]

### Problem 2: Deceptive Mortgage Redemption Insurance Deduction by a Lending Bank

A borrower obtained a P300,000.00 loan from a bank at age 76. Acting as both lender and insurance agent for an insurance pool, the bank required the borrower to apply for Mortgage Redemption Insurance and deducted the P1,476.00 premium from the loan proceeds despite knowing that the master policy fixed the maximum acceptance age at 60 years. After the borrower died three weeks later and the insurance pool disapproved the application due to age ineligibility, the borrower's estate sued the bank to declare the P300,000.00 mortgage loan fully extinguished by the insurance coverage and to recover damages. May the bank be compelled to treat the P300,000.00 loan as settled, or what is the proper extent of its liability under Article 20?

**Suggested Answer:** The bank cannot be compelled to treat the P300,000.00 principal loan as extinguished, but it is liable under Articles 19, 20 and 21 in relation to Article 1897 of the Civil Code to refund the P1,476.00 insurance premium with legal interest and to pay moral damages and attorney's fees.[^172] In [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_109937_1994.html), the Supreme Court held that when a lending bank acts as an insurance agent and accepts an application and premium from a borrower whom it knows exceeds the 60-year age limit without disclosing the limits of its agency authority under Article 1897, the non-disclosure amounts to a deception that brings Articles 19, 20 and 21 into play.[^173] However, the bank's liability cannot extend to the full value of the loan or policy because assuming that a 76-year-old borrower could have secured alternative coverage from another insurer before his death is speculative and unrecoverable under Article 2199, limiting recovery to reimbursement of the P1,476.00 premium with legal interest, moral damages under Article 2219 and attorney's fees under Article 2208(11).[^174]

### Problem 3: Employer Counterclaim Against a Labor Union for Illegal Strike Barricades

During a collective bargaining deadlock strike, unidentified strikers placed barricades across the main entrance gate of a manufacturing plant for a week, obstructing delivery trucks and trapping non-striking workers inside. The employer dismissed several union officers and filed a claim seeking to hold the labor union itself liable for actual, moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees under Articles 19 and 20 of the Civil Code, without presenting substantial evidence identifying which specific union officers or members erected the barricades or acted in bad faith. May the labor union be held liable for damages under Articles 19 and 20?

**Suggested Answer:** No, the labor union cannot be held liable for damages under Articles 19 and 20 of the Civil Code.[^175] In [*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519), the Supreme Court held that although Articles 19 and 20 provide the legal foundation for an employer to claim damages for extraordinary losses caused by illegal acts committed in bad faith during a strike, civil liability rests exclusively on the individual union officers or members who personally committed the unlawful acts.[^176] Because neither the Labor Code nor Articles 19 and 20 of the Civil Code adopt vicarious or collective union liability (unlike Article 2180 for quasi-delicts under Article 2176) and depleting union funds sourced from members' dues would penalize innocent workers and impair the right to self-organization, the employer's failure to prove by substantial evidence the individual participation and bad faith of specific officers or members requires the denial of the damage claim.[^177]

# Quotes

> "The foregoing provision provides the legal basis for the award of damages to a party who suffers damage whenever one commits an act in violation of some legal provision. This was incorporated by the Code Commission to provide relief to a person who suffers damage because another has violated some legal provision."[^178]

> "Whereas Article 19 provides for a cause of action for damages in cases when there is no law violated, the act causing damage being within rights or duties of defendant, Article 20 furnishes a general sanction for violations of provisions of law which do not especially provide their own sanction. The complaint clearly alleges a violation of respondent Trazo's property rights with respect to her checking account. Article 429 of the Civil Code provides that the owner or lawful possessor of the thing has the right to exclude any person from the enjoyment and disposal thereof."[^179]

> "There is a common element under Articles 19 and 21, and that is, the act must be intentional. However, Article 20 does not distinguish: the act may be done either "willfully", or "negligently". The trial court as well as the respondent appellate court mistakenly lumped these three (3) articles together, and cited the same as the bases for the award of damages in the civil complaint filed against petitioners, thus:"[^180]

> "Article 20 concerns violations of existing law as basis for an injury. It allows recovery should the act have been willful or negligent. Willful may refer to the intention to do the act and the desire to achieve the outcome which is considered by the plaintiff in tort action as injurious. Negligence may refer to a situation where the act was consciously done but without intending the result which the plaintiff considers as injurious."[^181]

> "As previously discussed, Articles 19 and 20 of the Civil Code provide the foundation for employers to claim damages resulting from illegal acts committed during a strike. Article 20 explicitly states that "[e]very person who, contrary to law, willfully or negligently causes damage to another, shall indemnify the latter for the same." When read in conjunction with Article 19, only those union officers or employees who, in bad faith, commit illegal acts causing damage to others during a strike can be held liable for such damages. While Article 2180 of the Civil Code establishes who may be held vicariously liable for quasi-delicts under Article 2176, there is no corresponding provision addressing liability for acts governed by Articles 19 and 20 of the same Code."[^182]

> "Regardless, therefore, of whether or not a special law so provides, indemnification of the offended party may be had on account of the damage, loss or injury directly suffered as a consequence of the wrongful act of another. The indemnity which a person is sentenced to pay forms an integral part of the penalty imposed by law for the commission of a crime (Quemel v. Court of Appeals, 22 SCRA 44, citing Bagtas v. Director of Prisons, 84 Phil. 692). Every crime gives rise to a penal or criminal action for the punishment of the guilty party, and also to civil action for the restitution of the thing, repair of the damage, and indemnification for the losses. (United States v. Bernardo, 19 Phil. 265)."[^183]

# Tips

## Part 1: Diagnostic Checklist

> [!TIP]
> **Step-by-step analysis for an Article 20 claim or defense:**
> 1. **Identify the specific law or legal duty violated.** Determine whether the defendant breached a positive statute, an administrative regulation, a corporate by-law or a codal rule (such as Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 in [*Banal v. Tadeo, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/dec1987/gr_l-78911_1987.html), clinical laboratory statutes in [*Garcia, Jr. v. Salvador*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41343), Article 429 property rights in [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367), Article 1308 mutuality of contracts in [*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343), or Article 1897 agency limits in [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_109937_1994.html) and [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826)). If the defendant had no statutory or regulatory duty regarding the event, as with the voyage charterer in [*Caltex (Philippines), Inc. v. Sulpicio Lines, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/38239) or the passive financial sponsor in [*Abrogar v. Cosmos Bottling Co.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62921), Article 20 does not apply.
> 2. **Distinguish standalone statutory breach from an Article 19 abuse-of-rights theory.** When the act violates a positive statute or regulation, Article 20 applies whether the breach was willful or negligent ([*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html)). When no positive statute was violated and the plaintiff invokes Article 20 as the remedy for an abuse of rights under Article 19, the plaintiff must overcome the presumption of good faith and prove bad faith or malice ([*Mercado v. Ongpin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67004) and [*Diaz v. Encanto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61630)).
> 3. **Verify individual culpability rather than collective attribution.** In strike-related damage claims under Articles 19 and 20, verify whether substantial evidence identifies the specific union officers or members who personally committed the illegal acts in bad faith, because union funds and non-participating members are not vicariously liable ([*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519)).
> 4. **Match each head of damages to its specific codal anchor.** Actual damages require proof of pecuniary loss with a reasonable degree of certainty or else fail as speculative ([*Development Bank of the Philippines v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_109937_1994.html)) or convert into temperate damages when loss is certain but the exact amount is unreceipted ([*Comsavings Bank v. Spouses Capistrano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56165)). Moral damages under Article 2219 require bad faith or gross negligence amounting to willful injury ([*Sandejas v. Spouses Ignacio, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46434)) and are deleted when the defendant acted without malice ([*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343)) or when the plaintiff should have checked his own status ([*University of the East v. Jader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59575)).

## Part 2: Common Traps and Misconceptions

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 1: Lumping Articles 19, 20 and 21 together without checking intent and illegality.** Trial and appellate courts frequently cite Articles 19, 20 and 21 as a single undifferentiated block. As the Supreme Court warned in [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html) and [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826), Articles 19 and 21 require an intentional act in bad faith, whereas Article 20 requires an act contrary to law that may be either willful or negligent.
>
> **Trap 2: Assuming a special penal statute silent on civil indemnity bars private recovery.** Defense counsel often argue that offenses against public order such as Batas Pambansa Bilang 22 create no private civil liability. In [*Banal v. Tadeo, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/dec1987/gr_l-78911_1987.html), the Supreme Court rejected that argument and held that Article 20 supplies the civil indemnity whenever an unlawful act causes damage to an individual.
>
> **Trap 3: Treating any void marriage, acquitted criminal charge or administrative reversal as automatic proof of bad faith.** The invalidation of a bigamous marriage ([*Mercado v. Ongpin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67004)), the dismissal of a criminal complaint filed under a mistaken identity ([*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html)), or the partial payment of salaries withheld in a sabbatical dispute ([*Diaz v. Encanto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61630)) does not by itself authorize moral damages under Articles 19 and 20 when the actor acted in good faith. Conversely, dismissal at the preliminary investigation stage does not shield a complainant who deliberately fabricated criminal charges out of ill will ([*Sosmeña v. Bonafe*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66223)).
>
> **Trap 4: Confusing a pleading's omission of article numbers with failure to state a cause of action.** In [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367), the Supreme Court held that a complaint need only allege the ultimate facts showing an unlawful invasion of the plaintiff's property rights and need not cite Article 20 or Article 429 by number to survive a motion to dismiss.

## Part 3: What Worked and What Did Not in Real Cases

- **Pleading ultimate facts of property invasion or post-expiration commercial bad faith to defeat a motion to dismiss (Worked).** In [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367) and [*Tocoms Philippines, Inc. v. Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66168), plaintiffs whose complaints were dismissed by one lower court succeeded in reinstating their actions by detailing specific ultimate facts of unauthorized checking account debits and bad-faith treatment of an expiring distributorship that fell within Article 20 or the other human relations provisions.
- **Invoking regular civil court jurisdiction for tortious employer deductions or contractor claims lacking an employment nexus (Worked).** In [*Philippine Commercial International Bank v. Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61424) and [*Social Security System v. Ubaña*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61157), workers kept their damage suits in the Regional Trial Court by framing their causes of action around oppressive pre-investigation salary deductions without Labor Code prayers or around abuse of rights by a principal corporation with whom the contractor worker had no employment relationship.
- **Using an unperfected or over-age Mortgage Redemption Insurance deduction to cancel the principal mortgage loan (Did Not Work).** In [*Development Bank of the Philippines v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_109937_1994.html) and [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826), borrowers who argued that the bank's unauthorized collection of an insurance premium extinguished their principal loans failed to cancel the loans or foreclosure, although they recovered premium refunds, moral damages and attorney's fees under Articles 19, 20, 21 and 1897.
- **Suing a labor union collectively for strike blockade losses without proving individual participation (Did Not Work).** In [*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519), the employer's counterclaim against the union under Articles 19 and 20 failed entirely because union funds cannot be made to answer for individual misconduct and the record did not establish which specific union officers or members committed the blockade in bad faith.
- **Relying on a unilateral termination clause or a closed post office box while ignoring known contact details (Did Not Work).** In [*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343), [*Calatagan Golf Club, Inc. v. Clemente, Jr.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49070), [*Ardiente v. Spouses Pastorfide*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55955), [*Navarro-Banaria v. Banaria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66456) and [*Villanueva v. Rosqueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53481), defendants who stood on formal contractual, corporate, property, guardianship or administrative prerogatives while acting without notice or in bad faith were held liable for damages under the human relations provisions, whereas the lessor in [*CJH Development Corp. v. Aniceto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66462) prevailed because it gave repeated notices to vacate, waited for the denial of the preliminary injunction and followed the express inventory and storage procedure in the lease contract.
