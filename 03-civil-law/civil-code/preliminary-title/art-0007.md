> ART. 7. Laws are repealed only by subsequent ones, and their violation or non-observance shall not be excused by disuse, or custom or practice to the contrary.
>
> When the courts declare a law to be inconsistent with the Constitution, the former shall be void and the latter shall govern.
>
> Administrative or executive acts, orders and regulations shall be valid only when they are not contrary to the laws or the Constitution. (5a)[^civil-code]

# Explanation

## Scope and Three Rules of Article 7

Article 7 of the Civil Code establishes three rules that govern the hierarchy and continuity of Philippine legal norms. First, laws are repealed only by subsequent laws. Disuse, custom or practice to the contrary never excuses the violation or non-observance of a law. Second, when the courts declare a law inconsistent with the Constitution, the law is void and the Constitution governs. Third, administrative or executive acts, orders and regulations are valid only when they do not contravene the laws or the Constitution.[^civil-code]

## Only a Subsequent Law Repeals a Law

### Express and Implied Repeals

A later statute prevails over an earlier enactment when the two stand in irreconcilable conflict or when the subsequent law covers the whole subject matter of the former law. In [*Magno v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51590), the Supreme Court applied the first paragraph of Article 7 and the maxim *legis posteriores priores contrarias abrogant*.[^2] It held that Section 40 of Republic Act No. 7160 (effective January 1, 1992), which sets a two-year disqualification period for elective local candidates, repealed Section 12 of Batas Pambansa Blg. 881 (approved December 3, 1985), which set a five-year disqualification period.[^3] Section 534(f) of Republic Act No. 7160 contained a repealing clause for inconsistent provisions. Section 40 also specifically governed disqualifications for elective local positions and reflected clear legislative intent to shorten the period from five years to two years.[^4]

Repeal by implication is not favored. This rule applies with greater force to penal statutes, which are construed strictly against the State and liberally in favor of the accused. In [*People v. Purisima*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1978/nov1978/gr_42050_66_1978.html), the Supreme Court applied the first paragraph of Article 7 and rejected the State's contention that Presidential Decree No. 9(3) repealed Section 26 of Act No. 1780 and Manila Ordinance No. 3820 (as amended by Ordinance No. 3928).[^5] Presidential Decree No. 9(3) contained no repealing clause. Each measure carried distinct elements and penalties. All three enactments therefore continued to subsist side by side.[^6]

An invalid subsequent enactment cannot repeal an existing valid law. In [*Sinag v. Sangguniang Panlalawigan ng Batangas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69836), the Supreme Court declared Provincial Ordinance No. 002, series of 2009 void for failing the population requirement of the Local Government Code and the plebiscite required by the Constitution and that Code.[^7] Because Ordinance No. 2 was void, it could not repeal Ordinance No. 05, series of 1997. A valid ordinance may be amended or repealed only by another valid law.[^8]

Implied repeal can also occur between successive administrative regulations when a later regulation deletes a restrictive clause from an earlier one. In [*Fort Bonifacio Development Corp. v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49844), the Supreme Court held that Revenue Regulations No. 6-97 deleted the restrictive paragraph of Section 4.105-1 of Revenue Regulations No. 7-95 that limited transitional input tax credits to improvements on real property.[^9] Even without a specific repealing clause, deleting that paragraph created an irreconcilable inconsistency and effectively repealed that particular provision of Revenue Regulations No. 7-95.[^10]

### Issuances That Cannot Repeal a Statute

Administrative circulars, executive orders without legislative authority and presidential speeches cannot repeal a statute. In [*Palanca v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_106685_1994.html), the Supreme Court applied the first paragraph of Article 7 and ruled that Central Bank circulars liberalizing foreign exchange transactions did not repeal or amend Republic Act No. 529.[^11] A Central Bank circular cannot repeal a law because only a law can repeal another law.[^12]

In [*People v. Aquino*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/jul1991/gr_39274_1991.html), a trial judge dismissed a criminal prosecution for *kaingin* under Section 2751 of the Revised Administrative Code (as amended by Republic Act No. 3701) by relying on the spirit and meaning of presidential decrees and a proclamation drawn from an Independence Day speech of the President.[^13] Applying the first paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that a presidential speech announcing proposed policies is not a law. No spirit or meaning lacking statutory form can overturn an unrepealed penal statute. Absent a repealing law, a judge has no choice save to apply and enforce the statute.[^14]

In [*Mathay, Jr. v. Macalincag*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/dec1993/gr_97618_1993.html), the Supreme Court approved and adopted the dispositions of the Central Board of Assessment Appeals nullifying Schedules of Market Values prepared solely by individual Metro Manila assessors in violation of Section 9 of Presidential Decree No. 921.[^15] In its decision quoted by the Supreme Court, the Central Board of Assessment Appeals cited Article 7 of the New Civil Code to explain that Executive Order No. 392 could not repeal Presidential Decree No. 921 because a decree issued under martial law lawmaking powers is a legislative act that can be repealed only by another subsequent legislative act.[^16]

### Issuances With the Force of Law That May Repeal or Supersede Prior Rules

Not every presidential or judicial issuance lacks legislative or superseding effect. In [*People v. Gacott, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_116049_1995.html), a trial judge quashed a criminal case for violation of the Anti-Dummy Law. In denying reconsideration, he invoked Article 7 of the New Civil Code to claim that Letter of Implementation No. 2, Series of 1972 could not repeal Republic Act No. 1130.[^17] The Supreme Court annulled the quashal order and fined both the judge and defense counsel. Presidential Decrees such as Presidential Decree No. 1 and Letter of Implementation No. 2 issued under former President Marcos's martial law powers had the same force and effect as statutes enacted by Congress. Neither had been revised, revoked or repealed. Both remained operative under Section 3, Article XVII of the Constitution and validly abolished the Anti-Dummy Board.[^18]

Supreme Court circulars promulgated under constitutional rule-making power also have the force and effect of law on procedural matters. In [*First Lepanto Ceramics, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_110571_1994.html), the Supreme Court quoted [*Inciong v. De Guia*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/sep1987/am_r-249-rtj_1987.html) (which quoted the first paragraph of Article 7) and held that Supreme Court Circular No. 1-91 effectively repealed or superseded Article 82 of Executive Order No. 226 regarding where and in what manner appeals from Board of Investments decisions are filed.[^19] Circular No. 1-91 was issued under Section 5(5), Article VIII of the 1987 Constitution to implement Batas Pambansa Blg. 129. It changed only the procedural forum and appeal period without diminishing the substantive right to appeal.[^20]

## Disuse, Custom and Practice Do Not Excuse Non-Observance

### Passage of Time and Non-Implementation

A statute does not lose force merely because officials fail to implement it or tolerate violations over many years. In [*Sinag v. Sangguniang Panlalawigan ng Batangas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69836), Ordinance No. 5 abolishing Barangay San Rafael and merging it with Barangay Dacanlao remained unimplemented for years because of a status quo ante resolution and prolonged litigation.[^21] Applying Article 7, the Supreme Court held that the effectivity and validity of a law do not depend on actual implementation. Non-observance is not excused by disuse, custom or practice to the contrary.[^22]

In [*Municipality of Payao v. Municipality of Imelda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68647), a territorial dispute over Barangay Guintolan lasted more than thirty years because erroneous unofficial copies of Presidential Decree No. 1238 misprinted Barangay Guinobatan as Barangay Guintolan.[^23] Applying the first paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that no passage of time validates Payao's infringement of Presidential Decree No. 1239, which placed Barangay Guintolan under Imelda.[^24]

In [*De Gallego v. Land Authority*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/aug1981/gr_26848_1981.html), a landowner sought to cancel perpetual title restrictions on a Baclaran Estate lot by arguing that a prior Land Authority governor had not opposed a similar cancellation petition and that the neighborhood had turned commercial.[^25] Applying the first paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that encumbrances imposed under an administrative order having the force of law must remain until the law or the order is repealed, amended or modified. A wrong act cannot be cured by the commission of another wrong. Arguments about commercial development address legislative wisdom rather than judicial power.[^26]

### Administrative, Judicial and Domestic Practice

Long-standing administrative or judicial custom cannot override positive law or create a legal duty contrary to a statutory mandate. In [*Department of Education v. Rizal Teachers Kilusang Bayan for Credit, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65522), a private lending institution sought a writ of mandamus to compel the Department of Education to continue payroll deductions for teachers' loans based on past practice and estoppel.[^27] Applying the first sentence of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that continued practice in domestic legal matters does not rise to the level of a legal obligation. No practice, continued or otherwise, can establish a clear legal duty or right that compels an agency to violate its statutory mandate under Section 7 of Republic Act No. 9155 to protect teachers' welfare.[^28] The Supreme Court contrasted this domestic rule with customary international law, which requires both general state practice and *opinio juris*.[^29]

In [*Inciong v. De Guia*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/sep1987/am_r-249-rtj_1987.html), a trial judge stated that assigning cases without a raffle was very common in her station.[^30] The Supreme Court agreed with and adopted the report of the Investigating Justice, who cited Article 7 of the New Civil Code to stress that practice is not the law. A judge could not disregard the Supreme Court's circular on the raffle of cases until it was repealed or modified.[^31]

## Constitutional Supremacy and Judicial Review

### Legislative and Executive Acknowledgment of Judicial Review

The second and third paragraphs of Article 7 confirm constitutional supremacy and judicial review. In [*Francisco, Jr. v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47018), the Supreme Court traced judicial review to the courts' duty to settle actual controversies under the Constitution. Quoting all three paragraphs of Article 7, the Supreme Court adopted the observation of former Justice Vicente V. Mendoza that the executive and legislative branches effectively acknowledged the courts' power of judicial review in Article 7.[^32] Exercising that power, the Supreme Court declared Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the House Rules of Procedure in Impeachment Proceedings unconstitutional and held the second impeachment complaint against Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide, Jr. barred under Article XI, Section 3(5) of the Constitution.[^33]

Judicial review belongs not only to the Supreme Court. In [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815), the Supreme Court affirmed that Regional Trial Courts also have authority and jurisdiction to consider the constitutionality of statutes, executive orders, presidential decrees and other issuances when constitutionality is the *lis mota* of an actual controversy.[^34]

### Orthodox View of Unconstitutionality and Revival of Repealed Laws

Under the orthodox view expressed in the second paragraph of Article 7, an unconstitutional statute is void from the beginning. In [*Republic v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_79732_1993.html), the Supreme Court quoted Justice Isagani A. Cruz's treatise contrasting the orthodox view (which the treatise identified as expressed in Article 7) with the modern view.[^35] Under the strict orthodox view, an unconstitutional act is not a law. It confers no right, imposes no duty, affords no protection and creates no office. It is stricken from the statute books and binds all persons, not just the litigants.[^36] The Supreme Court noted that a law may nonetheless be valid in general and invalid only in retrospective application, or may leave interim effects or a *fait accompli* undisturbed. None of those exceptions applied where expropriation cases were still pending appeal when the controlling decree was declared unconstitutional.[^37]

The Supreme Court directly applied this general rule under Article 7 in [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815), [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914) and [*Macalintal v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69031). In [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815), the Supreme Court cited the second paragraph of Article 7 to hold that unconstitutional Letter of Instruction No. 1465 was void and inoperative, requiring refund of levies paid under it pursuant to Article 22 of the Civil Code.[^38] In [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914), the Supreme Court cited Article 7 (quoting its first paragraph) to hold that the three-month salary cap in Section 10 of Republic Act No. 8042 struck down in *Serrano v. Gallant Maritime Services, Inc.* (as cited in [*Yap*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914)) conferred no rights and could not limit an illegally dismissed seafarer's unexpired salaries.[^39] In [*Macalintal v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69031), the Supreme Court cited the second paragraph of Article 7 and added an important consequence: a law declared unconstitutional is deemed never to have existed and results in the revival of the prior law it repealed (Republic Act No. 11462).[^40]

## The Doctrine of Operative Fact

### Nature, Requisites and Scope of Executive and Judicial Acts

The doctrine of operative fact is an equitable exception to the general rule in Article 7 that a void law or administrative act produces no legal rights or duties. In [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257), the Supreme Court cited Article 7 as stating the general rule and explained (quoting *Serrano de Agbayani v. Philippine National Bank*, as cited in [*San Roque*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257)) that prior to judicial nullification, the actual existence of a statute or executive issuance is an operative fact whose consequences cannot always be erased without injustice.[^41] Two conditions must concur: (1) a legislative or executive measure must have been invalidated by the court and (2) the public or affected individuals must have relied on that measure in good faith before its invalidation.[^42]

The doctrine covers both legislative statutes and executive acts. In [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044), the Supreme Court cited Article 7 and clarified (quoting *Hacienda Luisita, Inc. v. Presidential Agrarian Reform Council*, as cited in [*Araullo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044)) that executive acts include both quasi-legislative issuances and quasi-judicial decisions or orders of the executive branch.[^43] Applying equity and fair play, the Supreme Court held that the operative fact doctrine applied to Disbursement Acceleration Program projects that could no longer be undone, such as roads, bridges, hospitals and classrooms, where undoing them would cause physical destruction and wastefulness.[^44]

The Supreme Court similarly applied the operative fact doctrine to an invalid administrative election resolution and an unconstitutional election postponement statute. In [*Partido Demokratiko Pilipino-Lakas ng Bayan (PDP-Laban) v. Commission on Elections En Banc*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68780), the Supreme Court declared COMELEC Resolution No. 10147 void under the third paragraph of Article 7 for extending the 30-day statutory deadline for Statements of Contributions and Expenditures.[^45] However, because candidates and political parties relied on the resolution in good faith and COMELEC had previously extended deadlines in 2010 and 2013 without challenge, the Supreme Court deemed statements filed on or before June 30, 2016 timely filed.[^46] In [*Macalintal v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69031), after declaring Republic Act No. 11935 unconstitutional, the Supreme Court applied operative fact so the Barangay and Sangguniang Kabataan Elections proceeded on the last Monday of October 2023 while incumbent officials continued in a hold-over capacity until successors were elected and qualified, with their terms deemed ended on December 31, 2022 under revived Republic Act No. 11462.[^47]

In [*Office of the Court Administrator v. Castañeda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2024/oct2024/am_rtj-12-2316_2024.html), the Supreme Court quoted [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257) (which quoted Article 7 and *Serrano de Agbayani v. Philippine National Bank*) and extended the operative fact doctrine by analogy, alongside the *de facto* officer doctrine, to judicial acts.[^48] It declared valid the orders and decisions rendered by a Regional Trial Court judge during her suspension from January 12, 2010 to October 9, 2012 because litigants had no knowledge of her suspension and relied on her apparent judicial authority in good faith.[^49]

### Strict Limits and When Operative Fact Does Not Apply

The operative fact doctrine is never automatic. It must be scrutinized with great care and applies only in extraordinary circumstances meeting stringent conditions. Decided cases mark seven limits:

1. **It never validates an unconstitutional or unlawful act.** In [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044)[^50] and [*Duterte v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70257)[^51], the Supreme Court stressed that the doctrine nullifies the void act and cannot legitimize noncompliance with rules, laws or the Constitution.
2. **Wrongdoers and authors of an invalid act cannot invoke it.** In [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044), the doctrine protected only beneficiaries of irreversible projects who relied in good faith. It did not apply to the authors, proponents and implementors of the Disbursement Acceleration Program unless proper tribunals made concrete findings of good faith in their favor.[^52] In [*Duterte v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70257), the Supreme Court cited Article 7 and stated that operative fact has doubtful applicability where the transmittal of the Articles of Impeachment was itself tainted by the House of Representatives' noncompliance with Article XI, Section 3 of the Constitution and its own impeachment rules.[^53]
3. **A mere unformalized administrative practice or lower-court ruling does not suffice.** In [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257), the Supreme Court held that an unformalized Bureau of Internal Revenue practice or decisions of the Court of Tax Appeals or Court of Appeals are neither laws nor executive issuances. Under Section 246 of the Tax Code, which strictly codifies operative fact in tax rulings, only a formalized rule or ruling of the Commissioner (such as BIR Ruling No. DA-489-03 from December 10, 2003 to October 6, 2010) protects taxpayers who rely on it in good faith.[^54]
4. **It cannot be raised for the first time on appeal or applied without party invocation.** In [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815)[^55] and [*Municipality of Tupi v. Faustino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586)[^56], the Supreme Court rejected operative fact in part because no party raised the doctrine in the lower courts (and in [*Tupi*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586), neither party raised it even before the Supreme Court).
5. **It does not apply where no inequity results or where applying it would cause injustice.** In [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815), allowing a private corporation to keep fertilizer levies collected under an unconstitutional decree would unjustly enrich it contrary to Article 22 of the Civil Code.[^57] In [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914), shielding employers under an unconstitutional salary cap would be iniquitous to overseas Filipino workers and let employers profit from illegal dismissal.[^58] In [*Philippine Coconut Producers Federation, Inc. (COCOFED) v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/23897), quoting [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914) and *Chavez v. National Housing Authority* (as cited in [*COCOFED*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/23897)), the Supreme Court refused to apply operative fact to validate private title to bank shares bought with public coconut levy funds because claimants held no legal right to those shares and applying the doctrine would serve injustice to the Government and the people.[^59]
6. **It does not apply where the effects can easily be undone.** In [*Municipality of Tupi v. Faustino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586), quoting [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257) and [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044), the Supreme Court held that an illegal P1,000.00 traffic fine collected under a void municipal ordinance was fungible money that could be refunded to the motorist who sued.[^60]
7. **Interim effects do not shield cases still pending appeal.** [*Republic v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_79732_1993.html) did not use the term operative fact. It recognized that interim effects or a *fait accompli* may survive a declaration of nullity. The expropriation cases there were still pending appeal when the valuation decree was invalidated, so no such exception barred applying the nullification.[^61]

## Administrative and Executive Acts Must Conform to the Law and Constitution

### Invalid Regulations That Expand, Restrict or Contradict Statutes

The third paragraph of Article 7 confines delegated rule-making strictly within the boundaries of the enabling statute and the Constitution. Administrative agencies may fill in operational details to enforce a law, but they cannot amend an act of Congress, enlarge or restrict statutory coverage or define crimes not punished by statute. In case of discrepancy between the basic law and an implementing rule or regulation, the basic law prevails.

The Supreme Court has directly applied the third paragraph of Article 7 across criminal, labor, tax, audit and election controversies:

- **Penal regulations exceeding the statute:** In [*People v. Maceren*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_32166_1977.html), Fisheries Administrative Order No. 84-1 penalized electro fishing in fresh water fisheries even though the old Fisheries Law did not expressly punish electro fishing. Applying the third paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court held the regulation invalid because declaring what constitutes a crime is exclusively legislative.[^62]
- **Procedural rules contradicting statutory execution:** In [*Juan v. Musñgi*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/oct1987/gr_l-76053_1987.html), Section 2, Rule XI of the National Labor Relations Commission Rules delayed execution of Commission decisions for ten calendar days from receipt, contrary to Article 223 of the Labor Code making those decisions immediately executory. Applying the third paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that the rule could not prevail over the Labor Code.[^63]
- **Tax regulations restricting statutory credits:** In [*Fort Bonifacio Development Corp. v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49844), Section 4.105-1 of Revenue Regulations No. 7-95 restricted real estate dealers' 8% transitional input tax credit under Section 105 of the Old National Internal Revenue Code to improvements on real property, contradicting Section 100's definition of goods as including real properties held for sale. Applying Article 7, the Supreme Court declared the restrictive portion a nullity.[^64]
- **Tax circulars expanding tax liability:** In [*Bureau of Internal Revenue v. First E-Bank Tower Condominium Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66002), Revenue Memorandum Circular No. 65-2012 subjected condominium association dues and assessments to value-added tax, income tax and withholding tax beyond statutory authority. Citing Article 7, the Supreme Court affirmed the trial court's invalidation of the circular.[^65]
- **Social legislation rules narrowing statutory beneficiaries:** In [*Bartolome v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/58177), Rule XV, Section 1(c)(1) of the Amended Rules on Employees' Compensation restricted the phrase dependent parents in Article 167(j) of the Labor Code to legitimate parents. Applying the third paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court struck down the restriction as unauthorized administrative legislation.[^66]
- **Administrative orders distorting statutory compensation rates:** In [*Abrenica v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653), Department of Health Administrative Order No. 2006-0011 fixed a flat monthly hazard pay rate contrary to the proportional minimum rates in Section 21 of Republic Act No. 7305. Applying the third paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court sustained the Commission on Audit's disallowance of disbursements based on that void issuance.[^67]
- **Election resolutions extending non-extendible statutory deadlines:** In [*Partido Demokratiko Pilipino-Lakas ng Bayan (PDP-Laban) v. Commission on Elections En Banc*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68780), COMELEC Resolution No. 10147 extended the 30-day statutory deadline in Section 14 of Republic Act No. 7166 for filing Statements of Contributions and Expenditures. Applying the third paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court declared the resolution void.[^68]

### Valid Administrative Amendments and Remedies for Invalid Exactions

An administrative issuance is valid when it is issued under statutory authority and does not contradict the law or the Constitution. In [*Pagpalain Haulers, Inc. v. Trajano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37312), an employer quoted Article 8 and the third paragraph of Article 7 in challenging Department of Labor and Employment Department Order No. 9, Series of 1997, which dispensed with submitting books of account to register a local union chapter.[^69] The Supreme Court dismissed the petition. Department Order No. 9 was issued under Article 5 of the Labor Code. The Labor Code itself did not require submitting books of account for registration. Earlier Supreme Court rulings requiring books of account (*Progressive Development Corp. v. Secretary of Labor* and *Protection Technology, Inc. v. Secretary of Labor*, as cited in [*Pagpalain Haulers*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37312)) merely interpreted the prior implementing rules, which the Secretary of Labor had authority to amend.[^70]

Invalidating an administrative act or local ordinance also carries remedial limits:

- **Proof of judicial consignation:** In [*Bureau of Internal Revenue v. First E-Bank Tower Condominium Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66002), although Revenue Memorandum Circular No. 65-2012 was void under Article 7, the Supreme Court declined to order direct release of consignated tax payments because compliance with the factual requisites of judicial consignation had to be proven by motion before the trial court after finality.[^71]
- **Equitable excuse from audit refund:** In [*Abrenica v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653), while the Supreme Court sustained the disallowance of hazard pay granted under void Department of Health Administrative Order No. 2006-0011, it excused the recipient health workers from refunding the amounts they individually received on equitable and social justice grounds under *Madera* (as cited in [*Abrenica*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653)).[^72]
- **Refunds limited to parties before the court:** In [*Municipality of Tupi v. Faustino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586), the Supreme Court affirmed the refund of the P1,000.00 fine paid by the motorist who sued under void Municipal Ordinance No. 688, but deleted the trial court's order to refund fines paid by other non-party motorists who were not joined in the action.[^73]

## Case Discussions

### Operative Fact Inapplicable to Tainted Impeachment Transmittal

[*Duterte v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70257) resolved the motion for reconsideration of the July 25, 2025 Decision on the impeachment of Vice President Sara Z. Duterte. Three verified complaints were filed against her on December 2, 4 and 19, 2024 under the first mode in Article XI, Section 3(2) of the Constitution. They were referred to the Speaker only on February 5, 2025. On that date 215 House members filed a fourth complaint under the second mode in Article XI, Section 3(4). The House transmitted the Articles of Impeachment to the Senate and sent the first three complaints to the Archives. On February 18, 2025, Vice President Duterte and Atty. Israelito P. Torreon et al. filed petitions for certiorari and prohibition. On July 25, 2025, the Supreme Court partially granted the petitions and declared the Articles of Impeachment unconstitutional and *void ab initio* for violating the one-year bar under Article XI, Section 3(5) and due process. Respondent House of Representatives, through the Office of the Solicitor General, moved for reconsideration and asked for prospective application under the operative fact doctrine.[^74]

The Supreme Court En Banc cited Article 7 of the Civil Code as stating the general rule for the applicability of laws and administrative acts and quoted all three paragraphs. It held that the operative fact doctrine is rooted in equity and fair play, applies only in extraordinary circumstances where consequences can no longer be undone, cannot legitimize noncompliance with rules, laws or the Constitution and cannot be invoked by a party directly responsible for an unconstitutional act. The Supreme Court stated that the doctrine had doubtful applicability here because the House failed to comply with Article XI, Section 3 and its own House Rules on Impeachment, tainting the transmittal to the Senate. The Supreme Court denied the Motion for Reconsideration with finality, noted eight motions and oppositions, declared the Resolution immediately executory, prohibited further pleadings and ordered immediate entry of judgment. It underscored that the July 25, 2025 Decision did not absolve Vice President Duterte and that the grounds may again be raised at the House's discretion based on any evidence that may have been discovered, if any. This resolution shows that a party whose own breach tainted the act cannot use operative fact to escape the general rule of invalidity under Article 7.[^75]

### Non-Implementation Does Not Nullify an Ordinance

[*Sinag v. Sangguniang Panlalawigan ng Batangas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69836) grew out of a dispute over PHP 41,435,765.19 in real property tax shares claimed by Barangay San Rafael and Barangay Dacanlao in Calaca, Batangas. On June 23, 1997, the Sangguniang Panlalawigan of Batangas enacted Ordinance No. 05, series of 1997 abolishing Barangay San Rafael and merging it with Barangay Dacanlao. Voters ratified the merger in a February 28, 1998 plebiscite. In G.R. No. 132603, the Supreme Court had issued a March 10, 1998 status quo resolution. Its September 18, 2000 Decision deferred execution of the plebiscite result until the outcome of Civil Case No. 3442. Branch 9 of the Regional Trial Court of Balayan, Batangas upheld Ordinance No. 5 on November 15, 2006. While the appeal was pending, the Sanggunian enacted Provincial Ordinance No. 002, series of 2009 repealing Ordinance No. 5 without a plebiscite. Former officials and residents of Barangay Dacanlao sued to nullify Ordinance No. 2 in Civil Case No. 4877. In its October 14, 2013 Order, Branch 9 granted their motion for judgment on the pleadings but dismissed the petition because Ordinance No. 5 had never been implemented. The Court of Appeals dismissed their Rule 41 appeal in CA-G.R. CV No. 104369 as an improper remedy because it saw only questions of law.[^76]

The Supreme Court En Banc held that the appeal raised questions of fact, so Rule 41 was the proper mode. Instead of remanding the case, it resolved the merits. Applying the first paragraph of Article 7, it held that the effectivity and validity of a law do not depend on actual implementation because violation or non-observance is not excused by disuse, custom or practice to the contrary. Although Barangay San Rafael could not be faulted for operating during the status quo ante order, Ordinance No. 2 effectively divided Barangay Dacanlao and failed both the 2,000-inhabitant requirement under Section 386 of the Local Government Code (as the NSO certified only 611 inhabitants as of August 1, 2007) and the mandatory plebiscite requirement. Petitioners did not prove or quote the Sanggunian's internal rules on the three-reading rule. The Supreme Court found it unnecessary to resolve that point because Ordinance No. 2 was already void for violating constitutional and statutory requisites. A void ordinance could not repeal Ordinance No. 5. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals October 26, 2016 Decision and August 16, 2017 Resolution and declared Provincial Ordinance No. 002, series of 2009 void. This decision confirms that non-implementation does not extinguish a valid law and a void enactment cannot repeal it.[^77]

### Analogous Extension of Operative Fact to Judicial Acts

[*Office of the Court Administrator v. Castañeda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2024/oct2024/am_rtj-12-2316_2024.html) answered letters from Spouses Arvin John and Maria Olympia Bailey and Atty. Ana Murray Y. Santillan. The Supreme Court En Banc referred the letters to the Office of the Court Administrator on July 12, 2022. They asked whether decisions issued by Judge Liberty O. Castañeda of Branch 67, Regional Trial Court of Paniqui, Tarlac during her suspension were valid. She was suspended from January 12, 2010 until her dismissal on October 9, 2012. She rendered decisions from March 23, 2010 to June 3, 2011 and adjudicated 1,237 cases in that period. In Civil Case No. 666-09, she had declared the Spouses Bailey's marriage null and void on September 21, 2010.[^78]

Quoting [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257) (which quoted Article 7 of the Civil Code and *Serrano de Agbayani v. Philippine National Bank*), the Supreme Court En Banc explained that although void acts generally produce no legal rights or duties, the operative fact doctrine protects those who relied in good faith on an invalid act prior to its nullification. While the doctrine traditionally applies to legislative or executive acts, the Supreme Court extended its equitable principles by analogy, together with the *de facto* officer doctrine, to judicial acts performed under color of authority by a suspended judge when litigants had no knowledge of her suspension and relied on her decisions in good faith. The Supreme Court declared valid the orders and decisions rendered by Judge Castañeda acting as a *de facto* officer during her suspension from January 12, 2010 to October 9, 2012 and directed that copies be furnished to the parties and entered into the records of the covered cases. This resolution shows that equitable protection for good-faith litigants can extend by analogy beyond legislative and executive measures to judicial acts.[^79]

### Unconstitutional Postponement Law, Revival and Operative Fact

In [*Macalintal v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69031), Atty. Romulo B. Macalintal (G.R. No. 263590) filed a petition for certiorari and prohibition. Atty. Alberto N. Hidalgo et al. (G.R. No. 263673) filed a petition for certiorari, prohibition and mandamus. Both assailed Republic Act No. 11935, approved on October 10, 2022, which postponed the December 5, 2022 Barangay and Sangguniang Kabataan Elections to the last Monday of October 2023. The Supreme Court observed that the law, as worded, gave no supporting reasons for the postponement.[^80]

The Supreme Court En Banc declared Republic Act No. 11935 unconstitutional for violating substantive due process, infringing the right of suffrage and being enacted in patent grave abuse of discretion. Citing the second paragraph of Article 7 (and quoting the first and second paragraphs), it held that an unconstitutional statute is a total nullity that produces no rights, imposes no duties and revives the law it repealed (Republic Act No. 11462). Applying the operative fact doctrine as an exception grounded in equity and practicality, however, the Supreme Court recognized that the December 5, 2022 date had already lapsed. It ruled that the declaration retroacts to the date of enactment, subject to recognition of the law's prior effects. The October 2023 elections would proceed as scheduled. Incumbent officials would remain in a hold-over capacity until their successors were elected and qualified. Their term was deemed ended on December 31, 2022 under Republic Act No. 11462. The next synchronized elections would be held on the first Monday of December 2025 and every three years thereafter. Congress remained free to amend Republic Act No. 9164 subject to the decision's guidelines. The Supreme Court granted the consolidated petitions and declared Republic Act No. 11935 unconstitutional. This decision confirms that declaring a repealing statute unconstitutional revives the prior statute unless equity requires leaving interim effects undisturbed.[^81]

### Void Extension of Statutory Election Deadline

[*Partido Demokratiko Pilipino-Lakas ng Bayan (PDP-Laban) v. Commission on Elections En Banc*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68780) involved Section 14 of Republic Act No. 7166, which requires candidates and political parties to file their Statements of Contributions and Expenditures within 30 days after election day. For the May 9, 2016 elections, COMELEC Resolution No. 9991 of October 2, 2015 fixed June 8, 2016 as the final and non-extendible deadline. On June 23, 2016, COMELEC Resolution No. 10147 extended the deadline to June 30, 2016. PDP-Laban filed a Petition for Certiorari on July 7, 2016. Leon Estrella Peralta, Melchor Gruela Magdamo and Othello Estropigan Dalanon intervened as taxpayers.[^82]

The Supreme Court En Banc applied the third paragraph of Article 7 and held that COMELEC gravely abused its discretion by extending a mandatory statutory deadline. Because Resolution No. 10147 contravened Section 14 of Republic Act No. 7166, it was void and could not be a source of legal rights. Quoting *Serrano de Agbayani v. Philippine National Bank* (as cited in [*PDP-Laban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68780)), the Supreme Court nevertheless applied the operative fact doctrine because candidates and political parties relied on Resolution No. 10147 in good faith and COMELEC had previously extended deadlines in 2010 and 2013 without challenge. It reserved the constitutionality of Section 14 (not being the *lis mota*) and declined to rule on the commissioners' liability for betrayal of public trust, if any, because impeachment must be instituted in the proper forum. The Supreme Court declared COMELEC Resolution No. 10147 void for being repugnant to the law and deemed Statements of Contributions and Expenditures for the May 9, 2016 elections submitted on or before June 30, 2016 timely filed. This decision illustrates both the invalidity of an administrative rule contradicting a statute under paragraph 3 and the good-faith application of operative fact.[^83]

### Disallowance Under Void Department Order and Equitable Excuse

[*Abrenica v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653) involved San Lazaro Hospital health workers with Salary Grades 20 to 26. From January to June 2009 they received hazard pay fixed at P4,989.75 a month under Department of Health Administrative Order No. 2006-0011. Notice of Disallowance No. 09-006-101(MDS)-(09) dated November 23, 2009 disallowed the excess over the 5% minimum rate of monthly basic salary under Section 21 of Republic Act No. 7305. The Commission on Audit relied on an earlier Supreme Court ruling (*A.M. No. 03-9-02-SC*, as cited in [*Abrenica*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653)) that found the fixed rate in the administrative order void. The National Government Section affirmed the disallowance in Decision No. 2010-003 and ordered refund. The Commission Proper affirmed in Decision No. 2014-158 dated August 15, 2014 and later dismissed the motion for reconsideration as filed out of time. Petitioners filed a Rule 64 in relation to Rule 65 Petition for Certiorari.[^84]

The Supreme Court En Banc held that the motion for reconsideration was timely but that the petition itself was filed out of time. It still resolved the merits because the motion had been wrongly dismissed, the health workers appeared entitled to hazard pay and equity called for it. The Supreme Court cited Article 7 (quoting all three paragraphs) and applied its third paragraph. An administrative rule or regulation is valid only if it conforms to the enabling statute. In case of discrepancy, the basic law prevails because an agency cannot amend an act of Congress. The Commission on Audit acted within its constitutional mandate in disallowing disbursements made under a void Department of Health issuance. Later issuances recognizing the administrative order did not help because practice, no matter how long continued, cannot create a vested right contrary to law. On the refund liability, however, the Supreme Court held that the Commission on Audit committed grave abuse of discretion in holding the recipients liable on the ground of bad faith, because their good or bad faith is inconsequential under *Madera* (as cited in [*Abrenica*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653)). Because petitioners were legally entitled to hazard pay for hazardous work and only the Department of Health's irregular implementation caused the disallowance, ordering refund would cause social injustice and undue prejudice. The Supreme Court partially granted the petition and affirmed Commission on Audit Proper Decision No. 2014-158 dated August 15, 2014 with modification that petitioners are no longer required to refund the disallowed amounts they individually received. This decision shows that a void administrative order supports a disallowance under Article 7 even when equity excuses innocent recipients from refunding.[^85]

### Thirty Years of Non-Observance Does Not Validate Infringement

[*Municipality of Payao v. Municipality of Imelda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68647) involved two adjacent municipalities, now in Zamboanga Sibugay, created in 1977 under Presidential Decree Nos. 1238 and 1239. A territorial dispute over Barangay Guintolan lasted more than thirty years because unofficial copies of Presidential Decree No. 1238 erroneously listed Barangay Guintolan instead of Barangay Guinobatan. After two earlier proceedings, Branch 31 of the Regional Trial Court of Imelda, Zamboanga Sibugay (Special Civil Case No. IM-3 (I-115)) granted Imelda's Petition for Mandamus and ordered Payao to cease and desist from exercising jurisdiction over Barangay Guintolan. The Twenty-Second Division of the Court of Appeals affirmed in CA-G.R. SP No. 07137-MIN and held that *res judicata* did not apply.[^86]

The Supreme Court Third Division affirmed that mandamus was proper and *res judicata* did not bar the suit because the earlier proceedings involved different causes of action and relied on an erroneous copy of Presidential Decree No. 1238. Applying the first paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that despite the lapse of more than thirty years, a law's violation or non-observance is not excused by disuse, custom or practice to the contrary. No passage of time could validate Payao's infringement of Presidential Decree No. 1239. The Supreme Court also reminded the bench, bar and public that laws are evidenced by official publication in the Official Gazette or attested custodian copies. It denied the Petition for Review on Certiorari, affirmed the Court of Appeals February 28, 2018 Decision and September 21, 2018 Resolution with modification and ordered Payao, its agents or persons under its control or authority to desist from performing any and all acts that prevent Imelda from exercising supervisory authority over Barangay Guintolan. This decision confirms that decades of contrary practice cannot override a statute's clear mandate.[^87]

### Void Tax Circular and Recovery of Consignated Funds

In [*Bureau of Internal Revenue v. First E-Bank Tower Condominium Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66002), the condominium corporation filed a petition for declaratory relief in Branch 146 of the Regional Trial Court of Makati City (Special Civil Action No. 12-1236) challenging Bureau of Internal Revenue Memorandum Circular No. 65-2012, which subjected condominium association dues, membership fees and assessments to 12% value-added tax, 32% income tax and withholding tax. The trial court declared the circular invalid in its September 5, 2013 Resolution. In its December 18, 2013 Order it denied both motions for reconsideration. It noted that the funds were consignated only on November 17, 2013 but allowed First E-Bank to move for their withdrawal upon finality. The Court of Appeals dismissed both parties' appeals in CA-G.R. CV No. 102266 for alleged lack of jurisdiction, pointing to the Court of Tax Appeals. Both the Bureau of Internal Revenue (G.R. No. 215801) and First E-Bank (G.R. No. 218924) elevated the case to the Supreme Court.[^88]

The Supreme Court First Division reversed the Court of Appeals' jurisdictional dismissal. While the case was pending below, *British American Tobacco v. Camacho* (as cited in [*First E-Bank*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66002)) held that the Court of Tax Appeals had no jurisdiction over the validity of tax rules, so the parties correctly appealed to the Court of Appeals. The Supreme Court then resolved the merits and held that a condominium corporation is not engaged in trade or business when it collects dues for the upkeep of common areas. It held that the Commissioner of Internal Revenue may interpret tax laws under Section 4 of the National Internal Revenue Code but cannot override, supplant, expand or modify them. Issuing Revenue Memorandum Circular No. 65-2012 constituted grave abuse of discretion and was invalid. Citing Article 7 (quoting all three paragraphs) and applying its rule against void administrative acts, the Supreme Court confirmed that a void administrative act cannot be the source of legal rights or duties and taxes paid under invalid measures may be recovered in proper proceedings. Because compliance with the five requisites of judicial consignation is a factual question, however, First E-Bank could initiate the proper motion for release before the trial court only after finality and proof of compliance. The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals June 26, 2014 and November 27, 2014 Resolutions, denied both petitions and affirmed the Regional Trial Court September 5, 2013 Resolution and December 18, 2013 Order. This decision underscores that interpretive tax circulars cannot create tax burdens absent clear statutory authority under Article 7.[^89]

### Refund of Illegal Fine Without Operative Fact Bar

In [*Municipality of Tupi v. Faustino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586), the Sangguniang Bayan of Tupi, South Cotabato enacted Ordinance No. 688, Series of 2014 fixing speed limits along a national highway. On October 6, 2014, Atty. Herminio B. Faustino was flagged down for driving at 70 kph where the limit was 40 kph and was fined P1,000.00. Two days later he filed a petition for declaratory relief, annulment of the ordinance and damages before Branch 39 of the Regional Trial Court of Polomolok, South Cotabato (Special Civil Action No. 104-14). In its January 20, 2016 Decision, the trial court declared the ordinance invalid and *void ab initio*, permanently enjoined its enforcement and ordered refund of all fines collected. Its May 15, 2017 Omnibus Order denied both motions for reconsideration. The Municipality of Tupi filed a Rule 45 petition on a pure question of law.[^90]

The Supreme Court En Banc held that declaratory relief was not the proper remedy because the ordinance had already been enforced against Faustino. The proper remedy was certiorari and prohibition. The ordinance never took effect because it was not published as the Local Government Code requires. It also contravened Sections 35, 36 and 38 of Republic Act No. 4136 because the municipality did not first classify its highways, post signs and secure Land Transportation Office approval. The Supreme Court upheld the refund of Faustino's P1,000.00 fine as an incidental relief, but held that the trial court erred in ordering refunds to other motorists who were not joined as parties and whom Faustino did not represent. Quoting [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257) (which quoted Article 7 of the Civil Code and *Serrano de Agbayani v. Philippine National Bank*) and [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044), the Supreme Court held that the operative fact doctrine did not bar refunding Faustino's P1,000.00 fine for two reasons. First, no party raised the doctrine before the trial court or the Supreme Court, precluding its consideration under Section 8, Rule 51 in relation to Section 4, Rule 56 of the Rules of Court. Second, there was no good-faith public reliance, the fungible fine could be restored and denying refund of an illegal exaction paid under protest would be iniquitous. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Regional Trial Court January 20, 2016 Decision and May 15, 2017 Omnibus Order with modification and deleted the order to refund fines collected from motorists other than Faustino. This decision confirms that operative fact does not shield refundable illegal exactions and was never meant to operate when unraised by the parties.[^91]

### Domestic Practice Does Not Create a Legal Obligation

In [*Department of Education v. Rizal Teachers Kilusang Bayan for Credit, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65522), teachers complained of unauthorized and excessive loan deductions by a private lender, Rizal Teachers Kilusang Bayan for Credit, Inc. (RTKBCI). By Memorandum dated July 4, 2001, the Department of Education held the remittance of collections for February to June 2001 and suspended the salary deduction scheme for RTKBCI pending resolution of the complaints. Branch 19 of the Regional Trial Court of Manila (Civil Case No. 01-102346) granted RTKBCI's Complaint for Mandamus and Damages, ordering the Department of Education to release P111,989,006.98, continue payroll deductions and pay P5,000,000.00 actual damages and P500,000.00 attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed in CA-G.R. SP No. 106515 with modification deleting the actual damages award.[^92]

The Supreme Court Second Division reversed. It held that estoppel under Article 1432 of the Civil Code cannot contravene a clear statute or public policy and RTKBCI failed to prove the elements of estoppel. Applying the first sentence of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that continued practice in domestic legal matters does not rise to the level of a legal obligation. No practice, continued or otherwise, could create a clear legal duty or right compelling the Department of Education to violate its statutory mandate under Section 7 of Republic Act No. 9155 and its *parens patriae* duty to protect teachers' welfare. Nor did RTKBCI prove general and consistent practice under *opinio juris*. Section 36 of Republic Act No. 8760 (the 2000 General Appropriations Act) also automatically lapsed at the end of that fiscal year. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals May 30, 2012 Decision and dismissed the Complaint for Mandamus and Damages in Civil Case No. 01-102346. This decision establishes that long-standing administrative accommodation cannot ripen into a mandatory obligation contrary to statute under Article 7.[^93]

### Unauthorized Administrative Restriction of Statutory Beneficiaries

In [*Bartolome v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/58177), John Colcol was an unmarried and childless electrician on board the vessel Maersk Danville. His great-grandfather Cornelio Colcol had adopted him in 1985 and died in 1987 while John was still a minor. John died in an accident on June 2, 2008. His biological mother Bernardina P. Bartolome claimed death benefits under Presidential Decree No. 626. Both the Social Security System La Union branch and the Employees' Compensation Commission (ECC Case No. SL-18483-0218-10) denied the claim based on Rule XV, Section 1(c)(1) of the Amended Rules on Employees' Compensation, which limited the phrase dependent parents in Article 167(j) of the Labor Code to legitimate parents. Bartolome appealed directly under Rule 43.[^94]

The Supreme Court Third Division quoted all three paragraphs of Article 7 and held that the Employees' Compensation Commission engaged in unauthorized administrative legislation. Rule-making power is confined to carrying out the enacted law and cannot amend, expand or restrict statutory terms. When a discrepancy arises, the basic law prevails. Because Article 167(j) of the Labor Code did not qualify dependent parents as legitimate parents, the Commission's restriction was invalid. The Supreme Court also struck down the word illegitimate in Rule XV, Section 1(c)(1) as unconstitutional for violating equal protection. Moreover, the adoptive parent's death during John's minority restored Bartolome's parental authority and documentary evidence of shared residence and John's Social Security System beneficiary designation established her dependency. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Employees' Compensation Commission March 17, 2010 Decision and directed the Commission to release the secondary-beneficiary benefits of deceased covered employee John Colcol to Bartolome, with no costs. This decision demonstrates that implementing rules cannot insert restrictions absent from the statute under Article 7.[^95]

### Unconstitutional Executive Program and Limits of Operative Fact

In [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044), consolidated petitions assailed the constitutionality of the Disbursement Acceleration Program (DAP), Department of Budget and Management National Budget Circular No. 541 and related executive issuances. Under the DAP, the Executive withdrew unobligated agency allotments and declared unreleased appropriations as savings prior to fiscal year-end, transferred savings across branches and funded projects lacking General Appropriations Act cover.[^96]

The Supreme Court En Banc cited Article 7 (quoting all three paragraphs) and stated that a legislative or executive act declared unconstitutional cannot give rise to any right or obligation. Because rigidly undoing all completed public infrastructure projects (such as roads, bridges, hospitals and classrooms) would cause physical destruction and wastefulness, however, the Supreme Court applied the operative fact doctrine. It clarified (quoting *Hacienda Luisita, Inc. v. Presidential Agrarian Reform Council*, as cited in [*Araullo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044)) that executive acts include both quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial executive acts, unlike mere unformalized administrative practices. Crucially, the doctrine applies only in extraordinary circumstances meeting stringent conditions, never validates an unconstitutional act, covers only projects that can no longer be undone whose beneficiaries relied in good faith and cannot protect DAP authors, proponents and implementors absent concrete findings of good faith by proper tribunals determining criminal, civil, administrative or other liabilities. The Supreme Court partially granted the petitions, declared the three specified DAP acts and practices unconstitutional under Article VI, Section 25(5) and separation of powers and declared void the use of unprogrammed funds without National Treasurer certification that revenue collections exceeded revenue targets. This landmark decision defines both the reach and the strict personal limits of operative fact under Article 7.[^97]

### Operative Fact Requires a Formal Issuance, Not Mere Practice

[*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257) resolved motions for reconsideration of the Supreme Court's February 12, 2013 Decision. San Roque Power Corporation (G.R. No. 187485) argued that alleged Bureau of Internal Revenue and Court of Tax Appeals practice excusing the 120+30-day waiting periods under Section 112 of the National Internal Revenue Code was an operative fact warranting prospective application. In G.R. No. 196113, the Commissioner of Internal Revenue argued that Taganito Mining Corporation could not rely on BIR Ruling No. DA-489-03 because a Deputy Commissioner issued it.[^98]

The Supreme Court En Banc cited Article 7 (quoting all three paragraphs) as enunciating the general rule that a void law or administrative act cannot be the source of legal rights or duties, with operative fact as a narrow exception. It held that operative fact requires an invalidated legislative or executive measure relied upon in good faith. A mere administrative practice not formalized into a rule or ruling is neither a law nor an executive issuance and cannot trigger operative fact. Likewise, Court of Tax Appeals or Court of Appeals rulings are not executive issuances and have no value as precedents. Under *verba legis*, Section 112's 120+30-day periods are mandatory and jurisdictional. Moreover, Section 246 of the Tax Code specifically incorporates operative fact in taxation and leaves no room for broader invocation. Only taxpayers who relied in good faith on BIR Ruling No. DA-489-03 (validly issued under delegated power pursuant to Section 7 of the Tax Code) between December 10, 2003 and October 6, 2010 (when *Aichi*, as cited in [*San Roque*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257), reversed it) were protected. The Supreme Court denied both Motions for Reconsideration with finality. This resolution draws the line between a formal executive issuance and an unformalized practice under Article 7.[^99]

### Operative Fact Inapplicable to Unconstitutional Coconut Levy Distribution

[*Philippine Coconut Producers Federation, Inc. (COCOFED) v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/23897) reviewed rulings in the ill-gotten wealth cases Civil Case Nos. 0033-A and 0033-F. There the Sandiganbayan rendered Partial Summary Judgments declaring unconstitutional portions of Presidential Decree Nos. 755, 961 and 1468, invalidating Philippine Coconut Authority implementing rules (Administrative Order No. 1, Series of 1975 and Resolution No. 074-78) and declaring UCPB shares, CIIF companies and San Miguel Corporation shares owned by the Republic. Petitioners COCOFED et al. (G.R. Nos. 177857-58) and Danilo S. Ursua (G.R. No. 178193) appealed under Rule 45 and invoked the operative fact doctrine to protect alleged farmer ownership of UCPB shares.[^100]

Quoting [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914) (which quoted Article 7 of the Civil Code) and *Chavez v. National Housing Authority* (as cited in [*COCOFED*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/23897)), which quoted *De Agbayani* and the second and third paragraphs of Article 7, the Supreme Court En Banc held that operative fact did not apply. Claimants held no legal right to UCPB shares because their receipts under Republic Act No. 6260 pertained to the Coconut Investment Fund (Coconut Investment Company), a distinct entity from UCPB funded by the Coconut Consumers Stabilization Fund under Presidential Decree No. 276. Applying operative fact to validate haphazard private distribution of public funds under constitutionally infirm decrees would be iniquitous and serve injustice to the Government, the coconut industry and the people. The Supreme Court denied the petitions, affirmed the Sandiganbayan Partial Summary Judgments and May/June 2007 Resolutions with modification (declaring the 64.98% UCPB shares owned by the Republic and the 6 CIIF oil mills, 14 CIIF holding companies and 33,133,266 San Miguel Corporation shares with all dividends and increments owned by and ordered reconveyed to the Government for the benefit of all coconut farmers and the coconut industry) and reserved Eduardo M. Cojuangco, Jr.'s separate petition in G.R. No. 180705 for separate adjudication. This decision confirms that operative fact cannot vest private ownership over public funds distributed under unconstitutional decrees.[^101]

### Operative Fact Cannot Preserve an Unconstitutional Salary Cap

In [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914), seafarer Claudio S. Yap, an electrician on M/T SEASCOUT, was constructively and illegally dismissed with nine months remaining on his 12-month contract at US$1,430.00 per month (including a US$130.00 tanker allowance). The Labor Arbiter awarded US$12,870.00 (nine months' salary), P100,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and 10% attorney's fees. The National Labor Relations Commission initially reduced salary to three months (US$4,290.00) then restored nine months on partial reconsideration. The Court of Appeals reduced the salary award back to three months under the fifth paragraph of Section 10 of Republic Act No. 8042. While Yap's Rule 45 petition was pending, *Serrano v. Gallant Maritime Services, Inc.* (as cited in [*Yap*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914)) struck down the three-month cap as unconstitutional. Respondents argued *Serrano* should not apply retroactively. They also belatedly claimed that Yap's basic salary was only US$1,300.00 excluding the US$130.00 tanker allowance (making three months US$3,900.00 rather than US$4,290.00). They further manifested that P878,914.47 was garnished from Intermare's bank account and deposited with the National Labor Relations Commission Cashier of Tacloban City on February 14, 2007 and that on February 16, 2007 the Labor Arbiter ordered release of P781,870.03 to Yap, P86,744.44 to his former lawyer as attorney's fees and P3,570.00 as execution and deposit fees, prompting them to pray that Yap return US$8,970.00 or its peso equivalent.[^102]

The Supreme Court Second Division cited Article 7 of the Civil Code (quoting its first paragraph) to support the general rule that an unconstitutional act is not a law, confers no rights, imposes no duties and is inoperative as if never passed. It refused to apply the operative fact exception because Yap was not at fault for his illegal dismissal. Shielding employers under an unconstitutional cap would be iniquitous to overseas Filipino workers and would send a wrong signal that employers may violate security of tenure and profit from an unconstitutional law. The Supreme Court also rejected respondents' belated challenge to the tanker allowance because it was raised for the first time in their Supreme Court Memorandum and formed part of the contractual basic salary. It granted the petition, modified the Court of Appeals February 28, 2007 Decision and August 30, 2007 Resolution by awarding Yap his salaries for the entire nine-month unexpired portion at US$1,430.00 per month and affirmed all other awards, with no costs. This decision shows that equity under operative fact cannot be invoked by an erring employer to defeat a worker's full statutory recovery.[^103]

### Revenue Regulation Nullified and Partially Repealed by Deletion

[*Fort Bonifacio Development Corp. v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49844) resolved a motion for reconsideration of the April 2, 2009 Decision. That Decision had reversed the Court of Tax Appeals and Court of Appeals, restrained the Bureau of Internal Revenue from collecting P28,413,783.00 (fourth quarter of 1996) and ordered refund or issuance of a tax credit for P347,741,695.74 paid as output value-added tax (third quarter of 1997). On Motion for Reconsideration, tax authorities defended Section 4.105-1 of Revenue Regulations No. 7-95, which limited real estate dealers' 8% transitional input tax credit under Section 105 of the Old National Internal Revenue Code to improvements on real property. They also claimed Revenue Regulations No. 6-97 did not repeal it.[^104]

The Supreme Court En Banc applied Article 7 and held that an administrative rule or regulation cannot contravene the law on which it is based. Because Section 100 (as amended by Republic Act No. 7716) defined goods or properties to include real properties held primarily for sale or lease, restricting Section 105's transitional input tax credit to improvements was a legislative act beyond the authority of the Commissioner of Internal Revenue and the Secretary of Finance. An administrative agency cannot amend an act of Congress. In case of discrepancy, the basic law prevails, rendering the restrictive portion of Revenue Regulations No. 7-95 a nullity. The Supreme Court further held that Revenue Regulations No. 6-97 effectively repealed that restrictive paragraph by deleting it and creating an irreconcilable inconsistency even without a specific repealing clause. Prior tax payment on beginning inventory was also not required under Section 105. The Supreme Court denied the Motion for Reconsideration with finality for lack of merit. This resolution illustrates both the nullity of a restrictive tax regulation under Article 7 paragraph 3 and implied partial repeal by subsequent deletion.[^105]

### Unconstitutional Levy Void and Refundable Under Unjust Enrichment

In [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815), Letter of Instruction No. 1465, issued by President Marcos on June 3, 1985, imposed a capital recovery component on the domestic sale of fertilizers. Fertiphil Corporation paid P10 per bag to the Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority, which remitted the collections to the depositary bank of private corporation Planters Products, Inc. (PPI). Fertiphil paid levies from July 8, 1985 to January 24, 1986 (stated as P6,689,144 in the facts and decreed as P6,698,144.00 in the trial court judgment) and sued for collection and damages in the Regional Trial Court of Makati City. The trial court declared the levy unconstitutional and ordered PPI to pay Fertiphil P6,698,144.00 with 12% interest from judicial demand, P100,000.00 attorney's fees and costs. The Court of Appeals affirmed with modification deleting attorney's fees. Before the Supreme Court, PPI invoked the operative fact doctrine to retain the levies.[^106]

The Supreme Court Third Division affirmed that Regional Trial Courts have jurisdiction to resolve constitutional issues and held that the levy was an exercise of the taxing power and was unconstitutional for lack of a public purpose. It added that the levy would still be invalid even if treated as a police power measure because it was enacted to benefit a private corporation. It rejected PPI's operative fact defense on two grounds. First, PPI never raised operative fact before the trial court or Court of Appeals and could not raise it belatedly on appeal. Second, citing the second paragraph of Article 7 (and quoting the first and second paragraphs), an unconstitutional law is void, produces no rights and imposes no duties. Ordering refund was not iniquitous because PPI unduly benefited from levies deposited in its bank account. Denying refund would unjustly enrich PPI contrary to Article 22 of the Civil Code. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals November 28, 2003 Decision. This decision confirms that Article 7 paragraph 2 mandates restitution of unconstitutional private exactions when operative fact is unraised or inequitable.[^107]

### Article 7 as Acknowledgment of Judicial Review

In [*Francisco, Jr. v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47018), former President Joseph E. Estrada filed the first impeachment complaint against Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide, Jr. and seven Associate Justices on June 2, 2003. It was referred to the House Committee on Justice on August 5, 2003. On October 23, 2003, Representatives Gilberto C. Teodoro, Jr. and Felix William B. Fuentebella filed a second impeachment complaint, relying on Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the House Rules of Procedure in Impeachment Proceedings. Consolidated original petitions challenged the constitutionality of those rules and the second complaint, while respondents invoked the political question doctrine and judicial restraint.[^108]

The Supreme Court En Banc rejected the political question objection and explained that judicial review is both a power and a duty under Article VIII, Section 1 of the Constitution. Quoting all three paragraphs of Article 7, the Supreme Court adopted the observation of former Justice Vicente V. Mendoza that the executive and legislative branches effectively acknowledged the courts' power of judicial review in Article 7. Because initiation occurs upon filing and referral to the House Committee on Justice, the second complaint violated the one-year bar in Article XI, Section 3(5) of the Constitution. The Supreme Court declared Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V of the House Rules of Procedure in Impeachment Proceedings approved on November 28, 2001 unconstitutional and held the October 23, 2003 second impeachment complaint barred. This decision treats Article 7 as the political branches' own acknowledgment of judicial review.[^109]

### Subsequent Statute Repeals Earlier Conflicting Law

In [*Magno v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51590), private respondent Carlos C. Montes petitioned to disqualify Nestor B. Magno as a mayoralty candidate in San Isidro, Nueva Ecija for the May 14, 2001 elections based on Magno's 1995 Sandiganbayan conviction for four counts of direct bribery (with probation discharge on March 5, 1998). COMELEC disqualified Magno under the five-year disqualification period in Section 12 of Batas Pambansa Blg. 881 (Omnibus Election Code) and denied reconsideration. Magno filed a Rule 65 Petition for Certiorari and later a supplemental petition seeking proclamation over proclaimed winner Sonia Lorenzo.[^110]

The Supreme Court En Banc held that direct bribery involves moral turpitude, but reversed Magno's disqualification. Noting the glaring conflict between Section 12 of Batas Pambansa Blg. 881 (1985) and Section 40 of Republic Act No. 7160 (effective January 1, 1992), the Supreme Court applied the maxim *legis posteriores priores contrarias abrogant*, the repealing clause in Section 534(f) of Republic Act No. 7160 and the first paragraph of Article 7. Laws are repealed only by subsequent ones and not the other way around. When a subsequent law entirely encompasses the subject matter of a former enactment, the earlier law is deemed repealed. Section 40 of Republic Act No. 7160 governed local elective positions specifically and reduced disqualification to two years, which expired on March 5, 2000. However, the Supreme Court held that neither it nor the case was the proper forum to rule on Sonia Lorenzo's proclamation or to declare Magno the winner. His proper remedy was a timely election protest. The Supreme Court partially granted the petition, reversed and set aside COMELEC's May 7, 2001 and May 12, 2001 resolutions and denied Magno's supplemental prayer for proclamation. This decision exemplifies repeal of an earlier general rule by a later conflicting statute under Article 7 paragraph 1.[^111]

### Valid Administrative Amendment Does Not Violate Article 7 or Article 8

In [*Pagpalain Haulers, Inc. v. Trajano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37312), the employer moved to dismiss the petition for certification election filed on May 14, 1997 by ILO-PHILS because the union's books of account were not verified under oath. Both the Med-Arbiter and the Secretary of Labor ruled for the union under Department of Labor and Employment Department Order No. 9, Series of 1997, which dispensed with submitting books of account for registration of a local chapter. Before the Supreme Court, Pagpalain quoted Article 8 and the third paragraph of Article 7 of the Civil Code in arguing that Department Order No. 9 could not supplant earlier Supreme Court decisions (*Progressive Development Corp. v. Secretary of Labor* and *Protection Technology, Inc. v. Secretary of Labor*, as cited in [*Pagpalain Haulers*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37312)).[^112]

The Supreme Court Third Division rejected Pagpalain's contention. Under Article 8 of the Civil Code, courts interpret law and do not enact it. Those earlier decisions merely interpreted the previous Book V Omnibus Rules, so once Department Order No. 9 validly amended Book V to delete the books-of-account submission prerequisite, those rulings became inapplicable to registration. An administrative order is valid when it is (1) issued on the authority of law and (2) not contrary to the law and Constitution. Department Order No. 9 was issued under Article 5 of the Labor Code. Pagpalain failed to show any conflict with the Labor Code (which does not require submitting books of account for registration, though unions must still maintain them under Article 241(h) and (j)) or the Constitution. Courts do not pass judgment on the wisdom of executive policy changes. The Supreme Court dismissed the petition for lack of merit, affirmed the Secretary of Labor's February 27, 1998 resolution and imposed costs against petitioner. This decision defines the boundary where an administrative amendment complies with the law and remains valid.[^113]

### Martial Law Decrees and Implementing Letters Have Force of Law

In [*People v. Gacott, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_116049_1995.html), Assistant City Prosecutor Perfecto E. Pe filed Criminal Case No. 11529 on February 2, 1994 for violation of the Anti-Dummy Law (Commonwealth Act No. 108) before Branch 47 of the Regional Trial Court of Puerto Princesa City. Accused Arne Strom and Grace A. Reyes, through counsel Atty. Elvira T. Bermejo, moved to quash because Republic Act No. 1130 vested the power to prosecute in the Anti-Dummy Board. The prosecution answered that Letter of Implementation No. 2, Series of 1972 had abolished the Board. Judge Eustaquio Z. Gacott, Jr. quashed the case on March 18, 1994. In a later order denying reconsideration, he invoked Article 7 of the New Civil Code to hold that a letter of implementation is not a law and could not repeal Republic Act No. 1130. The People filed a special civil action for certiorari.[^114]

The Supreme Court Second Division held that Judge Gacott committed gross ignorance of the law by failing even to read Letter of Implementation No. 2, which expressly stated that it was issued pursuant to Presidential Decree No. 1. Presidential Decrees and implementing issuances promulgated under former President Marcos's martial law powers have the same force and effect as statutes enacted by Congress. Neither Presidential Decree No. 1 nor Letter of Implementation No. 2 had been revised, revoked or repealed. Both remained operative under Section 3, Article XVII of the Constitution and validly abolished the Anti-Dummy Board. Courts are duty bound under Section 1, Rule 129 to take judicial notice of all laws of the land. The Supreme Court also faulted defense counsel for violating Canons 5 and 10 of the Code of Professional Responsibility by misrepresenting that private respondents had nothing to do with the quashal order when it was not issued *motu proprio* but on their motion. The Supreme Court annulled and set aside the dismissal order (printed as dated March 18, 1999 in the dispositive text), reinstated Criminal Case No. 11529, reprimanded and fined Judge Gacott P10,000.00 for gross ignorance of the law with a stern warning, reprimanded and fined Atty. Elvira T. Bermejo P10,000.00 for ignorance of the law and for failing to observe candor, fairness and good faith. Both received a stern warning. It ordered copies spread on their personal records. This decision clarifies that presidential decrees and martial law implementing issuances carrying statutory force validly repeal prior laws.[^115]

### Central Bank Circular Cannot Repeal a Statute

In [*Palanca v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_106685_1994.html), Simplicio A. Palanca sold a lot on installment to Jose S. Sanicas on January 22, 1977. Edgardo S. Sanicas later assumed his brother's account. Paragraph 11 of the contract increased the unpaid balance proportionately in the event of monetary fluctuation on the basis of P6.72 to US$1.00. Palanca refused the buyer's tender of P44,955.87 and claimed P155,630.40 under the escalator clause. The buyer consigned the amount on May 29, 1987. Applying Article 1250 of the Civil Code, the trial court found no extraordinary inflation and ordered Palanca to execute a deed of conveyance in view of the P44,979.87 consigned. The Court of Appeals (CA-G.R. CV No. 20245) fixed the amount payable at P70,688.17 without escalation. Palanca sought review and asked for a proportionate increase under paragraph 11.[^116]

The Supreme Court En Banc denied the petition, but not on the ground that there was no extraordinary inflation. It held that paragraph 11 violated Republic Act No. 529 because it let the obligee demand payment in an amount of Philippine money measured by a foreign currency. The sale was a domestic transaction payable in pesos with no foreign currency directly involved, so the obligation had to be paid in the stipulated peso amounts without exchange-rate adjustment. The Supreme Court added that the 1992 Monetary Board resolution and Central Bank Circulars Nos. 1318, 1338, 1348 and 1353 merely liberalized foreign exchange transactions and did not repeal or amend Republic Act No. 529. Applying the first paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that a Central Bank circular cannot repeal a law because only a law can repeal another law. This decision shows that an administrative circular cannot repeal a statute under Article 7.[^117]

### Supreme Court Procedural Circular Supersedes Statutory Appeal Forum

In [*First Lepanto Ceramics, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_110571_1994.html), the Board of Investments granted First Lepanto Ceramics, Inc.'s application to amend its registration on December 10, 1992. Oppositor Mariwasa Manufacturing, Inc. appealed to the Court of Appeals under Supreme Court Circular No. 1-91. First Lepanto moved to dismiss, insisting that Article 82 of Executive Order No. 226 vested exclusive appellate jurisdiction over Board of Investments decisions in the Supreme Court and could not be superseded by Circular No. 1-91. The Court of Appeals denied dismissal, prompting a petition for certiorari and prohibition before the Supreme Court.[^118]

The Supreme Court Second Division upheld Circular No. 1-91, which was issued under Section 5(5), Article VIII of the 1987 Constitution to implement Batas Pambansa Blg. 129. Quoting [*Inciong v. De Guia*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/sep1987/am_r-249-rtj_1987.html) (which quoted the first paragraph of Article 7), the Supreme Court explained that although a circular is not strictly a statute, a Supreme Court circular has the force and effect of law and must be obeyed until repealed or modified. Where and in what manner appeals are brought pertain to procedure rather than substantive rights. Circular No. 1-91 therefore effectively repealed or superseded Article 82 of Executive Order No. 226 insofar as the procedural method and forum for Board of Investments appeals were concerned, transferring them to the Court of Appeals within fifteen days without diminishing the substantive right to appeal. The Supreme Court dismissed the petition for certiorari and prohibition for lack of merit and lifted its July 19, 1993 temporary restraining order. This decision distinguishes Supreme Court procedural rules under Article VIII, Section 5(5) from ordinary executive circulars.[^119]

### Executive Order Cannot Repeal a Presidential Decree

In [*Mathay, Jr. v. Macalincag*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/dec1993/gr_97618_1993.html), taxpayers Ismael Mathay, Jr., Rufino S. Javier and Consuelo Puyat-Reyes filed three original prohibition petitions (G.R. Nos. 97618, 97760 and 102319). They challenged revised real property Schedules of Market Values prepared solely by the local assessors of Quezon City, Pasig and Makati instead of jointly by all Metro Manila assessors under Section 9 of Presidential Decree No. 921. The Supreme Court referred the cases to the Central Board of Assessment Appeals, which declared the schedules void and rejected respondents' argument that Executive Order No. 392 abolished the Metro Manila Commission and repealed Presidential Decree No. 921.[^120]

In its decision quoted by the Supreme Court, the Central Board of Assessment Appeals cited Article 7 of the New Civil Code to rule that an executive order (Executive Order No. 392) cannot repeal a legislative act (Presidential Decree No. 921), that a legislative act can be repealed only by another subsequent legislative act and that implied repeals are never favored. After reviewing the records, the Supreme Court En Banc agreed with the Board's conclusion that the solely prepared schedules violated Section 9 of Presidential Decree No. 921 and Department of Finance Assessment Regulations No. 7-77. It expressly approved and adopted as its own the Board's dispositions declaring null and void the Schedules of Market Values for Quezon City, Pasig and Makati (and the Makati compromise agreements) and ordering the respective assessors and treasurers to act accordingly. This resolution reinforces that an executive order cannot repeal an existing legislative decree.[^121]

### Orthodox View of Unconstitutionality and Pending Appeals

In [*Republic v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_79732_1993.html), the Republic expropriated land of Henrico Uvero et al. for the Nabua-Bato-Agos Section of the Philippine-Japan Highway Loan road. The Regional Trial Court awarded just compensation at fair market value rather than the assessor's or owner's valuation under Presidential Decree No. 76 (as amended by Presidential Decree No. 1533). The Court of Appeals affirmed. While the Republic's petition for review was pending, *Export Processing Zone Authority v. Dulay* (as cited in [*Republic v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_79732_1993.html)) declared those valuation decrees unconstitutional. The Republic urged prospective application only.[^122]

Quoting Justice Isagani A. Cruz's treatise, the Supreme Court Third Division contrasted the orthodox view (which the treatise stated is expressed in Article 7 of the Civil Code) with the modern view. Under the strict orthodox view, a statute per se repugnant to the Constitution is a total nullity deemed never to have existed. The Supreme Court acknowledged two qualifications. A law may be valid in general yet invalid only in retrospective application impairing vested contractual rights. Judicial nullification may also leave interim effects or a *fait accompli* undisturbed for equity and fair play. Neither exception applied here because the expropriation cases were still pending appeal when *Export Processing Zone Authority v. Dulay* was rendered and invoked. The Supreme Court declined to pass on the provisional 10% deposit under Rule 67 (not contested by private respondents) and dismissed the petition, with no costs. This decision shows how the orthodox view quoted from Justice Cruz's treatise operates in pending appeals when no *fait accompli* has intervened.[^123]

### Presidential Speech Cannot Repeal a Penal Statute

In [*People v. Aquino*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/jul1991/gr_39274_1991.html), the Municipal Court of Balungao, Pangasinan convicted Bartolome Padilla on November 29, 1972 of *kaingin* in a forest reserve under Section 2751 of the Revised Administrative Code (as amended by Republic Act No. 3701). It sentenced him to seven months of imprisonment and a P1,200.00 fine. On appeal for trial *de novo* before Branch XIV of the Court of First Instance of Pangasinan (Criminal Case No. 148-R), Padilla moved to dismiss. He invoked a June 12, 1974 proclamation of President Marcos promising to recognize private rights over alienable public lands. Judge Narciso A. Aquino dismissed the case on August 22, 1974. He admitted that Republic Act No. 3701 had not been repealed but relied on the spirit and meaning of various presidential decrees, orders and proclamations on giving land to the landless. The People petitioned for review on certiorari.[^124]

The Supreme Court Third Division first noted that double jeopardy was unavailable because dismissal was at the accused's instance and due process was satisfied because Padilla had every opportunity to be heard. Applying the first paragraph of Article 7, the Supreme Court held that a law may be repealed only by a subsequent law. Absent a repealing law, a judge has no choice save to apply and enforce Section 2751. No spirit or meaning lacking statutory form can overturn a prior statute. The so-called proclamation was only an extract from an Independence Day speech announcing proposed policies, so it was not a law. Subsequent Presidential Decrees Nos. 389 and 705 also continued to penalize *kaingin* in forest reserves. Padilla's claim of an oral assurance from the Municipal Treasurer was a matter of defense at trial, not a jurisdictional bar. The Supreme Court granted due course, reversed and set aside Judge Aquino's August 22, 1974 Order, ordered the trial court to resume proceedings in Criminal Case No. 148-R and imposed costs against Padilla. This decision confirms that judges must enforce unrepealed statutes regardless of executive speeches or policy announcements.[^125]

### Labor Commission Rule Cannot Override Immediate Execution Under Statute

In [*Juan v. Musñgi*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/oct1987/gr_l-76053_1987.html), Labor Arbiter Porfirio Villanueva ordered employer Fernando Juan (Foto Me) on July 3, 1984 to reinstate employee Celso P. Musñgi with full backwages. The National Labor Relations Commission affirmed on November 20, 1985. The records later disappeared and had to be reconstituted. Labor Arbiter Eduardo G. Magno then issued a July 22, 1986 order directing Juan to deposit P49,626.40 and a September 25, 1986 writ of execution. Juan filed an original petition for certiorari and prohibition. He claimed that the Commission's resolution never became final because he had no notice of it.[^126]

The Supreme Court First Division held that the Commission's resolution had become final and executory. While Article 223 of the Labor Code grants ten days to appeal a Labor Arbiter's decision, it provides that decisions of the Commission itself are immediately executory even pending appeal unless stayed by the Secretary of Labor for special reasons. The Supreme Court noted that Section 2, Rule XI of the Commission's Rules made its decisions executory only after ten calendar days from receipt. Applying the third paragraph of Article 7, it held that the rule could not prevail over Article 223 because administrative regulations must be in harmony with the law they implement. The Supreme Court also found that the bailiff had served the resolution on the office of petitioner's counsel of record on December 9, 1985 without any notice of withdrawal or substitution. Another counsel for petitioner was given a copy during the reconstitution hearings. It dismissed the petition for lack of merit and permanently lifted the October 10, 1986 temporary restraining order. This decision confirms that agency procedural rules yielding to a statute cannot delay statutorily mandated immediate execution.[^127]

### Practice Is Not the Law in Judicial Raffling

In [*Inciong v. De Guia*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/sep1987/am_r-249-rtj_1987.html), Atty. Ceferino Inciong charged Judge Leticia S. Mariano de Guia of Branch 2, Regional Trial Court of Balayan, Batangas with inducing court employees to falsify court records to make it appear that Criminal Case No. 2589 had been raffled to her sala. In a rejoinder, Judge de Guia stated that assigning cases without a raffle was very common in Balayan. Despite complainant's later unexplained oral withdrawal and the judge's reappointment, the Supreme Court ordered a *de novo* investigation by Court of Appeals Associate Justice Justo P. Torres, Jr.[^128]

The Supreme Court En Banc agreed with and adopted the findings and recommendation of Justice Torres. Justice Torres shared the finding of Deputy Senior Judicial Administrator Arturo Buena that suspicion arose of irregularities in the raffle of cases in Balayan. He then cited Article 7 of the New Civil Code to stress that practice is not the law. Even if unraffled assignment were common, Judge de Guia could not go against the Supreme Court's circular on the raffle of cases until it was repealed or modified. The Supreme Court found it proven that Judge de Guia induced two court employees to falsify records and violated Circular No. 7 dated September 23, 1974, as amended, though suspicion of corrupt motives in acquitting the accused remained a suspicion only. It found Judge de Guia guilty of misconduct and irregularities in office, imposed a fine equivalent to three months' pay with a stern warning and instructed the Court Administrator to investigate compliance with Circular No. 7 in the Regional Trial Court of Balayan, Batangas. This resolution shows that widespread local practice cannot excuse violating a binding Supreme Court circular.[^129]

### Prior Agency Non-Opposition Cannot Excuse Non-Observance

In [*De Gallego v. Land Authority*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/aug1981/gr_26848_1981.html), Caridad O. de Gallego, registered owner of a Baclaran Estate lot covered by TCT No. 46402 (Rizal Registry), petitioned Branch 7 of the Court of First Instance of Rizal (LRC Case No. 458) to cancel three title encumbrances inscribed in 1954: (1) a five-year ban on transfer without the Secretary's written consent, (2) a perpetual ban on transfer except to landless qualified persons (outside hereditary succession) and (3) reversion for violation. The Land Authority agreed to cancel Condition No. 1 because five years had elapsed but opposed cancelling Conditions Nos. 2 and 3. The trial court denied the petition on July 19, 1966 and denied reconsideration on September 8, 1966. De Gallego appealed, arguing that the area had become commercial and that a previous Land Authority governor had not opposed a similar cancellation petition.[^130]

The Supreme Court First Division held that Conditions Nos. 2 and 3 were imposed under Lands Administrative Order No. R-3, which has the force and effect of law. Applying the first paragraph of Article 7, it held that until the law or that order is repealed, amended or modified, the conditions must remain. A previous governor's failure to oppose a similar cancellation petition did not excuse non-observance because a wrong act cannot be cured by the commission of another wrong. Arguments that commercial development rendered the conditions obsolete concerned the wisdom and expediency of legislation, which belong exclusively to the legislature. The Supreme Court modified the July 19, 1966 and September 8, 1966 Orders by ordering cancellation of Condition No. 1 (the five-year period having expired) and maintaining Conditions Nos. 2 and 3 as they are, with no costs. This decision confirms that prior official tolerance or changed economic conditions do not repeal statutory restrictions under Article 7.[^131]

### Implied Repeal Disfavored Especially in Penal Statutes

In [*People v. Purisima*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1978/nov1978/gr_42050_66_1978.html), the People filed twenty-six consolidated petitions for review. They assailed orders of three Courts of First Instance: Judge Amante P. Purisima of Manila Branch VII (17 petitions), Judge Maximo A. Maceren of Manila Branch XVIII (8 petitions) and Judge Wenceslao M. Polo of Samar (1 petition). The orders quashed Informations under Presidential Decree No. 9(3) for carrying bladed or deadly weapons outside residence without alleging connection to subversion, rebellion, insurrection, lawless violence, criminality, chaos or public disorder. The People contended that Presidential Decree No. 9(3) repealed Section 26 of Act No. 1780 and Manila Ordinance No. 3820 (as amended by Ordinance No. 3928).[^132]

The Supreme Court En Banc applied the first paragraph of Article 7 and rejected the People's claim of implied repeal. Presidential Decree No. 9(3) contained no repealing clause. Repeal by implication is not favored. That rule holds with greater force for penal statutes construed strictly against the State and liberally in favor of the accused. Because Act No. 1780, Manila Ordinance No. 3820 and Presidential Decree No. 9(3) all subsisted with different elements and penalties, an Information under Presidential Decree No. 9(3) had to allege both elements, including the required subversive or lawless-violence motivation, to satisfy the constitutional right to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation. The Supreme Court denied all twenty-six Petitions for Review and affirmed the quashal orders without costs, subject to its observations on the State's right to file amended Informations under Presidential Decree No. 9(3) or new ones under existing statutes or city ordinances as the facts may warrant, without resolving whether double jeopardy could be invoked if new complaints were filed. This decision anchors the presumption against implied repeal of penal statutes in Article 7.[^133]

### Administrative Regulation Cannot Define a Crime Absent Statutory Authority

In [*People v. Maceren*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_32166_1977.html), five accused were charged on March 7, 1969 in the Municipal Court of Sta. Cruz, Laguna (Criminal Case No. 5429) with electro fishing in violation of Fisheries Administrative Order No. 84-1 issued by the Secretary of Agriculture and Natural Resources and the Commissioner of Fisheries under the Fisheries Law. The municipal court quashed the complaint. On appeal, the Court of First Instance of Laguna (Civil Case No. SC-36) affirmed dismissal on June 9, 1970 because electric current is not an obnoxious or poisonous substance under Section 11 of the Fisheries Law. The prosecution appealed under Republic Act No. 5440.[^134]

The Supreme Court Second Division applied the third paragraph of Article 7, holding that administrative or executive acts, orders and regulations are valid only when they are not contrary to the laws or the Constitution. Delegated rule-making power is confined to carrying into effect the enacted law and cannot extend, amend or expand statutory requirements. In case of discrepancy between the basic law and an implementing regulation, the basic law prevails. Declaring what constitutes a crime and how it shall be punished belongs exclusively to the legislature. While violating a validly issued regulation authorized by statute can constitute a crime punishable under the statute, the Fisheries Law did not expressly punish electro fishing. Fisheries Administrative Orders Nos. 84 and 84-1 therefore exceeded delegated authority and were invalid. The Supreme Court noted that Presidential Decree No. 704 of May 16, 1975 now expressly punishes electro fishing. Because the municipal court sat in a provincial capital, its order was directly appealable to the Supreme Court, so the Court of First Instance had no appellate jurisdiction. The Supreme Court set aside the Court of First Instance June 9, 1970 decision for lack of appellate jurisdiction and affirmed the municipal court's dismissal order in Criminal Case No. 5429, with *costs de oficio*. This foundational decision sets the classic Article 7 boundary against administrative criminal legislation.[^135]

## Related Provisions

- **[Article 4](art-0004.md) (Non-Retroactivity of Laws):** Governs whether statutes operate prospectively or retroactively, whereas Article 7 governs the voidness of unconstitutional laws. Its equitable operative fact exception is discussed in [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914)[^136] and the survival of interim effects in [*Republic v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_79732_1993.html)[^137].
- **[Article 5](art-0005.md) (Acts Against Mandatory or Prohibitory Laws):** Declares acts executed against mandatory or prohibitory laws void unless the law authorizes their validity, complementing Article 7's rule that administrative or executive acts contrary to law are void.[^civil-code]
- **[Article 8](art-0008.md) (Judicial Decisions as Part of the Legal System):** Explains that judicial decisions applying or interpreting laws form part of the legal system, analyzed alongside Article 7 in [*Pagpalain Haulers, Inc. v. Trajano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37312)[^138] (decisions interpreting superseded implementing rules do not freeze executive rule-making) and [*Abrenica v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653)[^139] (the Commission on Audit properly applied a prior Supreme Court ruling that the fixed hazard pay rate was void).
- **[Article 11](art-0011.md) (Customs Contrary to Law):** Prohibits customs contrary to law, public order or public policy, reinforcing Article 7's first paragraph that custom or practice never excuses non-observance of a law.[^civil-code]
- **[Article 22](art-0022.md) (Unjust Enrichment):** Supplies the restitution ground requiring refund of levies collected under a law declared unconstitutional under Article 7, as applied in [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815).[^140]
- **Civil Code, Article 1432 (Estoppel):** Estoppel is an equitable principle adopted only insofar as it does not conflict with the Civil Code and other laws, applied alongside Article 7 in [*Department of Education v. Rizal Teachers Kilusang Bayan for Credit, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65522).[^141]
- **1987 Constitution, Article VIII, Section 1 and Section 5(5) (Judicial Review and Rule-Making Power):** Ground the courts' duty to invalidate unconstitutional acts acknowledged in Article 7 ([*Francisco, Jr. v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47018)[^142]) and the Supreme Court's authority to issue procedural circulars with the force of law ([*First Lepanto Ceramics, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_110571_1994.html)[^143]).
- **National Internal Revenue Code, Section 246 (Non-Retroactivity of Tax Rulings):** Specifically codifies the operative fact doctrine for formalized Bureau of Internal Revenue rules and rulings, precluding broader invocation in tax cases under [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257).[^144]

## Key Cases

- [*People v. Maceren*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_32166_1977.html): Five accused were charged with electro fishing under Fisheries Administrative Order No. 84-1, which the municipal court quashed and the Court of First Instance affirmed. Applying Article 7 paragraph 3, the Supreme Court held the regulation invalid because the Fisheries Law did not punish electro fishing and defining crimes is exclusively legislative, setting aside the Court of First Instance decision for lack of appellate jurisdiction and affirming the municipal court's dismissal with *costs de oficio*.[^145]
- [*People v. Purisima*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1978/nov1978/gr_42050_66_1978.html): Judges of three Courts of First Instance quashed Informations under Presidential Decree No. 9(3) in 26 cases for omitting connection to subversion or lawless violence. Applying Article 7 paragraph 1, the Supreme Court held Presidential Decree No. 9(3) had no repealing clause and did not impliedly repeal Act No. 1780 Section 26 or Manila Ordinance No. 3820 (as amended), denying the 26 petitions without costs while noting the State's right to file amended or new Informations as facts warrant and reserving double jeopardy.[^146]
- [*De Gallego v. Land Authority*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/aug1981/gr_26848_1981.html): A Baclaran Estate landowner petitioned the Court of First Instance of Rizal to cancel three title encumbrances based on commercial development and a former governor's non-opposition in another case. Applying Article 7 paragraph 1, the Supreme Court held that encumbrances imposed under an administrative order with the force of law remain until it is repealed or modified and a wrong act cannot cure another wrong, modifying the 1966 orders to cancel only expired Condition No. 1 while retaining Conditions Nos. 2 and 3.[^147]
- [*Inciong v. De Guia*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/sep1987/am_r-249-rtj_1987.html): A trial judge accused of inducing employees to falsify records stated that unraffled case assignment was common in her court. Adopting the report of the Investigating Justice (who cited Article 7 paragraph 1), the Supreme Court held that practice is not the law and fined Judge de Guia three months' pay with a stern warning for misconduct and violating Circular No. 7 (as amended).[^148]
- [*Juan v. Musñgi*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/oct1987/gr_l-76053_1987.html): An employer sought to annul a Labor Arbiter's execution order, claiming he had no notice of the NLRC resolution. Applying Article 7 paragraph 3, the Supreme Court held that the 10-day waiting period in Section 2, Rule XI of the NLRC Rules could not prevail over Article 223 of the Labor Code making NLRC decisions immediately executory, dismissing the petition and permanently lifting the temporary restraining order.[^149]
- [*People v. Aquino*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/jul1991/gr_39274_1991.html): A trial judge dismissed a *kaingin* appeal under Section 2751 of the Revised Administrative Code (as amended by Republic Act No. 3701) based on the spirit of presidential issuances and a proclamation drawn from an Independence Day speech. Applying Article 7 paragraph 1, the Supreme Court held that a speech is not a repealing law and judges must enforce unrepealed statutes, reversing the August 22, 1974 order and ordering trial resumed with costs against private respondent.[^150]
- [*Republic v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/nov1993/gr_79732_1993.html): Both the Regional Trial Court and Court of Appeals awarded fair market value just compensation despite Presidential Decree No. 1533, which was struck down while the Republic's appeal was pending. Quoting Justice Isagani A. Cruz's treatise on the orthodox view expressed in Article 7 paragraph 2, the Supreme Court held that no exception or *fait accompli* barred applying the unconstitutionality ruling to pending appeals and dismissed the petition without costs.[^151]
- [*Mathay, Jr. v. Macalincag*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/dec1993/gr_97618_1993.html): Taxpayers challenged Metro Manila real property valuation schedules prepared solely by individual local assessors contrary to Section 9 of Presidential Decree No. 921. Approving and adopting the dispositions of the Central Board of Assessment Appeals (which cited Article 7 to hold Executive Order No. 392 could not repeal Presidential Decree No. 921), the Supreme Court declared the Quezon City, Pasig and Makati schedules and Makati compromise agreements null and void.[^152]
- [*First Lepanto Ceramics, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_110571_1994.html): A company challenged the Court of Appeals' refusal to dismiss a Board of Investments appeal filed under Supreme Court Circular No. 1-91 instead of Article 82 of Executive Order No. 226. Quoting [*Inciong v. De Guia*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/sep1987/am_r-249-rtj_1987.html) (which quoted Article 7 paragraph 1), the Supreme Court held Circular No. 1-91 had the force of law and validly superseded procedural appeal provisions, dismissing the petition and lifting the temporary restraining order.[^153]
- [*Palanca v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_106685_1994.html): The trial court and Court of Appeals refused to apply a peso-dollar escalation clause in a domestic land sale for lack of extraordinary inflation. The Supreme Court denied the vendor's petition on another ground because the clause violated Republic Act No. 529. Applying Article 7 paragraph 1, it added that 1992 Central Bank circulars liberalizing foreign exchange did not repeal that law because a circular cannot repeal a law.[^154]
- [*People v. Gacott, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_116049_1995.html): A trial judge quashed an Anti-Dummy Law prosecution and, in denying reconsideration, invoked Article 7 to claim Letter of Implementation No. 2 could not repeal Republic Act No. 1130. The Supreme Court held Presidential Decree No. 1 and Letter of Implementation No. 2 had statutory force under martial law powers and Article XVII, Section 3 of the Constitution, annulling the dismissal, reinstating the case and reprimanding and fining both the judge and defense counsel P10,000.00 each.[^155]
- [*Pagpalain Haulers, Inc. v. Trajano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37312): An employer quoted Article 8 and Article 7 paragraph 3 in challenging Department of Labor and Employment Department Order No. 9 dispensing with books of account for union chapter registration. The Supreme Court dismissed the petition and affirmed the Secretary of Labor because Department Order No. 9 was issued under Article 5 of the Labor Code, did not contravene the Labor Code or Constitution and superseded only earlier implementing rules.[^156]
- [*Magno v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51590): COMELEC disqualified a local mayoralty candidate under the five-year period in Section 12 of Batas Pambansa Blg. 881. Applying Article 7 paragraph 1, the Supreme Court held Section 40 of Republic Act No. 7160 (two-year period) repealed Section 12 for local positions, reversing COMELEC's resolutions while denying petitioner's supplemental prayer for proclamation for lack of jurisdiction.[^157]
- [*Francisco, Jr. v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47018): Consolidated petitions challenged the second impeachment complaint against Chief Justice Hilario G. Davide, Jr. and House impeachment rules. Quoting Article 7 as the executive and legislative acknowledgment of judicial review, the Supreme Court declared Sections 16 and 17 of Rule V unconstitutional and held the second complaint barred under Article XI, Section 3(5).[^158]
- [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815): The Regional Trial Court invalidated Letter of Instruction No. 1465's fertilizer levy and ordered PPI to refund P6,698,144.00 with 12% interest from judicial demand plus attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed without attorney's fees. Applying Article 7 paragraph 2 and Article 22, the Supreme Court held the unconstitutional levy void and operative fact both belatedly raised and inequitable, denying the petition and affirming the Court of Appeals.[^159]
- [*Fort Bonifacio Development Corp. v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49844): On reconsideration of a decision restraining collection of P28,413,783.00 and ordering refund or credit of P347,741,695.74 output VAT, tax authorities defended Section 4.105-1 of Revenue Regulations No. 7-95. Applying Article 7, the Supreme Court held the regulation void for restricting statutory goods to improvements, held Revenue Regulations No. 6-97 impliedly repealed that paragraph by deletion and denied reconsideration with finality.[^160]
- [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914): After the Court of Appeals capped an illegally dismissed seafarer's unexpired salary at three months under Section 10 of Republic Act No. 8042, the cap was struck down in *Serrano*. Citing Article 7 (quoting paragraph 1), the Supreme Court held operative fact inapplicable to let erring employers profit from an unconstitutional cap, awarding nine months' salary at US$1,430.00 per month and affirming all other awards.[^161]
- [*Philippine Coconut Producers Federation, Inc. (COCOFED) v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/23897): Petitioners appealed Sandiganbayan Partial Summary Judgments nullifying coconut levy decrees and PCA implementing rules and declaring UCPB shares, CIIF companies and 33,133,266 San Miguel Corporation shares government-owned. Quoting [*Yap*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914) and *Chavez v. NHA* on Article 7, the Supreme Court held operative fact inapplicable to validate private ownership of public funds, affirming the judgments with modification and reserving G.R. No. 180705.[^162]
- [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257): On reconsideration, a taxpayer sought operative fact protection based on alleged BIR and CTA practice regarding the 120+30-day VAT refund periods. Citing Article 7 as the general rule that a void act creates no rights, the Supreme Court held operative fact requires a formal legislative or executive measure (governed strictly by Section 246 of the Tax Code for BIR rulings such as BIR Ruling No. DA-489-03) and denied both motions for reconsideration with finality.[^163]
- [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044): Consolidated petitions challenged the Disbursement Acceleration Program and DBM National Budget Circular No. 541. Quoting Article 7, the Supreme Court declared specified DAP acts unconstitutional and uncertified use of unprogrammed funds void, applying operative fact only to irreversible projects benefiting good-faith recipients while excluding DAP authors, proponents and implementors absent concrete findings of good faith by proper tribunals.[^164]
- [*Bartolome v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/58177): SSS and ECC denied a biological mother's death-benefit claim under an ECC rule limiting dependent parents in Labor Code Article 167(j) to legitimate parents. Applying Article 7 paragraph 3, the Supreme Court struck down the restriction as unauthorized administrative legislation, reversed the ECC Decision and ordered release of secondary-beneficiary benefits to petitioner.[^165]
- [*Department of Education v. Rizal Teachers Kilusang Bayan for Credit, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65522): The Regional Trial Court and Court of Appeals granted mandamus compelling DepEd to remit P111,989,006.98 and continue payroll deductions for a private lender. Applying Article 7 paragraph 1, the Supreme Court held continued domestic practice cannot create a legal duty contravening Section 7 of Republic Act No. 9155, reversing the Court of Appeals and dismissing the complaint.[^166]
- [*Municipality of Tupi v. Faustino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586): The Regional Trial Court invalidated Municipal Ordinance No. 688 and ordered refund of all fines collected. Quoting [*San Roque*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257) (on Article 7) and [*Araullo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044), the Supreme Court held operative fact unraised and inapplicable to Faustino's refundable P1,000.00 fine, affirming the decision with modification deleting refunds for non-party motorists.[^167]
- [*Bureau of Internal Revenue v. First E-Bank Tower Condominium Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66002): The Regional Trial Court invalidated BIR Revenue Memorandum Circular No. 65-2012 taxing condominium dues. The Court of Appeals dismissed both appeals on jurisdiction. Citing Article 7, the Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals on jurisdiction, denied both petitions and affirmed the trial court, holding that recovery of consignated tax payments requires a post-finality motion proving judicial consignation requisites.[^168]
- [*Municipality of Payao v. Municipality of Imelda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68647): The Regional Trial Court and Court of Appeals granted Imelda's mandamus petition over Barangay Guintolan under Presidential Decree No. 1239. Applying Article 7 paragraph 1, the Supreme Court held that over thirty years of dispute did not validate infringing Presidential Decree No. 1239, affirming with modification ordering Payao to desist from interfering with Imelda's supervisory authority.[^169]
- [*Abrenica v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653): COA disallowed part of the fixed hazard pay paid to San Lazaro Hospital workers under void DOH Administrative Order No. 2006-0011. Applying Article 7 paragraph 3, the Supreme Court sustained the disallowance because the administrative order contravened Section 21 of Republic Act No. 7305, but modified COA Decision No. 2014-158 to excuse petitioners from refunding the amounts individually received on equitable grounds.[^170]
- [*Partido Demokratiko Pilipino-Lakas ng Bayan (PDP-Laban) v. Commission on Elections En Banc*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68780): Petitioners challenged COMELEC Resolution No. 10147 extending the 30-day statutory deadline in Section 14 of Republic Act No. 7166 for filing SOCEs. Applying Article 7 paragraph 3, the Supreme Court declared Resolution No. 10147 void as repugnant to law, but applied operative fact to deem SOCEs submitted on or before June 30, 2016 timely filed.[^171]
- [*Macalintal v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69031): Consolidated petitions challenged Republic Act No. 11935 postponing the December 2022 BSKE. Applying Article 7 paragraph 2, the Supreme Court declared Republic Act No. 11935 unconstitutional and recognized the revival of Republic Act No. 11462, while applying operative fact so the October 2023 elections proceeded and incumbent officials served in hold-over capacity with terms deemed ended December 31, 2022.[^172]
- [*Office of the Court Administrator v. Castañeda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2024/oct2024/am_rtj-12-2316_2024.html): Affected litigants asked whether decisions rendered by a suspended Regional Trial Court judge were valid. Quoting [*San Roque*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257) on Article 7 and operative fact, the Supreme Court extended operative fact by analogy alongside the *de facto* officer doctrine and declared valid the orders and decisions rendered by Judge Castañeda during her suspension from January 12, 2010 to October 9, 2012.[^173]
- [*Sinag v. Sangguniang Panlalawigan ng Batangas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69836): The Regional Trial Court dismissed a petition to nullify a 2009 provincial ordinance that repealed an unimplemented 1997 barangay abolition ordinance. The Court of Appeals dismissed the appeal as an improper remedy. Applying Article 7 paragraph 1, the Supreme Court held non-implementation does not defeat a law's validity, reversed the Court of Appeals and declared Provincial Ordinance No. 002, series of 2009 void for failing population and plebiscite requisites.[^174]
- [*Duterte v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70257): Respondent House of Representatives sought reconsideration and prospective operative-fact application of the July 25, 2025 Decision voiding the Articles of Impeachment against Vice President Sara Z. Duterte. Citing Article 7 as the general rule, the Supreme Court held operative fact has doubtful applicability where the transmittal itself was tainted by constitutional and procedural noncompliance and denied the Motion for Reconsideration with finality.[^175]

## Suggested Answers

### Hypothetical 1: Administrative Circular Invoked to Override a Statute

**Question:** A landowner sells a residential lot in the Philippines on installment under a contract payable in Philippine pesos. The contract contains a clause adjusting the remaining peso balance upward based on the peso-to-U.S.-dollar exchange rate. A statute expressly requires domestic obligations to be paid in Philippine currency without foreign-currency adjustment. Years later, the Central Bank issues circulars liberalizing the purchase and sale of foreign exchange. When the buyer consigns the unadjusted peso balance, the seller refuses to execute a deed of conveyance, arguing that the Central Bank circulars repealed the statute. Decide.

**Answer:** The seller's argument fails and the unadjusted peso obligation governs. Under the first paragraph of Article 7 of the Civil Code, laws are repealed only by subsequent laws and their violation or non-observance is not excused by disuse, custom or practice to the contrary.[^civil-code] In [*Palanca v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_106685_1994.html), the Supreme Court held that Central Bank circulars liberalizing foreign exchange trading did not repeal or amend Republic Act No. 529.[^176] A Central Bank circular cannot repeal a statute because only a subsequent law can repeal an existing law. Because no foreign currency was directly involved in the domestic sale of land, the buyer is obliged to pay only the stipulated peso amounts without any exchange-rate adjustment.[^177]

### Hypothetical 2: Unconstitutional Levy and the Doctrine of Operative Fact

**Question:** A presidential issuance imposes a P10-per-bag levy on all domestic sales of an agricultural input and directs that the proceeds be remitted directly to the bank account of a private corporation to rehabilitate its operations. A competing distributor pays millions of pesos under the levy and later sues in the Regional Trial Court for a refund, challenging the issuance as unconstitutional. The Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals declare the levy unconstitutional and order a refund. For the first time before the Supreme Court, the private corporation invokes the doctrine of operative fact to keep the levies already collected before the declaration of nullity. Should the private corporation prevail?

**Answer:** No. The private corporation must refund the levies it received. Under the second paragraph of Article 7 of the Civil Code, when the courts declare a law inconsistent with the Constitution, the law is void and the Constitution governs.[^civil-code] As held in [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815), an unconstitutional law produces no rights, imposes no duties and is inoperative as if it had never been passed.[^178] The doctrine of operative fact does not aid the corporation for two reasons. First, the corporation did not raise the doctrine before the Regional Trial Court or the Court of Appeals and cannot raise it for the first time on appeal. Second, the doctrine is an equitable exception that applies only to avoid undue burden and inequity. Ordering a refund is not iniquitous where the private corporation unduly benefited from an unconstitutional exaction. Allowing it to keep the funds would unjustly enrich it at the distributor's expense contrary to Article 22 of the Civil Code.[^179]

### Hypothetical 3: Implementing Rule Creating a Criminal Offense Beyond the Statute

**Question:** A fisheries statute prohibits fishing with obnoxious or poisonous substances and authorizes the Department Secretary to issue regulations consistent with the law to carry out its provisions. The statute does not mention or penalize electro fishing. The Department Secretary promulgates an administrative order prohibiting and penalizing electro fishing in fresh water fisheries. Five fishermen are charged in the municipal court with violating that administrative order. They move to quash the complaint, arguing that the administrative order is void. How should the court rule?

**Answer:** The court should grant the motion to quash and dismiss the criminal complaint. Under the third paragraph of Article 7 of the Civil Code, administrative or executive acts, orders and regulations are valid only when they are not contrary to the laws or the Constitution.[^civil-code] In [*People v. Maceren*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_32166_1977.html), the Supreme Court held that delegated rule-making power is confined to carrying into effect the enacted statute and cannot amend, extend or expand statutory requirements.[^180] When a discrepancy exists between the basic law and an implementing regulation, the basic law prevails. Declaring what acts constitute a crime and how they shall be punished is an exclusively legislative power. Because the fisheries statute did not expressly punish electro fishing and electric current is not an obnoxious or poisonous substance, the Department Secretary exceeded delegated authority and the administrative order is invalid.[^181]

# Quotes

> "Article 7 of the Civil Code embodies the basic principle that administrative or executive acts, orders and regulations shall be valid only when they are not contrary to the laws or the Constitution."[^182]

> "In case of discrepancy between the basic law and a rule or regulation issued to implement said law, the basic law prevails because said rule or regulation cannot go beyond the terms and provisions of the basic law (People vs. Lim, 108 Phil. 1091)."[^183]

> "Under Article 7 of the Civil Code, a law may be repealed only by a subsequent law."[^184]

> "Besides, a Central Bank Circular cannot repeal a law. Only a law can repeal another law. Article 7 of the Civil Code of the Philippines provides:"[^185]

> "In accordance therewith, Section 40 of RA 7160 is deemed to have repealed Section 12 of BP 881. Furthermore, Article 7 of the Civil Code provides that laws are repealed only by subsequent ones, and not the other way around. When a subsequent law entirely encompasses the subject matter of the former enactment, the latter is deemed repealed."[^186]

> "The general rule is that an unconstitutional law is void. It produces no rights, imposes no duties and affords no protection. It has no legal effect. It is, in legal contemplation, inoperative as if it has not been passed. Being void, Fertiphil is not required to pay the levy. All levies paid should be refunded in accordance with the general civil code principle against unjust enrichment. The general rule is supported by Article 7 of the Civil Code, which provides:"[^187]

> "As mandated by Article 7 of the Civil Code, an administrative rule or regulation cannot contravene the law on which it is based. RR 7-95 is inconsistent with Section 105 insofar as the definition of the term "goods" is concerned. This is a legislative act beyond the authority of the CIR and the Secretary of Finance. The rules and regulations that administrative agencies promulgate, which are the product of a delegated legislative power to create new and additional legal provisions that have the effect of law, should be within the scope of the statutory authority granted by the legislature to the objects and purposes of the law, and should not be in contradiction to, but in conformity with, the standards prescribed by law."[^188]

> "The general rule is that a void law or administrative act cannot be the source of legal rights or duties. Article 7 of the Civil Code enunciates this general rule, as well as its exception: “Laws are repealed only by subsequent ones, and their violation or non-observance shall not be excused by disuse, or custom or practice to the contrary. When the courts declared a law to be inconsistent with the Constitution, the former shall be void and the latter shall govern. Administrative or executive acts, orders and regulations shall be valid only when they are not contrary to the laws or the Constitution.”"[^189]

> "The doctrine of operative fact recognizes the existence of the law or executive act prior to the determination of its unconstitutionality as an operative fact that produced consequences that cannot always be erased, ignored or disregarded. In short, it nullifies the void law or executive act but sustains its effects. It provides an exception to the general rule that a void or unconstitutional law produces no effect. But its use must be subjected to great scrutiny and circumspection, and it cannot be invoked to validate an unconstitutional law or executive act, but is resorted to only as a matter of equity and fair play. It applies only to cases where extraordinary circumstances exist, and only when the extraordinary circumstances have met the stringent conditions that will permit its application."[^190]

> "Continued practice in domestic legal matters does not rise to the level of a legal obligation. The first sentence of Article 7 of the Civil Code states, "[l]aws are repealed only by subsequent ones, and their violation or non-observance shall not be excused by disuse, or custom or practice to the contrary." There can be no clear legal duty and clear legal right where to do so would compel DepEd to violate its power, duties, and functions under Section 7 of RA 9155, specifically toward the protection and promotion of the teachers' welfare. In the latter case, no practice, continued or otherwise, would establish and validate such clear legal duty and clear legal right."[^191]

> "Despite the lapse of more than thirty (30) years since this turf war began, Article 7 of the Civil Code states that a law's "violation or non­ observance shall not be excused by disuse, or custom or practice to the contrary." No amount of passage of time will validate the Municipality of Payao's patent infringement of the clear directive of P.D. 1239, in conjunction with local government laws and principles."[^192]

> "Concomitantly, a law that has been declared unconstitutional is deemed not to exist and results in the revival of the laws that it has repealed. Stated otherwise, an unconstitutional law returns us to the status quo ante and this return is beyond the power of the Court to stay."[^193]

> "Nevertheless, the effectivity and validity of a law is not dependent on its actual implementation. Article 7 of the Civil Code provides that the violation or non-observance of laws shall not be excused by disuse or custom or practice to the contrary."[^194]

> "Thus, the doctrine of operative fact is not a tool to legitimize noncompliance with rules, regulations, laws, or the Constitution, or to validate unlawful or unconstitutional acts. It can only be invoked by the party who acted in good faith, and cannot be used by a party directly responsible in the commission of an illegal or unlawful act."[^195]

# Tips

## Diagnostic Checklist

> [!TIP]
> **How to Analyze an Article 7 Problem Step by Step**
> 1. **Identify which paragraph of Article 7 is triggered.** Ask whether the dispute involves (a) an alleged repeal or long-standing contrary practice (paragraph 1), (b) a statute or legislative issuance clashing with the Constitution (paragraph 2) or (c) an administrative or executive issuance clashing with a statute or the Constitution (paragraph 3).
> 2. **Verify the legal rank of the later issuance.** Check whether it is a subsequent statute or martial law decree with statutory force (such as Presidential Decree No. 1 and Letter of Implementation No. 2 in [*People v. Gacott, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1995/mar1995/gr_116049_1995.html)), a Supreme Court procedural circular under Article VIII, Section 5(5) ([*First Lepanto Ceramics, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/mar1994/gr_110571_1994.html)) or merely an executive circular, speech or void ordinance that cannot repeal a statute ([*Palanca v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1994/dec1994/gr_106685_1994.html), [*People v. Aquino*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/jul1991/gr_39274_1991.html) or [*Sinag v. Sangguniang Panlalawigan ng Batangas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69836)).
> 3. **Test for implied repeal strictly.** Check for an express repealing clause, irreconcilable conflict or complete substitution of subject matter ([*Magno v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51590)). In penal statutes, apply the heightened presumption against implied repeal ([*People v. Purisima*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1978/nov1978/gr_42050_66_1978.html)).
> 4. **Compare every administrative rule word for word against its enabling statute.** Check whether the agency added a criminal offense ([*People v. Maceren*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_32166_1977.html)), restricted statutory definitions or beneficiaries ([*Fort Bonifacio Development Corp. v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49844), [*Bartolome v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/58177)), created new tax burdens ([*Bureau of Internal Revenue v. First E-Bank Tower Condominium Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66002)) or extended a non-extendible deadline ([*Partido Demokratiko Pilipino-Lakas ng Bayan (PDP-Laban) v. Commission on Elections En Banc*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68780)).
> 5. **Screen any operative fact plea against all strict conditions.** Confirm that (a) a party timely raised the doctrine below, (b) a formalized legislative, executive or covered judicial act existed prior to nullification, (c) the claimant relied in good faith without being the author of the illegality and (d) undoing prior effects would cause extraordinary inequity or irreversible destruction.

## Common Traps

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 1: Confusing Unformalized Practice With a Formal Executive Issuance**
> Parties frequently invoke the operative fact doctrine based on how an agency allegedly behaved in practice. In [*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257), the Supreme Court squarely rejected that theory because an unformalized administrative practice is neither a law nor an executive issuance. Under Article 7 paragraph 1, contrary practice never excuses noncompliance ([*Department of Education v. Rizal Teachers Kilusang Bayan for Credit, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65522)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 2: Treating Operative Fact as a Blanket Shield for Implementors or Refundable Money**
> Do not write in a bar exam answer that operative fact automatically saves everything done before a law is struck down. In [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044), the doctrine protected irreversible public projects benefiting good-faith recipients but did not protect DAP authors, proponents and implementors without concrete findings of good faith. In [*Duterte v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70257), the Supreme Court reiterated that the party directly responsible for an unconstitutional act cannot invoke operative fact. In [*Municipality of Tupi v. Faustino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586) and [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815), an illegal fine and private levies were ordered returned.

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 3: Assuming Every Administrative Amendment Violates Earlier Jurisprudence**
> When an earlier Supreme Court ruling merely interpreted a previous implementing rule rather than a statutory text, the agency remains free to amend its own implementing rule within statutory limits. In [*Pagpalain Haulers, Inc. v. Trajano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37312), Department Order No. 9 validly deleted the books-of-account registration requirement because the Labor Code itself did not require submission for registration.

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 4: Overlooking Revival of Repealed Laws and Statutory Codifications**
> When an amending or repealing statute is declared unconstitutional, the earlier statute it purported to repeal is revived automatically ([*Macalintal v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69031)). Also check whether a special statute regulates operative fact in the field, such as Section 246 of the National Internal Revenue Code in tax controversies ([*Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. San Roque Power Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56257)).

## What Worked and What Failed in Decided Cases

- **What worked to strike down an issuance or defeat a contrary practice:** Showing a direct textual clash between an agency issuance and the enabling statute succeeded in [*People v. Maceren*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_32166_1977.html), [*Juan v. Musñgi*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/oct1987/gr_l-76053_1987.html), [*Fort Bonifacio Development Corp. v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49844), [*Bartolome v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/58177), [*Bureau of Internal Revenue v. First E-Bank Tower Condominium Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66002), [*Abrenica v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653) and [*Partido Demokratiko Pilipino-Lakas ng Bayan (PDP-Laban) v. Commission on Elections En Banc*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68780). Producing the official text of a charter or an NSO census certification proved decisive in [*Municipality of Payao v. Municipality of Imelda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68647) and [*Sinag v. Sangguniang Panlalawigan ng Batangas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69836).
- **What worked to sustain equitable relief despite invalidity:** Demonstrating good-faith public or litigant reliance on a formal law, election resolution, executive project or judicial act succeeded in [*Araullo v. Aquino III*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57044), [*Partido Demokratiko Pilipino-Lakas ng Bayan (PDP-Laban) v. Commission on Elections En Banc*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68780), [*Macalintal v. Commission on Elections*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69031) and [*Office of the Court Administrator v. Castañeda*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri2024/oct2024/am_rtj-12-2316_2024.html). Demonstrating actual hazardous work performed under an irregular agency rate excused refund liability in [*Abrenica v. Commission on Audit*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68653).
- **What failed:** Invoking decades of non-observance, common local practice or prior official leniency failed in [*De Gallego v. Land Authority*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/aug1981/gr_26848_1981.html), [*Inciong v. De Guia*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1987/sep1987/am_r-249-rtj_1987.html), [*Department of Education v. Rizal Teachers Kilusang Bayan for Credit, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65522), [*Municipality of Payao v. Municipality of Imelda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68647) and [*Sinag v. Sangguniang Panlalawigan ng Batangas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/69836). Raising operative fact belatedly on appeal, for refundable fines, for private windfalls or to shield constitutional noncompliance failed in [*Planters Products, Inc. v. Fertiphil Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44815), [*Yap v. Thenamaris Ship's Management*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36914), [*Philippine Coconut Producers Federation, Inc. (COCOFED) v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/23897), [*Municipality of Tupi v. Faustino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586) and [*Duterte v. House of Representatives*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70257). Seeking release of consignated taxes without proving judicial consignation before the trial court failed in [*Bureau of Internal Revenue v. First E-Bank Tower Condominium Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66002). Seeking refunds for non-party motorists failed in [*Municipality of Tupi v. Faustino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65586).
