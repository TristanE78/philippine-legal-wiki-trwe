> ART. 19. Every person must, in the exercise of his rights and in the performance of his duties, act with justice, give everyone his due, and observe honesty and good faith.[^civil-code]

# Explanation

In brief, Article 19 of the Civil Code imposes a primordial limitation on all legal rights and duties by requiring every person to act with justice, to give everyone his due and to observe honesty and good faith.[^2] The provision lays down a rule of conduct rather than an independent cause of action.[^3] By itself, Article 19 does not grant damages unless coupled with [Article 20](art-0020.md) for acts contrary to law or [Article 21](art-0021.md) for willful acts contrary to morals, good customs or public policy.[^4] Bad faith is the core requirement in an abuse-of-rights claim and is never presumed.[^5]

## Nature and Elements of Abuse of Rights

The Code Commission drafted Article 19 to remedy the defect of the Spanish Civil Code of 1889 by infusing norms of good conscience into the exercise of rights.[^6] Under [*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html), even a legal right granted by statute or contract becomes the source of a legal wrong when exercised beyond the bounds of justice and good faith.[^7] The Supreme Court emphasized that Article 19 sets a standard of conduct that operates alongside [Article 20](art-0020.md) or [Article 21](art-0021.md) to furnish the remedy of damages.[^8] No rigid formula governs its application because courts examine the attendant circumstances of each dispute.[^9]

In [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html), the Supreme Court articulated the classic threefold test for abuse of rights under Article 19.[^10] First, there must be a legal right or duty.[^11] Second, that right or duty must be exercised in bad faith.[^12] Third, the act must be performed for the sole intent of prejudicing or injuring another person.[^13]

The Supreme Court refined how this three-part test applies in [*Tocoms Philippines, Inc. v. Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66168).[^14] Reconciling [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html)[^15] with [*University of the East v. Jader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59575)[^16] and [*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html),[^17] the Supreme Court clarified that bad faith is the crucial element of an Article 19 violation even when sole intent to injure is not mechanically shown.[^18] Because good faith is presumed, a party claiming abuse of rights bears the burden of proving bad faith by clear and convincing evidence.[^19] Bad faith implies a dishonest purpose, moral obliquity or conscious doing of a wrong rather than mere poor judgment or simple negligence.[^20]

## Statutory Boundaries With Articles 20, 21, 26 and 2176

Supreme Court decisions carefully separate Article 19 from related tort and contract provisions. In [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134), the Supreme Court held that Article 19 describes the degree of care required and is not by itself the basis of an actionable tort unless pleaded with [Article 20](art-0020.md) or [Article 21](art-0021.md).[^21] [Article 20](art-0020.md) reaches both willful and negligent violations of an existing statute.[^22] By contrast, [Article 21](art-0021.md) reaches only willful acts that violate no positive statute yet offend morals, good customs or public policy.[^23]

Where a complaint actually alleges a violation of an existing statutory right, [Article 20](art-0020.md) governs instead of Article 19. In [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367), the Supreme Court corrected the Court of Appeals and ruled that an employer's unilateral instruction to debit a resigned worker's personal bank account alleged a breach of ownership under [Article 429](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0429.md) actionable under [Article 20](art-0020.md).[^24] Conversely, where an alleged injury rests purely on negligence without a contractual relation or statutory violation, [Article 2176](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2176.md) governs rather than Articles 19, 20 and 21, as settled in [*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692).[^25]

Article 19 likewise yields to specific statutory rules and contract remedies. In [*Capitle v. Vda. de Gaban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46978), the Supreme Court held that Article 19 "supplements but does not supplant a specific provision of law" and cannot override 1889 Spanish Civil Code succession rules or acquisitive prescription after 67 years of exclusive possession.[^26] Similarly, in [*Equatorial Realty Development, Inc. v. Mayfair Theater, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34246), the Supreme Court noted that arbitrary withdrawal of an unaccepted offer may support a claim under Article 19. It held that a contractual right of first refusal inside a lease is enforced by rescission under [Article 1381](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1381.md) rather than Article 19 damages alone.[^27]

In [*Couples for Christ (CFC) School of the Morning Star v. Malonda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70275), the Supreme Court added that [Article 26](art-0026.md) on privacy and peace of mind also requires proof of bad faith or malice just like Articles 19 and 21.[^28] Nor does a suit for constitutional just compensation qualify as an Article 19 damages claim. In [*National Power Corp. v. Heirs of Macabangkit Sangkay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40020), the Supreme Court held that an action for just compensation for a subterranean tunnel, also called inverse condemnation, rests on the Constitution and differs from an action for damages. The five-year period for damages under Republic Act No. 6395 therefore did not bar it.[^29]

## Presumption of Good Faith and *Damnum Absque Injuria*

Because Article 19 is "not a panacea for all human hurts and social grievances,"[^30] losses flowing from the lawful and good-faith exercise of a right constitute *damnum absque injuria*. In [*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407), the Supreme Court rejected damages where a banquet organizer discreetly asked an uninvited actor to leave an invitation-only party and the claimant relied on mere conjectures of bias.[^31] Good-faith pursuit of civil or criminal remedies also stays outside Article 19 even when the suit fails, as the Supreme Court ruled in [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832)[^32] and [*Tan v. Valeriano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63274).[^33]

In commercial and banking relationships, reasonable steps to verify creditworthiness, audit compliance or enforce valid contract terms do not trigger liability. The Supreme Court absolved credit card issuers in [*BPI Express Card Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36305)[^34] and [*Pantaleon v. American Express International, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54483),[^35] protected a supplier exercising contractual audit and non-renewal rights in [*Dart Philippines, Inc. v. Spouses Calogcog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49588),[^36] cleared a bank employee who had no duty to steer a buyer to the proper loan division in [*Spouses Cabasal v. BPI Family Savings Bank, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67045)[^37] and absolved a developer in [*International Exchange Bank v. Rudy S. Labos and Associates, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68446).[^38] Extrajudicial repossession expressly authorized in an expired lease after repeated notices also avoids Article 19 liability, as held in [*CJH Development Corp. v. Aniceto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66462).[^39]

## Bad Faith in Contracts, Banking and Corporate Governance

Where a contracting party exercises legal rights dishonestly or to evade good-faith obligations under [Article 1159](../book-4-title-01-obligations/art-1159.md), Article 19 sanctions liability. Bad faith was established in [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134) where a buyer paid with a check drawn on a closed account and tried to shift liability to a third party,[^40] and in [*Sea Commercial Co., Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37724) where a supplier used its non-exclusive dealer's marketing work to underprice and bid directly against it.[^41] Relying on a pre-termination clause void under [Article 1308](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1308.md) without proving a ground for pre-termination supports actual damages but not moral damages absent bad faith, as held in [*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343).[^42]

In creditor-debtor and banking dealings, arbitrary refusal or undisclosed limits breach Article 19. In [*Go Cinco v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50379), a creditor's unjustified refusal to release a mortgage and accept approved refinancing proceeds did not extinguish the debt without consignation under [Article 1256](../book-4-title-01-obligations/art-1256.md) yet stopped continuing interest and triggered Article 19 damages.[^43] Banks incur similar liability when they terminate a credit line without contractually stipulated prior notice in [*Gonzales v. Philippine Commercial and International Bank*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52978),[^44] deduct insurance premiums while concealing policy coverage limits under [Article 1897](../book-4-title-10-agency/art-1897.md) in [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826)[^45] or stonewall payment directives in [*Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corp. Ltd. v. Catalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46208).[^46]

Equity and clean hands also restrict relief against bad-faith litigants and directors. Borrowers who procure three foreclosure postponements without republication cannot invoke that lack of republication to annul the auction, as ruled in [*Security Bank Corp. v. Spouses Martel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66868).[^47] Lessees in peaceful possession may not suspend rent outside [Article 1658](../book-4-title-08-lease/art-1658.md), as held in [*Reyes v. Arca*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1965/nov1965/gr_l-21447_1965.html).[^48] In [*Cebu Country Club, Inc. v. Elizagaque*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44566), directors who blackballed a membership application using an undisclosed unanimous-vote amendment and ignored inquiries became solidarily liable under Section 31 of the Corporation Code and Articles 19 and 21.[^49]

## Public Humiliation, Property, Utilities and Education

Even when an actor has probable cause or an ownership right under [Article 429](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0429.md), public humiliation or denial of fair notice crosses into Article 19 liability. In [*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html), probable cause under [Article 429](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0429.md) shielded a supermarket from exemplary damages after a shopper forgot to pay for a P3.85 file, yet mocking him publicly and imposing an unauthorized P5.00 fine violated Articles 19, 21 and [26](art-0026.md), with moral damages reduced under [Article 2214](../book-4-title-18-damages-1/art-2214.md).[^50] Similarly, baselessly branding a wedding coordinator a thief and searching her bag breached Articles 19 and 21 in [*Carpio v. Valmonte*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48395),[^51] while sending an accusatory letter to a shopper's employer despite an official receipt breached Article 19 in [*California Clothing, Inc. v. Quiñones*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56295).[^52]

Utility operators and adjacent landowners likewise violate Article 19 when acting arbitrarily. Abruptly disconnecting water service without fair notice or hearing violated Article 19 in [*Metropolitan Waterworks and Sewerage System v. Act Theater, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47088)[^53] and [*Metroheights Subdivision Homeowners Association, Inc. v. CMS Construction and Development Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64658).[^54] Obstructing a neighbor's sole street access with junk and a concrete structure while an easement suit was pending violated Articles 19 and [26](art-0026.md) in [*Naga Centrum, Inc. v. Spouses Orzales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62297).[^55] In education, letting a failing law student march at commencement without timely notice breached Articles 19 and [20](art-0020.md) in [*University of the East v. Jader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59575), though the student's failure to check his own grades barred moral damages.[^56]

## Employment, Union Strikes and Court Jurisdiction

Article 19 reinforces non-discrimination in compensation while respecting statutory boundaries between regular courts and labor tribunals. In [*International School Alliance of Educators v. Quisumbing*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53093), the Supreme Court cited the Constitution, international law, the Labor Code and Article 19 as part of the policy against inequality. It set aside a 25% salary differential favoring foreign-hires over local-hires doing equal work.[^57] When a plaintiff invokes Articles 19 and 20 without an employer-employee relationship or without a reasonable causal connection to Labor Code claims, regular courts retain jurisdiction, as ruled in [*Pioneer Concrete Philippines, Inc. v. Todaro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41994),[^58] [*Social Security System v. Ubaña*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61157)[^59] and [*Philippine Commercial International Bank v. Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61424).[^60]

Conversely, pleading Articles 19, 20 and 21 cannot divest Labor Arbiters of jurisdiction over damages directly arising from illegal dismissal, as settled in [*Victorias Milling Co., Inc. v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/aug1991/gr_66880_1991.html).[^61] Employees claiming under Article 21 must also come with clean hands, defeating a teacher's suit after she defied a Board reinstatement order in [*Garciano v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/aug1992/gr_96126_1992.html).[^62] In [*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519), the Supreme Court En Banc ruled that Articles 19 and [20](art-0020.md) allow strike damages only against specific individual officers or members proven by substantial evidence to have committed illegal acts in bad faith, excluding union-wide liability and [Article 2180](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2180.md) vicarious responsibility.[^63]

## Family Relations, Public Officers, Malicious Prosecution and Conflict of Laws

In personal and family relations, Article 19 combines with [Article 21](art-0021.md) and [Article 2219](../book-4-title-18-damages-1/art-2219.md) to redress willful deception and bad faith. Deceitful marriage promises inducing a woman to surrender her virginity violate Article 21 in [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html).[^64] Continuous fraud concealing a prior marriage supports moral damages for bigamy in [*Manuel v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42205),[^65] whereas an honest belief that a foreign divorce was valid defeats Article 19 damages in [*Mercado v. Ongpin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67004).[^66] A wife and guardian also violated Article 19 in [*Navarro-Banaria v. Banaria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66456) by intentionally withholding her infirm 90-year-old husband from his birthday party without notice after repeated assurances.[^67]

Public officers, mortgagees and complainants likewise face liability when abusing official power or legal process. A customs commissioner who defied a preliminary injunction and excluded a subordinate from centennial memorabilia violated Articles 19, 20 and 21 in [*Villanueva v. Rosqueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53481).[^68] Warrantless police seizure of a mortgaged motorcycle paired with public slander and groundless criminal charges breached Articles 19 and 20 in [*Uypitching v. Quiamco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40476).[^69] Prosecutions motivated by grudge and lacking probable cause incur damages in [*Sosmeña v. Bonafe*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66223).[^70] Finally, in conflict of laws, [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36425) applied the "State of the most significant relationship" test to hold Articles 19 and 21 actionable in Philippine courts where injury to a Manila-based Filipina crew member centered in the Philippines.[^71]

## Case Discussions

### [*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519): Individualized Strike Damages Under Articles 19 and 20

CASA was the bargaining representative of Central Azucarera's rank-and-file employees. It filed an unfair labor practice complaint on August 16, 2017 over the bargaining and the milling bonus. The Labor Arbiter dismissed the complaint on April 10, 2018 and denied the company's counterclaim for lack of merit. The NLRC affirmed in toto on June 29, 2018. The Court of Appeals agreed that the labor tribunals had no jurisdiction over the counterclaim, since it was not connected with an illegal dismissal case. It still held that the company was not entitled to the PHP 64,759,254.72 in actual damages it claimed for alleged strike blockade losses.[^72]

The Supreme Court En Banc denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals with modification. It held that Articles 19 and [20](art-0020.md) allow an employer to recover damages for illegal acts committed in bad faith during a strike. The employer must prove by substantial evidence the illegal acts, the bad faith and the resulting injury. Liability falls only on the officers or members who personally committed the acts and not on the union as a whole. [Article 2180](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2180.md) did not apply, because no provision extends vicarious liability to acts governed by Articles 19 and 20. The record did not show which individuals took part or that they acted in bad faith, so the counterclaim for actual, moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees failed.[^73]

### [*Couples for Christ (CFC) School of the Morning Star v. Malonda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70275): Bad Faith Required Under Articles 19 and 26

Subdivision residents sued the school for damages over noise from its campus and halls. The Regional Trial Court of Butuan City, Branch 3 dismissed Civil Case No. 7890 on June 25, 2021. It found that the school did not intend to prejudice the residents and acted in good faith by executing an Affidavit of Undertaking. The Court of Appeals reversed on August 24, 2023 and awarded abatement, PHP 500,000.00 in nominal damages and PHP 100,000.00 in attorney's fees.[^74]

The Supreme Court granted the school's petition, reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated the trial court's decision. It held that the noise came from legitimate school operations and was not a nuisance per se. The school had no intent to injure or vex the residents and undertook abatement measures that a CENRO Report of February 5, 2020 found successful. Articles 19, 20, 21 and [26](art-0026.md) all require malice or bad faith. The residents did not prove it. The Supreme Court said not every inconvenience gives rise to a cause of action for damages.[^75]

### [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826): Agency Non-Disclosure Under Articles 19 to 21 and Article 1897

The Regional Trial Court of Mariveles, Bataan, Branch 4 denied Miranda's main cause of action on June 21, 2010. It still ordered Land Bank to pay PHP 150,000.00 in moral damages, PHP 5,700.82 that was unduly deducted from the first loan proceeds, PHP 100,000.00 in attorney's fees and costs. The Court of Appeals affirmed on February 24, 2015 and denied reconsideration on September 28, 2015. Land Bank sought partial review in G.R. No. 220706 of the moral damages, attorney's fees and costs. Miranda filed a separate petition in G.R. No. 220986.[^76]

The Supreme Court held that the Court of Appeals committed no reversible error in sustaining the moral damages. It left the amount undisturbed because moral damages rest on the trial court's discretion. The attorney's fees and costs were just and equitable. The Supreme Court relied on an earlier ruling that rested on Articles 19, 20 and 21 in relation to [Article 1897](../book-4-title-10-agency/art-1897.md). It affirmed with modification by imposing 6% yearly interest on all monetary awards from finality.[^77]

### [*International Exchange Bank v. Rudy S. Labos and Associates, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68446): Bare Assertions Cannot Prove Bad Faith

International Exchange Bank, now Union Bank of the Philippines, sued Rudy S. Labos & Associates, Inc., the Labos spouses and Rockwell Land Corporation for a sum of money in Civil Case No. 05-313. The Regional Trial Court of Makati City, Branch 62 ordered the borrowers to pay Php 5,729,726.94 plus interest and fees on December 22, 2009 and absolved Rockwell. The Court of Appeals first reversed on November 23, 2011. It overturned itself on reconsideration in its December 20, 2012 Resolution and affirmed the trial court in toto. The bank sought to hold Rockwell solidarily liable for consenting to two successive condominium assignments.[^78]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed Rockwell's exoneration. It held that bare assertions of an ulterior motive fail the clear-and-convincing burden for Article 19 bad faith. Under Article 1207, solidary liability exists only when the obligation says so or the law or its nature requires it. Rockwell's solidary liability was neither stated nor required, so the Court of Appeals correctly refused to hold it jointly and severally liable with the borrowers.[^79]

### [*Spouses Cabasal v. BPI Family Savings Bank, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67045): Explaining Valid Bank Policy Without Bad Faith

The Cabasal spouses sued BPI and its employee Alma De Leon after a prospective buyer abandoned an assumption-of-mortgage deal. De Leon had told the buyer the deal was against bank policy. The Regional Trial Court of Parañaque City, Branch 274 decided the consolidated damages and annulment cases on December 1, 2011. It awarded P3,387,773.96 in actual, P200,000.00 in moral and P100,000.00 in exemplary damages. It also awarded P100,000.00 plus P2,000.00 per appearance in attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals reversed the damages award on February 15, 2017 and affirmed possession after foreclosure.[^80]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It held that Article 19 is not a panacea for all human hurts. It added that the act complained of must be shown to be done in bad faith or with intent to injure. De Leon was under no obligation to direct the buyer to the proper loan division. Her failure to do so was not negligence or bad faith and was not the proximate cause of the failed deal. The buyer was an engineer and an experienced businessman who should have known where to go. Because De Leon was not negligent or in bad faith, BPI was not vicariously liable under [Article 2180](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2180.md).[^81]

### [*Security Bank Corp. v. Spouses Martel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66868): Clean Hands and Foreclosure Postponement Estoppel

The Martel spouses signed a Real Estate Mortgage in favor of Security Bank on August 26, 1994 for a P26,700,000.00 principal debt. They thrice requested postponement of the foreclosure sale without republication. They then sued in Civil Case No. 03-1316 to void the P25,303,072.21 auction on a P34,645,909.44 claim. The Regional Trial Court of Makati City, Branch 134 first nullified the foreclosure in its August 5, 2014 Decision. It reversed itself in its December 22, 2014 Order and dismissed the complaint. The Court of Appeals reinstated the nullification on September 28, 2016.[^82]

The Supreme Court granted the bank's petition and reinstated the December 22, 2014 dismissal. It held that the spouses requested the rescheduling without republication as an underhanded tactic and then sued to nullify the auction. That showed bad faith. The Supreme Court agreed that the public and the spouses' creditors and heirs, if anyone, had a cause of action against a sale held without republication. The spouses were estopped from questioning the sale because they did not come to court with clean hands.[^83]

### [*Mercado v. Ongpin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67004): Good-Faith Mistake in Second Marriage Defeats Damages

Rene Ongpin married Alma Mantaring in Quezon City in 1972. Mantaring later obtained a divorce decree in Nevada. Believing he was free to marry, Ongpin married Mary Elizabeth Mercado in New Jersey on April 21, 1989. They separated on March 16, 2000. The marriage was later declared void. The trial court awarded Mercado P250,000.00 moral damages, P100,000.00 exemplary damages and P150,000.00 attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals deleted all three awards in its February 21, 2013 Decision.[^84]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the deletion on September 30, 2020. It held that malice or bad faith must be proved to sustain damages under Article 19. Contracting a void second marriage is not by itself actionable under Articles 19, 20 or 21. Ongpin believed in good faith that his divorce from Mantaring was valid. Mercado did not prove malice or deceit. The Supreme Court noted her December 19, 2019 entry of appearance with motion to dismiss.[^85]

### [*Navarro-Banaria v. Banaria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66456): Wife's Bad Faith Withholding Husband From Celebration

The respondents are relatives of a 90-year-old man and sued his wife, Navarro-Banaria, for damages. She had repeatedly confirmed that he would attend his milestone celebration. She then kept him away without notice. The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 216 ruled for the respondents on May 23, 2011 in Civil Case No. Q-0452212. The Court of Appeals affirmed with modification on October 15, 2014. It deleted the US$3,619.00 travel claims and reduced moral damages to P300,000.00 in total. It reduced exemplary damages to P30,000.00 and attorney's fees to P50,000.00.[^86]

The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals on July 28, 2020. It held that she acted in bad faith and with malice. It added that a spouse abuses a right when she uses it to inflict damage. It affirmed the P30,000.00 exemplary damages as an example for the public good. It also upheld the P50,000.00 attorney's fees because the respondents had to sue because of her intentional acts.[^87]

### [*CJH Development Corp. v. Aniceto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66462): Lawful Lease Repossession and Legal Advice Without Malice

Corazon Aniceto owned El Rancho Cafe and Restaurant on Camp John Hay land in Baguio City. She signed a Lease Contract with CJH Development on December 1, 2003 that ran until November 30, 2004. After the lease expired, CJH repossessed the premises and demolished the restaurant. She sued CJH and its lawyers for P2,183,625.00 in actual damages, P1,000,000.00 moral damages, P500,000.00 exemplary damages and P200,000.00 attorney's fees. The trial court awarded these sums. The Court of Appeals reversed and absolved the defendants under Article 19, but it still awarded P2,183,625.00 for her personal properties.[^88]

The Supreme Court denied Aniceto's petition and granted CJH's on July 6, 2020. It held that a lease stipulation letting the lessor take possession is valid even without judicial action. The entry and repossession were within CJH's contractual rights. The lawyers only advised their client to protect its interests and did not act in bad faith. The circumstances showed no malice or unjustifiable harm. The Supreme Court deleted the P2,183,625.00 award and ordered any remaining stored properties turned over to Aniceto.[^89]

### [*Sosmeña v. Bonafe*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66223): Fabricated Criminal Complaints Out of Grudge

Menandro Sosmeña managed Expo Logistics Philippines, Inc. Benigno Bonafe, Jimmy Escobar, Joel Gomez and Hector Pangilinan worked for him. After Benigno was asked to spy for a foreign partner, the relationship soured and the workers resigned. On February 4, 2002, Sosmeña filed criminal complaints against them in Pasay City, including malicious mischief and theft. The complaints were dismissed. The workers sued for malicious prosecution. The Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch 22 awarded P200,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and P25,000.00 attorney's fees on April 22, 2014. The Court of Appeals affirmed on June 30, 2017.[^90]

The Supreme Court denied the petition on June 8, 2020. It held that Articles 19, 20 and 21 and [Article 2219](../book-4-title-18-damages-1/art-2219.md)(8) support a malicious prosecution claim. The workers had to prove four elements by a preponderance of evidence. These include the absence of probable cause and legal malice. The Supreme Court found them proved. It reduced the awards to P30,000.00 moral damages, P20,000.00 exemplary damages and P10,000.00 attorney's fees for each respondent. All amounts earn 6% interest per annum.[^91]

### [*Tocoms Philippines, Inc. v. Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66168): Reconciling the Three-Element Test and Pleadings Review

Tocoms Philippines, Inc. sued Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc. and others for damages and injunction on February 4, 2013 in Civil Case No. 73779-TG. It alleged that Philips demanded a one-sided buy-back of its inventory. The terms were a 40% discount on phased-out models and a 60% discount on Class B products. It also alleged that Philips recalled the ICC stickers that let it sell stock. It claimed P20,000,000.00 in actual damages. The Regional Trial Court of Pasig City, Branch 266 denied the motion to dismiss. The Court of Appeals reversed on March 13, 2014.[^92]

The Supreme Court granted the petition on February 5, 2020. It cited Chevron Philippines, Inc. v. Mendoza on the three elements of abuse of rights. It also held that Article 19 may be invoked when a right is exercised in bad faith, even without a sole intent to injure. Bad faith is the crucial element. If the allegations are hypothetically admitted, they show bad faith under Article 19 and Article 21. Philips never had the chance to prove its good faith. The Supreme Court reinstated the case and ordered the trial court to try it with utmost dispatch.[^93]

### [*Metroheights Subdivision Homeowners Association, Inc. v. CMS Construction and Development Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64658): Water Disconnection Without Notice and Corporate Officer Shield

Metroheights Subdivision Homeowners Association, Inc. sued CMS Construction and Development Corporation, the Cruz family and the MWSS on June 29, 1992. It complained that they cut off its existing waterline during pipe-laying works. The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 77 ruled for the association on March 30, 1999. It awarded P190,000.00 actual, P100,000.00 nominal, P100,000.00 exemplary damages and P50,000.00 attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals reversed in its October 10, 2012 Decision.[^94]

The Supreme Court granted the petition on October 17, 2018. It held that having a right is different from the manner of exercising it. Article 19 sets the standard. MWSS and CMS Construction were jointly and severally liable. Only P161,541.85 of the actual damages was proved by checks. Nominal damages cannot coexist with actual damages. It awarded P100,000.00 exemplary damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees and costs, all with 6% interest from finality. The Cruzes were not liable personally because the association failed to show any act under Section 31 of the Corporation Code.[^95]

### [*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692): Pure Negligence Belongs to Article 2176, Not Articles 19 to 21

LWV Construction Corporation recruited Filipino workers for Saudi Arabia. St. Martin Polyclinic examined the applicant Raguindin and reported him fit for employment. He was deployed, but a Saudi clinic later found him positive for HCV. LWV sued for sum of money and damages. The Metropolitan Trial Court of Mandaluyong City, Branch 60 awarded P84,373.41 actual damages and P20,000.00 attorney's fees on December 17, 2010. The Regional Trial Court of Mandaluyong City, Branch 211 affirmed on December 15, 2011. The Court of Appeals deleted the actual damages on July 11, 2014 and awarded P50,000.00 temperate damages.[^96]

The Supreme Court granted the petition on December 4, 2017 and dismissed the complaint. It held that the lower courts wrongly relied on Articles 19, 20 and 21. The claim rested on mere negligence, with no breach of law or contract. It therefore falls under [Article 2176](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2176.md). LWV also failed to prove negligence. The foreign certification was wrongly admitted. The test results did not show that Raguindin had HCV when the clinic examined him.[^97]

### [*Tan v. Valeriano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63274): Honest Administrative Complaints Against Public Officer Not Malicious

Romeo Valeriano was a COA resident auditor and president of the Holy Name Society of Bulan, Sorsogon. He gave a welcome address at a conference on January 4, 2001. Municipal councilors, including Jose Tan and Orencio Luzuriaga, believed it was electioneering. They filed an administrative complaint with the Civil Service Commission on January 5, 2001. They filed another with the Ombudsman on March 22, 2001 and later refiled with the Commission. Valeriano sued for malicious prosecution. The Regional Trial Court of Bulan, Branch 65 awarded P300,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and P30,000.00 attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed on September 25, 2008, citing the refiling as bad faith.[^98]

The Supreme Court reversed on August 2, 2017 and dismissed the complaint. It noted that the Constitution and the Revised Administrative Code bar civil servants from partisan political activity. The councilors had reason to believe Valeriano was violating that ban. The complaints came from a reasonable belief and not from malice. Merely submitting a case for prosecution does not make one liable. Valeriano did not prove a sinister design. Good faith is presumed.[^99]

### [*Naga Centrum, Inc. v. Spouses Orzales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62297): Blocking Access Pending Right-of-Way Suit Violates Articles 19 and 26

Spouses Ramon and Nenita Orzales had used Rizal Street to reach Valentin Street since 1965. Naga Centrum, Inc. later closed the street and enclosed it. Its lot measures 1.9 hectares. The spouses sued for a right of way in Civil Case No. 2004-0036. While the case was pending, Naga Centrum blocked their access with earth fill, junk and a building. The Regional Trial Court of Naga City, Branch 22 granted the easement on December 23, 2008. It ordered the spouses to pay P200,000.00 as indemnity plus ten thousand pesos for damages. It also ordered Naga Centrum to remove its obstructing structures within 15 days. The Court of Appeals affirmed on May 23, 2012.[^100]

The Supreme Court denied the petition on September 14, 2016. It held that Naga Centrum knew the spouses had no other way out. It acted with gross and evident malice and bad faith when it blocked their access during the case. This violated Article 19 and [Article 26](art-0026.md). The Supreme Court refused to move the easement to a new site. That would reward malice and let a party manipulate the case through acts on the disputed property.[^101]

### [*Philippine Commercial International Bank v. Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61424): Civil Jurisdiction Over Arbitrary Pre-Decision Salary Deductions

Josephine Gomez was a PCIB teller. An impostor withdrew funds from a savings account. The bank paid P50,600.00 to the true depositor. PCIB then charged the loss to Gomez. It deducted from her salary and took half of her bonuses and profit sharing. It did not answer her letter asking for the basis. She sued for damages. The Regional Trial Court of Makati City, Branch 145 ruled for her on May 25, 1999. It awarded P5,006.00 actual damages with 12% interest, moral damages and attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed on May 23, 2011.[^102]

The Supreme Court denied the petition on November 23, 2015. It held that her claim rests on a tort under Article 19 in relation to Article 21. It has no reasonable causal connection with the claims that Article 224 of the Labor Code gives to Labor Arbiters. The regular courts therefore had jurisdiction. The bank also singled her out while other employees involved went unpunished. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals in toto.[^103]

### [*Social Security System v. Ubaña*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61157): Civil Jurisdiction Over Equal-Pay Claim Against Principal

Debbie Ubaña worked as a processor at the Social Security System for six years through service contractors. She received P5,038.00 monthly, or P229.00 daily. A regular processor received far more. She sued the DBP Service Corporation, the SSS and others in Civil Case No. 7304 on December 26, 2002. She claimed P572,682.67 as salary differential and P300,000.00 moral damages. The Regional Trial Court of Daet, Branch 39 dismissed the case for lack of jurisdiction on October 1, 2003. It reversed itself on March 6, 2007. The Court of Appeals affirmed on July 29, 2011.[^104]

The Supreme Court denied the petition on August 24, 2015. It held that no employer-employee relation existed between Ubaña and the SSS. She could therefore sue the SSS under Articles 19 and 20 to recover the salary due her. The Supreme Court called the pay gap unfair and cited the principle of equal pay for equal work. It remanded the case to Branch 39 for trial.[^105]

### [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134): Article 19 Coupled With Articles 20 and 1159 in Bad-Faith Breach

Dan T. Lim delivered scrap paper worth P7,220,968.31 to Arco Pulp and Paper Company in 2007. Arco Pulp paid with a check for P1,487,766.68 that was dishonored because the account was closed. It later claimed that a memorandum of agreement with a third party novated the debt. The Regional Trial Court of Valenzuela City, Branch 171 agreed and dismissed the complaint on September 19, 2008. The Court of Appeals reversed. It ordered Arco Pulp and its president to pay the debt with 12% interest, plus P50,000.00 each in moral damages, exemplary damages and attorney's fees.[^106]

The Supreme Court denied the petition in part on June 25, 2014. It held that no novation occurred because the creditor never consented to a new debtor. It held that Article 19 by itself is not the basis of an actionable tort. It sets the standard of care. A breach of contract in bad faith violates [Article 1159](../book-4-title-01-obligations/art-1159.md). It supports moral damages under [Article 2220](../book-4-title-18-damages-1/art-2220.md) and under Articles 19 and 20. Interest was reduced to 6% per annum from the May 5, 2007 demand.[^107]

### [*California Clothing, Inc. v. Quiñones*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56295): Sending Accusatory Letter to Customer's Employer

Shirley Quiñones, a Cebu Pacific Air ticketing agent, bought jeans at a Guess boutique in Cebu City on July 25, 2001. The store issued a receipt and released the item. Its staff later believed she had not paid. They confronted her at her office and sent a letter to her employer that described the incident. The Regional Trial Court of Cebu City, Branch 58 dismissed the complaint and the counterclaim on June 20, 2003. The Court of Appeals reversed on August 3, 2006. It ordered California Clothing and Michelle Ybañez to pay P50,000.00 moral damages and P20,000.00 attorney's fees jointly and solidarily.[^108]

The Supreme Court denied the petition on October 23, 2013. It agreed that asking her about payment was not wrong. But the letter to her employer was outrightly accusatory. It imputed deliberate non-payment despite the receipt and the release of the item. The staff meant to tarnish her reputation in her employer's eyes. That exceeded the limits of Article 19. The Supreme Court also applied Articles 20 and 21 and affirmed the awards.[^109]

### [*National Power Corp. v. Heirs of Macabangkit Sangkay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40020): Inverse Condemnation Distinct From Article 19 Damages

The Heirs of Macabangkit Sangkay found that the National Power Corporation had built a tunnel under their land in 1979 without telling them. The Regional Trial Court of Iligan City, Branch 1 ordered just compensation of P113,532,500.00. It also awarded rentals of P30,000.00 monthly, P200,000.00 moral damages, P200,000.00 exemplary damages and 15% attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed on October 5, 2004. NPC argued that the claim had prescribed under the five-year period in Section 3(i) of Republic Act No. 6395.[^110]

The Supreme Court affirmed with modifications on August 24, 2011. It held that an action for just compensation, also called inverse condemnation, differs from an action for damages. The first rests on the Constitution. The second rests on statute and arises from the violation of a right. The five-year period covers only damages actions, so the claim was not barred. The Supreme Court mentioned Article 19 only to describe damages actions. It deleted the rentals, the moral and exemplary damages and the 15% fees for lack of factual and legal bases. It imposed 12% interest from November 21, 1997. It fixed counsel's fees at 10% of the award for one lawyer and at P5,000.00 for another.[^111]

### [*Gonzales v. Philippine Commercial and International Bank*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52978): Failure to Give Prior Notice of Credit Line Termination

Eusebio Gonzales was a PCIB client for about 15 years. He signed three promissory notes for P1,800,000.00 as a borrower along with the Panlilio spouses, who received the proceeds. He also held a credit line and a foreign currency deposit of USD 8,715.72. PCIB froze the deposit and ended the credit line. His P250,000.00 check was then dishonored. The Regional Trial Court of Makati City, Branch 138 found him solidarily liable on the notes and found no fault in the bank. The Court of Appeals affirmed on October 22, 2007.[^112]

The Supreme Court partly granted the petition on February 23, 2011. It affirmed his solidary liability on the notes. But it reversed on the dishonor. The bank had not given the prior notice the credit line agreement required. It had also not told him about the termination. Failure to give the required prior notice was prima facie evidence of bad faith. The case fell within the abuse of rights principle in Article 19. The Supreme Court ordered PCIB to pay PhP 50,000.00 nominal damages, PhP 50,000.00 moral damages, PhP 10,000.00 exemplary damages and PhP 50,000.00 attorney's fees.[^113]

### [*Pantaleon v. American Express International, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54483): Credit Review Delay in Good Faith and Clean Hands

Polo Pantaleon toured Europe with his family and a tour group. On October 25, 1991, his wife chose diamond pieces worth US$13,826.00 at a shop in Amsterdam. ### [*Cebu Country Club, Inc. v. Elizagaque*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44566): Concealed Black-Ball Rule and Solidary Director Liability

Section 31 of the Corporation Code made them jointly and severally liable. It cut moral damages from P2,000,000.00 to P50,000.00 and exemplary damages from P1,000,000.00 to P25,000.00. ### [*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343): Pre-Termination Under Void One-Sided Contract Clause

### [*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407): Discreet Removal of Uninvited Party Guest

### [*Capitle v. Vda. de Gaban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46978): Article 19 Cannot Override Prescription or Succession Law

Hendry called him a crook and a swindler. The company filed six criminal complaints against him. The fiscal dismissed all of them. Hendry also wrote to RETELCO that Tobias had been dismissed for dishonesty, so Tobias could not get work there. The Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch 9 awarded P80,000.00 actual, P200,000.00 moral and P20,000.00 exemplary damages plus P30,000.00 attorney's fees.[^166]

The Supreme Court denied the petition on August 25, 1989 and affirmed the Court of Appeals. It held that the company may have had the right to dismiss him. But the abusive way it exercised that right amounted to a legal wrong under Article 19, enforced through Articles 20 and 21. Moral damages are recoverable under Article 21. Exemplary damages are justified when the acts are deliberate, malicious and in bad faith.[^167]

### [*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html): Probable Cause Negates Exemplary Damages Despite Public Shaming

Jose Espino picked up a P3.85 file in a Grand Union supermarket and forgot to pay for it at the counter. A guard stopped him at the exit and took him to the delivery area. The manager, Nelia Santos-Fandino, said Ano, nakaw na naman ito. She then took a P5.00 bill from him as a fine. The Court of Appeals reversed the trial court on September 26, 1977. It awarded P75,000.00 moral damages, P25,000.00 exemplary damages and P5,000.00 attorney's fees.[^168]

The Supreme Court modified the judgment on December 28, 1979. It agreed that Espino did not intend to steal. The false accusation was made before many people after he was detained and had a fine imposed. It caused him humiliation and made the store liable under Articles 19 and 21 in relation to Article 2219. The Supreme Court found the awards excessive because his own forgetfulness started the incident. It cut moral damages to P5,000.00 and attorney's fees to P2,000.00 and deleted the exemplary damages. It ordered return of the P5.00 fine.[^169]

### [*Reyes v. Arca*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1965/nov1965/gr_l-21447_1965.html): Unjust Rent-Free Occupancy Contrary to Article 19

Eusebio Millar leased three Manila lots at P320.00 monthly from the Basa spouses under a December 3, 1949 contract with an option to buy. The spouses sold the lots in 1953 to Jose Reyes, Soledad Reyes and Carmelita Reyes de Pastor. Millar sued to void the sale. His rental deposits with the court reached P20,000.00. He withdrew them in 1959 under a surety bond. The Court of First Instance of Manila then let him stop paying rent. The buyers asked the Supreme Court to set aside the orders of March 20 and April 6, 1963.[^170]

The Supreme Court granted the petition on November 29, 1965. It held that a lessee has a duty under [Article 1657](../book-4-title-08-lease/art-1657.md) to pay rent. None of the legal grounds for suspending it existed. The trial court's orders thus sanctioned the unjust enjoyment of another's property without payment. That ran counter to the rule in Article 19. The Supreme Court ordered Millar to deposit P16,640.00 for rentals from January 1959 to April 1963 and P320.00 for every month after.[^171]

## Key Cases

- [*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html): Established Article 19 as a rule of conduct enforced via Articles 20 or 21 even where an employer possesses the legal right to dismiss an employee.[^190]
- [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html): Formulated the classic three elements of abuse of rights and reversed damages where a creditor acted without bad faith after diligent checks.[^191]
- [*Tocoms Philippines, Inc. v. Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66168): Reconciled the three-element test with earlier rulings to underscore bad faith as the decisive element of an Article 19 claim.[^192]
- [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134): Clarified that Article 19 is not by itself an actionable tort without Article 20 or Article 21 and linked bad-faith contractual breach to Article 1159.[^193]
- [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367): Drew the boundary between Article 19 and Article 20 by holding that an alleged unlawful bank debit violates property rights under Article 429 and falls under Article 20.[^194]
- [*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692): Held that ordinary negligence lacking an existing statutory or contractual violation is governed by Article 2176 quasi-delict rather than Articles 19, 20 and 21.[^195]
- [*Capitle v. Vda. de Gaban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46978): Ruled that Article 19 supplements but does not supplant specific provisions of law such as succession statutes and acquisitive prescription.[^196]
- [*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407): Emphasized that Article 19 is not a panacea for all social hurts and fails without competent evidence of malice beyond conjecture.[^197]
- [*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519): Held En Banc that strike-related damages under Articles 19 and 20 fall solely on specific bad-faith individuals rather than the union collectively or through Article 2180 vicarious liability.[^198]
- [*Metropolitan Waterworks and Sewerage System v. Act Theater, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47088): Confirmed that proprietary rights under Article 429 remain subject to Article 19 standards of fairness and prior notice before utility disconnection.[^199]
- [*University of the East v. Jader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59575): Held a university liable under Articles 19 and 20 for failing to promptly inform a candidate of his failing grade while denying moral damages due to his own inaction.[^200]
- [*International School Alliance of Educators v. Quisumbing*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53093): Cited Article 19 with constitutional and international equality norms in setting aside unequal pay for equal work.[^201]
- [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html): Applied Article 21 to award damages for moral seduction achieved through a deceitful marriage promise and noted that Articles 19, 20 and 21 together broaden the law on civil wrongs.[^202]
- [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36425): Applied the "State of the most significant relationship" test to uphold Philippine court jurisdiction over an Article 19 and 21 tort claim involving foreign elements.[^203]

## Suggested Answers

### Problem 1

After supplying raw materials on credit, Seller received a partial payment check drawn by Buyer against a closed account and soon learned Buyer signed an agreement shifting delivery obligations to a third party without Seller's consent. Buyer invokes novation and argues that an ordinary breach of contract gives no right to moral damages under Article 19. Decide.

**Suggested Answer:** Buyer is liable for the unpaid balance plus moral and exemplary damages. In [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134), the Supreme Court held that issuing a check drawn against a closed account and attempting to shift contractual duties to a third party without the creditor's consent demonstrate clear bad faith.[^204] While Article 19 is not by itself the basis of an actionable tort, bad-faith non-compliance with a contract violates [Article 1159](../book-4-title-01-obligations/art-1159.md) and supports moral damages under Articles 19 and 20 alongside [Article 2220](../book-4-title-18-damages-1/art-2220.md).[^205]

### Problem 2

An accredited clinic issued a medical report certifying an overseas job applicant as "fit for employment." After deployment abroad, a foreign clinic reported that the worker tested positive for hepatitis C. The recruitment agency sued the Philippine clinic under Articles 19, 20 and 21 of the Civil Code to recover deployment expenses without alleging any contract or statutory violation. Will the action prosper under Article 19?

**Suggested Answer:** No, the action will not prosper under Articles 19, 20 and 21. In [*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692), the Supreme Court ruled that where an alleged negligent certification violates neither a pre-existing contract nor an existing statute, [Article 2176](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2176.md) on quasi-delict governs instead of Articles 19, 20 and 21.[^206] Because the claimant must prove fault at the time of the examination by competent evidence rather than unauthenticated foreign records, bare reliance on a later medical test cannot sustain liability.[^207]

### Problem 3

Following an employee's resignation, Bank A directed Bank B to debit the former employee's personal checking account in Bank B for a previously credited subsidy without her consent, causing her personal checks to bounce. When sued, Bank A moved to dismiss by arguing that the complaint failed to plead Article 19 bad faith or sole intent to injure. Should the complaint be dismissed?

**Suggested Answer:** No, the complaint should not be dismissed because it states a cause of action under [Article 20](art-0020.md) rather than Article 19. In [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367), the Supreme Court distinguished Article 19, which applies when no statute is violated, from [Article 20](art-0020.md), which supplies the sanction when a defendant's act violates a positive legal right.[^208] Ordering an unauthorized debit of a depositor's personal bank account alleges an unlawful interference with ownership under [Article 429](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0429.md) that must proceed to trial regardless of whether Article 19's elements are pleaded.[^209]

# Quotes

> "This article, known to contain what is commonly referred to as the principle of abuse of rights, sets certain standards which must be observed not only in the exercise of one's rights but also in the performance of one's duties. These standards are the following: to act with justice; to give everyone his due; and to observe honesty and good faith. The law, therefore, recognizes a primordial limitation on all rights; that in their exercise, the norms of human conduct set forth in Article 19 must be observed. A right, though by itself legal because recognized or granted by law as such, may nevertheless become the source of some illegality. When a right is exercised in a manner which does not conform with the norms enshrined in Article 19 and results in damage to another, a legal wrong is thereby committed for which the wrongdoer must be held responsible. But while Article 19 lays down a rule of conduct for the government of human relations and for the maintenance of social order, it does not provide a remedy for its violation. Generally, an action for damages under either Article 20 or Article 21 would be proper."[^210]

> "The elements of an abuse of right under Article 19 are the following: (1) There is a legal right or duty; (2) which is exercised in bad faith; (3) for the sole intent of prejudicing or injuring another. Article 20 speaks of the general sanction for all other provisions of law which do not especially provide for their own sanction (Tolentino, supra, p. 71)."[^211]

> "The foregoing discussion highlights bad faith as the crucial element to a violation of Article 19. The mala fide exercise of a legal right in accordance with Article 19 is penalized by Article 21, under which "[a]ny person who wilfully causes loss or injury to another in a manner that is contrary to morals, good customs or public policy shall compensate the latter for the damage." Stated differently, Article 19 imposes upon all persons exercising their legal rights the duty to act with justice, give everyone his due, and to observe honesty and good faith. Failure to discharge such duties is compensable under Article 20 if the act is "contrary to law"; and under Article 21 if the act is legal but "contrary to morals, good customs, or public policy.""[^212]

> "Article 19 is the general rule which governs the conduct of human relations. By itself, it is not the basis of an actionable tort. Article 19 describes the degree of care required so that an actionable tort may arise when it is alleged together with Article 20 or Article 21."[^213]

> "Whereas Article 19 provides for a cause of action for damages in cases when there is no law violated, the act causing damage being within rights or duties of defendant, Article 20 furnishes a general sanction for violations of provisions of law which do not especially provide their own sanction. The complaint clearly alleges a violation of respondent Trazo's property rights with respect to her checking account. Article 429 of the Civil Code provides that the owner or lawful possessor of the thing has the right to exclude any person from the enjoyment and disposal thereof."[^214]

> "Article 19 of the Civil Code in Chapter 2 on Human Relations is a statement of principle that supplements but does not supplant a specific provision of law."[^215]

# Tips

## Part 1: Diagnostic Checklist

> [!TIP]
> **Step 1: Identify Whether a Legal Right or Duty Exists or a Positive Statute Was Violated.** Before invoking the three-element test in [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html) and [*Tocoms Philippines, Inc. v. Philips Electronics and Lighting, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66168), ask whether the actor acted within an existing legal right or duty. Where the act itself violates a positive statutory rule such as ownership under [Article 429](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0429.md), [*Philippine Bank of Communications v. Trazo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43367) directs the claim straight to [Article 20](art-0020.md) rather than Article 19.

> [!TIP]
> **Step 2: Pair Article 19 With Its Proper Remedial Provision.** Never rely on Article 19 standing alone in a complaint or bar answer because [*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html) and [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134) hold that Article 19 states a standard of conduct without providing its own remedy. Pair Article 19 with [Article 20](art-0020.md) when the injurious conduct willfully or negligently violates a statute or contract as in [*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343) and [*Arco Pulp and Paper Co., Inc. v. Lim*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57134), or pair it with [Article 21](art-0021.md) when a lawful act is performed willfully in a manner contrary to morals, good customs or public policy as in [*Baksh v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/feb1993/gr_97336_1993.html) and [*Navarro-Banaria v. Banaria*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66456).

> [!TIP]
> **Step 3: Check Whether Pure Negligence or a Specific Statutory Remedy Governs Instead.** If the alleged injury rests on ordinary negligence without a pre-existing contract or statutory breach, [*St. Martin Polyclinic, Inc. v. LWV Construction Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63692) holds that [Article 2176](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2176.md) on quasi-delicts governs instead of Articles 19, 20 and 21. Likewise, check whether a specific statutory or contractual rule controls the dispute, because [*Capitle v. Vda. de Gaban*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46978) holds that Article 19 supplements rather than supplants succession and prescription statutes and [*Equatorial Realty Development, Inc. v. Mayfair Theater, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34246) enforces a contractual right of first refusal through rescission under [Article 1381](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1381.md).

> [!TIP]
> **Step 4: Screen Forum Jurisdiction and Clean Hands.** In workplace disputes, verify whether an employer-employee relationship exists and whether the tort claim is intertwined with illegal dismissal under Labor Code Article 217 as in [*Victorias Milling Co., Inc. v. Intermediate Appellate Court*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/aug1991/gr_66880_1991.html) or stands apart in regular civil courts as in [*Philippine Commercial International Bank v. Gomez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61424), [*Social Security System v. Ubaña*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61157) and [*Pioneer Concrete Philippines, Inc. v. Todaro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41994). Also check whether the claimant acted with clean hands or contributed to the injury under [*Garciano v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1992/aug1992/gr_96126_1992.html), [*Pantaleon v. American Express International, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54483), [*Security Bank Corp. v. Spouses Martel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66868), [*University of the East v. Jader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59575) and [*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html).

## Part 2: Common Traps and Misconceptions

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 1: Treating Every Hurt or Failed Lawsuit as an Abuse of Rights.** A frequent mistake is assuming that any social embarrassment, business loss or dismissed complaint automatically violates Article 19. In [*Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43407), the Supreme Court stressed that Article 19 is not a panacea for all human hurts and fails when supported only by conjectures. Similarly, [*ABS-CBN Broadcasting Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/29832), [*Albenson Enterprises Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1993/jan1993/gr_88694_1993.html) and [*Tan v. Valeriano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63274) confirm that filing a civil, criminal or administrative action in good faith is *damnum absque injuria* even if the action is later dismissed.

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 2: Confusing Legitimate Contract Enforcement With Bad Faith.** Exercising express contractual remedies to protect a business or enforce a valid policy is not actionable absent malice. The Supreme Court rejected Article 19 liability where a supplier conducted a second audit and required pre-paid orders in [*Dart Philippines, Inc. v. Spouses Calogcog*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49588), where credit card issuers suspended a delinquent card or reviewed an unusually large foreign charge in [*BPI Express Card Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36305) and [*Pantaleon v. American Express International, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/54483), where a lessor repossessed expired premises after repeated notices in [*CJH Development Corp. v. Aniceto*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66462) and where a bank assistant bluntly stated bank policy against mortgage assumptions in [*Spouses Cabasal v. BPI Family Savings Bank, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67045).

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 3: Assuming Collective Union Liability or Automatic Moral Damages in Contract and Marriage Cases.** In strike-related counterclaims, [*Central Azucarera De San Antonio, Inc. v. CASA Sugar Mill Workers Union-FFW*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70519) warns that Articles 19 and 20 never impose collective union liability or [Article 2180](../book-4-title-17-extra-contractual-obligations/art-2180.md) vicarious liability without substantial proof of individual bad-faith acts. In void second marriages, [*Mercado v. Ongpin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67004) shows that an honest belief in a prior foreign divorce defeats Article 19 damages, unlike the deliberate concealment of a subsisting marriage in [*Manuel v. People*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42205). In contract pre-termination, [*GF Equity, Inc. v. Valenzona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43343) shows that relying on a stipulation void under [Article 1308](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1308.md) supports actual damages and attorney's fees under Articles 19 and 20 without moral or exemplary damages.

## Part 3: What Worked and What Did Not in Real Cases

> [!TIP]
> **What Worked for Claimants:** Claimants prevailed under Article 19 when they proved concrete acts of dishonesty, defiance of court or contract duties, or public humiliation beyond the actor's legitimate right. Liability attached when an employer verbally maligned a worker, filed six baseless criminal cases and sent a blackball letter in [*Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. Court of Appeals*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/aug1989/gr_81262_1989.html), when a mortgagee seized a motorcycle with police and shouted thief in [*Uypitching v. Quiamco*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40476), when a former employer filed false criminal complaints out of grudge in [*Sosmeña v. Bonafe*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66223) and when a customs head defied a preliminary injunction in [*Villanueva v. Rosqueta*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53481). Claimants also succeeded where a store sent an accusatory letter to a buyer's employer despite an official receipt in [*California Clothing, Inc. v. Quiñones*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56295), where a person who lost jewelry publicly branded a coordinator a thief in [*Carpio v. Valmonte*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48395), where utilities cut off water without fair notice in [*Metropolitan Waterworks and Sewerage System v. Act Theater, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47088) and [*Metroheights Subdivision Homeowners Association, Inc. v. CMS Construction and Development Corp.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64658), where a landowner blocked a street during litigation in [*Naga Centrum, Inc. v. Spouses Orzales*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/62297) and where banks, principals or directors concealed insurance limits, terminated credit without required notice, refused refinancing tender, undercut a dealer or blackballed a member in [*Land Bank of the Philippines v. Miranda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68826), [*Gonzales v. Philippine Commercial and International Bank*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/52978), [*Go Cinco v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50379), [*Sea Commercial Co., Inc. v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37724) and [*Cebu Country Club, Inc. v. Elizagaque*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44566). Claimants also prevailed where unequal pay was set aside in [*International School Alliance of Educators v. Quisumbing*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53093) and where a lessee's rent suspension was set aside in [*Reyes v. Arca*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1965/nov1965/gr_l-21447_1965.html). Claimants kept their Article 19 claims alive in [*Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corp. Ltd. v. Catalan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46208) and [*Saudi Arabian Airlines v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36425).

> [!TIP]
> **What Did Not Work for Claimants:** Claims failed whenever the plaintiff relied on bare allegations of ulterior motive as in [*International Exchange Bank v. Rudy S. Labos and Associates, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68446), complained of licensed campus noise mitigated in good faith as in [*Couples for Christ (CFC) School of the Morning Star v. Malonda*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70275) or sought rentals, moral damages and exemplary damages without factual and legal bases as in [*National Power Corp. v. Heirs of Macabangkit Sangkay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40020). Even where a defendant acted wrongfully, claimant fault eliminated or reduced moral and exemplary damages in [*University of the East v. Jader*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59575) and [*Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1979/dec1979/gr_l-48250_1979.html).
