> ART. 476. Whenever there is a cloud on title to real property or any interest therein, by reason of any instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding which is apparently valid or effective but is in truth and in fact invalid, ineffective, voidable, or unenforceable, and may be prejudicial to said title, an action may be brought to remove such cloud or to quiet the title.
>
> An action may also be brought to prevent a cloud from being cast upon title to real property or any interest therein.[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 476 of the Civil Code grants an owner or holder of an interest in real property a civil remedy to remove a cloud on title or to prevent a cloud from being cast upon that title. The first paragraph covers existing clouds arising from any instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding that appears valid or effective on its face but is actually invalid, ineffective, voidable or unenforceable and may prejudice the title. The second paragraph authorizes a preventive action to stop a cloud from being cast upon title to real property or any interest in it.

1. **Title requirement:** The plaintiff must hold legal title (registered ownership) or equitable title (beneficial ownership) to the real property involved.
2. **Cloud requirement:** The contested instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding must appear valid on its face yet be shown in fact to be invalid, ineffective, voidable or unenforceable and prejudicial to the plaintiff's title.
3. **Excluded disputes:** Physical intrusion, boundary disputes and titles covering an entirely different parcel of land are not clouds removable under Article 476.
4. **Jurisdiction and prescription:** Quieting of title is a *quasi in rem* real action whose trial-court jurisdiction depends on assessed value under Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 as amended by Republic Act No. 7691. It prescribes in thirty years when the plaintiff is out of possession and is imprescriptible while the plaintiff remains in possession.

The sections below explain the nature of the remedy, its two indispensable requisites, what qualifies as a cloud, the scope of the second paragraph, procedural and jurisdictional limits, prescription and laches, individual case discussions, related provisions, key cases and suggested answers.

## Nature, Origin and Quasi in Rem Character

Quieting of title is a common law remedy originating in equity jurisprudence for the removal of any cloud, doubt or uncertainty affecting title to real property, as explained in [*Baricuatro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258)[^2] and [*Calacala v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772).[^3] Its purpose is to secure an adjudication that an adverse claim of title or interest is invalid so that the complainant and those claiming under the complainant may remain free from hostile claims.[^4] In this proceeding, the competent court determines the respective rights of the parties to place things in their proper place so that the rightful owner may introduce improvements and enjoy the property without fear.[^5]

Suits to quiet title are characterized as proceedings *quasi in rem*, as held in [*Spouses Portic v. Cristobal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070),[^6] [*San Pedro v. Ong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47626)[^7] and [*Phil-Ville Development and Housing Corp. v. Bonifacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462).[^8] Technically, they are neither *in rem* nor *in personam* because an individual is named as defendant to subject that person's interest to the assailed claim over the property.[^9] Unlike an *in rem* judgment, a judgment in a *quasi in rem* action to quiet title is conclusive only between the parties who joined in the action and does not cut off the rights of all possible claimants.[^10]

Under Rule 63 of the Rules of Court, an action to quiet title is grouped with declaratory relief and similar remedies, as stated in [*Spouses Velarde v. Heirs of Candari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829)[^11] and [*Malana v. Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941).[^12] In [*Spouses Velarde*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829), the Supreme Court explained that declaratory remedies under Rule 63 center on the validity or construction of written instruments before breach and generally do not require execution because they declare the parties' rights and duties.[^13] Even when a complaint is captioned as quieting of title, the true nature of the action is governed by the material allegations in the pleading and the relief warranted by proof rather than by its caption, as applied in [*Spouses Velarde*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829)[^14] and [*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617).[^15]

## Two Indispensable Requisites

Read together with Article 477 of the Civil Code, Article 476 requires two indispensable elements to concur for an action to quiet title to prosper, as held in [*Calacala v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772),[^16] [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa v. Heirs of Jose Bacud*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822),[^17] [*Heirs of Manuel Eñano v. San Pedro Cineplex Properties, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68375)[^18] and [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation, Inc. v. Mabalacat Institute, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168).[^19] First, the plaintiff or complainant must have a legal or equitable title to or interest in the real property subject of the action. Second, the deed, claim, encumbrance or proceeding claimed to be casting a cloud on that title must be shown to be in fact invalid or inoperative despite its *prima facie* appearance of validity or legal efficacy.[^20] Absence of either requisite defeats the complaint.[^21]

### Legal or Equitable Title or Interest

In an action to quiet title, legal title denotes registered ownership while equitable title denotes beneficial ownership, as stated in [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822),[^22] [*Heirs of Deloy v. Basa-Joaquin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68698)[^23] and [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168).[^24] Equitable title is derived through a valid contract or relation based on recognized equitable principles and gives the holder the right to have legal title transferred.[^25] Lack of a Torrens certificate of title does not automatically bar the suit because the term title in Article 476 does not necessarily refer to an original or transfer certificate of title, as established in [*Spouses Portic v. Cristobal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070).[^26]

Plaintiffs successfully establish the first requisite when they hold valid ownership or beneficial rights recognized by law. In [*Spouses Portic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070), vendors who reserved ownership under a contract to sell retained title despite the issuance of a transfer certificate of title to a buyer who had not paid the full price.[^27] In [*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314), buyers under a notarized 1991 deed of absolute sale duly annotated on the seller's certificate of title sufficiently alleged equitable title to sustain an action to quiet title.[^28]

By contrast, the first requisite fails when the plaintiff's claimed source of title is void, extinguished or unproved:

- **Void free patent over private land:** In [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822), a free patent and original certificate of title issued over land already segregated from the public domain by private open, continuous, exclusive and notorious possession were null and void and conferred no legal title.[^29]
- **Unredeemed execution sale:** In [*Calacala v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772), former owners who failed to redeem forfeited property within one year from registration of the sheriff's certificate of sale lost all legal and equitable title under Section 33, Rule 39 of the Rules of Court even though the government did not secure a final deed within ten years.[^30]
- **Void simulated donation:** In [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168), an unnotarized deed of sale concealing a gratuitous donation of real property was void under Article 749 for lack of a public document and conferred no equitable title against the registered owner.[^31]
- **Void reconstituted title and bare tax declarations:** In [*Heirs of Deloy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68698), transfer certificates of title derived from a void reconstituted title could not prove a valid transfer without proof of sale or good faith for value. Tax declarations alone did not prove ownership.[^32] Similarly, in [*Heirs of Manuel Eñano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68375), a cancelled certificate of title with no Land Registration Authority microfilm record and a tax declaration failed to prove legal or equitable title.[^33]

### An Apparently Valid Yet Actually Invalid or Inoperative Cloud

Under the second requisite, a cloud on title exists when four elements concur, as enumerated in [*Phil-Ville Development and Housing Corp. v. Bonifacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462)[^34] and [*Green Acres Holdings, Inc. v. Cabral*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050):[^35] (1) there is an instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding, (2) it is apparently valid or effective, (3) it is in truth and in fact invalid, ineffective, voidable or unenforceable and (4) it may be prejudicial to the title sought to be quieted. Decisions such as [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822)[^36] and [*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617)[^37] also fold in Article 478 by noting that the third element includes an instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding that is extinguished, terminated or barred by extinctive prescription.

If the adverse party's instrument or title is actually valid and operative, no cloud exists and the action must be dismissed. In [*Desiderio Dalisay Investments, Inc. v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64119), the Supreme Court held that a perfected and consummated *dacion en pago* under Article 1245 vested valid ownership in the Social Security System, making its claim valid and operative rather than a cloud.[^38] In [*Heirs of Manuel Eñano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68375), the respondent established the complete genealogy of its transfer certificates of title from the mother original certificate of title, proving that its titles were genuinely valid and not a cloud.[^39] In [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822), a notarized 1963 affidavit enjoyed the presumption of regularity and was not proved invalid by unsubstantiated testimony of force and intimidation.[^40] In [*Calacala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772), the petitioners admitted the validity of the registered sheriff's certificate of sale, defeating the second requisite.[^41]

Even an invalid instrument does not constitute a cloud if the fourth element of prejudice is missing. In [*Phil-Ville*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462), although Eleuteria Rivera's transfer certificate of title was invalid, technical descriptions and a geodetic plan proved it covered Lot 23 far west of Phil-Ville's Lot 23-A, so it was not prejudicial to Phil-Ville's titles.[^42] In [*Spouses Yu v. Topacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769), a verification survey likewise proved that the defendants' valid transfer certificate of title covered a parcel 1,526 meters away from the plaintiff's lot, leaving no cloud to remove.[^43]

## What Constitutes a Cloud and What Does Not

### Instruments, Records, Claims, Encumbrances and Proceedings

In [*Green Acres Holdings, Inc. v. Cabral*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050), the Supreme Court defined the four statutory sources of a cloud under Article 476.[^44] An instrument is a document or writing that gives formal expression to a legal act or agreement to create, secure, modify or terminate a right. A record is a written account of an act, court proceeding, transaction or instrument drawn up under authority of law by a proper officer as permanent evidence. A claim is a cause of action or a demand for money or property. A proceeding is a regular and orderly progress in form of law covering steps in an action from commencement to execution of judgment, including a procedural step within a larger action or special proceeding.[^45]

Applying these categories, the Supreme Court has recognized the following as clouds removable under Article 476:

- **Unenforceable quasi-judicial decisions:** In [*Green Acres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050), a final Department of Agrarian Reform Adjudication Board decision cancelling a predecessor's titles satisfied all four statutory categories and was unenforceable against an innocent purchaser for value who was not impleaded and had no annotated notice of *lis pendens*.[^46] In [*Almazan v. Bacolod*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67520), Provincial Agrarian Reform Adjudicator and Department of Agrarian Reform Adjudication Board decisions rendered against strangers without any tenancy relationship with the registered owners similarly constituted alleged clouds cognizable by the Regional Trial Court.[^47]
- **Notice of *lis pendens*:** In [*Du v. Ortile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68441), the Supreme Court held under Article 476 that annotating a notice of *lis pendens* necessarily creates a cloud on the registered owner's certificate of title that puts the property in limbo and hinders disposition, supporting the requirement that the registered owner be impleaded in the pending suit.[^48]
- **Mortgages and foreclosures based on forged powers of attorney:** In [*San Pedro v. Ong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47626), annotated real estate mortgages executed without the registered owners' authority through forged special powers of attorney were encumbrances casting a removable cloud under Article 476.[^49] In [*Bank of Commerce v. Spouses San Pablo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215), quoting [*Baricuatro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258) on Article 476, an unauthorized mortgage annotated on the owners' certificate of title likewise cast a cloud removable through quieting of title.[^50]
- **Title derived from a forged extrajudicial settlement:** In [*Ocampo v. Ocampo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63159), quoting [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822) on Articles 476 and 477, a transfer certificate of title issued through a forged extrajudicial settlement with waiver cast a cloud on a co-owner's title.[^51]
- **Title issued on a construction permit or through bad-faith double registration:** In [*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617), a transfer certificate of title overlapping private land and a river based on a mere special work order or construction permit was void *ab initio* and cast a cloud under Article 476.[^52] In [*Baricuatro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258), transfer certificates of title registered in bad faith by subsequent buyers with notice of an earlier installment buyer's purchase and possession were nullified in an action to quiet title.[^53]
- **Stale execution levy and allegedly void execution sale:** In [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html), an annotated notice of embargo became unenforceable after the ten-year period to enforce the judgment expired without an execution sale and became a cloud removable under Articles 476 and 478.[^54] In [*Spouses Ragasa v. Spouses Roa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559), allegations of an execution sale conducted without notice to occupants and at a grossly inadequate price sufficiently stated a cause of action to quiet title under Article 476.[^55]

### Boundary Disputes, Physical Intrusion and Non-Overlapping Titles

Not every disturbance of real property rights creates a cloud under Article 476. In [*Vda. de Aviles v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34239), the Supreme Court held that quieting of title is not the proper remedy to settle a boundary dispute where both sides admit their partition agreement and contest only the measurement of their adjoining shares.[^56] Constructing a bamboo fence and moving earthen dikes are not clouds removable under Article 476. A court in a quieting of title action cannot fix boundaries using *evidence aliunde*.[^57]

Physical acts of dominion likewise do not constitute a cloud. In [*Spouses Velarde v. Heirs of Candari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829), instituting tenants, collecting rentals and appropriating produce were acts of physical intrusion rather than a cloud on title under Article 476, making the suit an *accion reivindicatoria*.[^58] Finally, where competing certificates of title cover entirely distinct parcels without overlapping technical descriptions, as in [*Phil-Ville*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462)[^59] and [*Spouses Yu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769),[^60] the defendant's certificate of title does not prejudice or cloud the plaintiff's title.

### Scope of the Second Paragraph on Preventing a Cloud

The second paragraph of Article 476 authorizes an action to prevent a cloud from being cast upon title to real property or any interest in it.[^civil-code] None of the Supreme Court decisions cited on this page directly applied the second paragraph to enjoin a threatened or future cloud before it was created. Rather, the second paragraph was only quoted alongside the first paragraph in [*Aroc v. People's Homesite and Housing Corp.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1978/jan1978/gr_39674_1978.html),[^61] [*Vda. de Aviles*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34239),[^62] [*Baricuatro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258),[^63] [*Spouses Portic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070),[^64] [*Calacala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772),[^65] [*Spouses Ragasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559),[^66] [*Bank of Commerce*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215) (quoting [*Baricuatro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258)),[^67] [*San Pedro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47626),[^68] [*Eland Philippines, Inc. v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53658) (quoting [*Calacala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772)),[^69] [*Phil-Ville*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462),[^70] [*Green Acres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050),[^71] [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822),[^72] [*Ocampo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63159) (quoting [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822)),[^73] [*Desiderio Dalisay Investments*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64119),[^74] [*Spouses Yu*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769),[^75] [*Tensuan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617),[^76] [*Almazan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67520),[^77] [*Heirs of Manuel Eñano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68375),[^78] [*Heirs of Deloy*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68698)[^79] and [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168).[^80]

## Jurisdiction, Procedure and Relation to Other Remedies

### Assessed-Value Jurisdiction, Rule 63 and Agrarian Boundaries

An action for quieting of title is a real action whose subject-matter jurisdiction is governed by the assessed value of the real property under Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 as amended by Republic Act No. 7691, as held in [*Bank of Commerce v. Spouses San Pablo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215)[^81] and [*Malana v. Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941).[^82] In [*Malana*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941), the Supreme Court explained that Section 1, second paragraph of Rule 63 uses the permissive word may for actions to quiet title authorized by Articles 476 to 481.[^83] It must therefore be read together with the mandatory provision of Section 33(3) of Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 as amended by Republic Act No. 7691, which gave Municipal Trial Courts exclusive original jurisdiction where the assessed value did not exceed P20,000.00 outside Metro Manila or P50,000.00 in Metro Manila.[^84] Because the assessed value in [*Malana*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941) was only P410.00, the Regional Trial Court properly dismissed the complaint *motu proprio* without prejudice for lack of jurisdiction.[^85] Likewise, in [*Bank of Commerce*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215), an assessed value of P4,900.00 placed the quieting of title action within the Municipal Trial Court's jurisdiction.[^86]

Where declaratory relief is sought under Section 1 of Rule 63, the action presupposes that no breach or violation of rights has occurred yet, as stated in [*Malana*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941)[^87] and [*Phil-Ville*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462).[^88] In [*Malana*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941), declaratory relief was unavailable because the occupants had already refused a demand to vacate and asserted ownership before the barangay conciliation body, making *accion publiciana* or *accion reivindicatoria* the proper remedy.[^89] By contrast, in [*Phil-Ville*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462), the plaintiff sued after receiving a notice to vacate but before actual dispossession and prayed only for validation of its titles, so declaratory relief under Rule 63 was properly granted even though the fourth element of a cloud under Article 476 was missing.[^90]

Regular courts retain jurisdiction over quieting of title suits involving agrarian reform decisions when no tenancy relationship exists between the litigants. In [*Almazan v. Bacolod*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67520), the Supreme Court held that the Department of Agrarian Reform Adjudication Board's jurisdiction under Section 50 of Republic Act No. 6657 is limited to agrarian disputes requiring proof of all six elements of a tenancy relationship.[^91] Because the complaint alleged no landlord-tenant relationship, consent or harvest sharing between the registered owners and the occupants, the Regional Trial Court had jurisdiction to try the quieting of title action.[^92] In [*Green Acres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050), an innocent purchaser for value who was not impleaded in the agrarian proceedings was likewise allowed to pursue quieting of title. The Supreme Court treated the final agrarian board decision as a cloud removable in that action.[^93]

### Quasi in Rem Summons, Co-Owner Standing, Summary Judgment and Res Judicata

Because quieting of title is *quasi in rem*, jurisdiction over the defendant's person is not a prerequisite to confer jurisdiction on the court once it acquires jurisdiction over the *res* by the institution of the suit, as held in [*San Pedro v. Ong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47626).[^94] Defective substituted service of summons on a non-indispensable party who fraudulently mortgaged the land merely infringes that party's due process right and bars a personal money judgment against that party without voiding the entire quieting of title proceedings.[^95]

Any co-owner may bring an action to quiet title for the benefit of all co-owners without impleading the other co-owners so long as the plaintiff does not repudiate the co-ownership, as ruled in [*Heirs of Manuel Eñano*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68375).[^96] If a co-owner repudiates the co-ownership by claiming sole ownership or sues against another co-owner, the other co-owners become indispensable parties.[^97]

Procedural shortcuts cannot substitute for proof of the requisites of Article 476. In [*Eland Philippines, Inc. v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53658), quoting [*Calacala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772) on Articles 476 and 477, the Supreme Court held that summary judgment is improper where the parties genuinely dispute the factual allegations establishing title and the invalidity of the alleged cloud.[^98] Conversely, in [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168), a demurrer to evidence properly dismisses a quieting of title complaint on the merits when the plaintiff's own evidence fails to establish equitable title or impugn the defendant's Torrens title.[^99]

A final judgment in a quieting of title suit has bounded preclusive effect. In [*Aroc v. People's Homesite and Housing Corp.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1978/jan1978/gr_39674_1978.html), the Supreme Court held that a prior final judgment in an Article 476 quieting of title case does not constitute *res judicata* barring a subsequent action to annul a government housing award and sale where the grantor agency was not impleaded and the second suit requires additional proof of charter violations and awardee disqualification.[^100] A judgment in an action to quiet title is not conclusive as to matters neither put in issue nor determined in that action.[^101]

### Distinction from Accion Reivindicatoria, Reconveyance, Review of Registration Decree and Collateral Attack

The Supreme Court carefully distinguishes quieting of title under Article 476 from neighboring real property remedies:

- **Recovery of possession (*accion reivindicatoria*) and reconveyance:** In [*Spouses Yu v. Topacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769), the Supreme Court explained that quieting of title removes an invalid cloud under Articles 476 and 477, recovery of possession under Article 434 requires proof of ownership and identity of the property and reconveyance compels transfer of wrongfully registered land to the rightful owner.[^102] Thus, denying quieting of title and reconveyance while granting recovery of possession for physical encroachment is not contradictory.[^103] Similarly, in [*Spouses Velarde*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829), a complaint grounded on physical intrusion was adjudicated as *accion reivindicatoria*.[^104]
- **Petition for review of a decree of registration within one year:** In [*Eland Philippines*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53658), where the one-year period from entry of the decree of registration under Section 32 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 had not yet elapsed, the Supreme Court held that quieting of title was not the proper remedy because the aggrieved party should file a petition for review of the decree of registration based on actual fraud.[^105]
- **No collateral attack when disputing ownership in defense:** In [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822), the Supreme Court ruled that raising the invalidity of the plaintiff's free patent and original certificate of title as an affirmative defense in an answer is an attack on ownership rather than a prohibited collateral attack on the certificate of title.[^106]
- **Combined reliefs in one complaint:** In [*Tensuan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617), a complaint captioned as *accion reivindicatoria* and annulment of title properly encompassed three-pronged relief for quieting of title under Article 476, reconveyance and annulment of the overlapping void titles.[^107]

## Prescription and Laches

As a general rule, an action for quieting of title is a real action that prescribes thirty years after the cause of action accrues when the plaintiff is not in possession of the property, as held in [*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314).[^108] In [*Gatmaytan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314), the cloud accrued on February 22, 1996 when the subsequent buyer obtained a Torrens title, so the complaint filed eighteen years later on December 10, 2014 had not prescribed and belated payment of any docket fee deficiency could still be permitted.[^109]

By way of exception, an action to quiet title involving property in the actual possession of the plaintiff is imprescriptible, as applied in [*Spouses Portic v. Cristobal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070),[^110] [*Spouses Ragasa v. Spouses Roa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559),[^111] [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822),[^112] [*Gatmaytan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314)[^113] and [*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617).[^114] In [*Spouses Ragasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559), citing Article 480 of the Civil Code and *Sapto v. Fabiana* (as cited in [*Spouses Ragasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559)), the Supreme Court reversed a dismissal under the four-year period of Article 1146 because an owner in continuous and notorious possession may wait until possession is disturbed or title is attacked before suing.[^115] In [*Tensuan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617), the Supreme Court clarified that plaintiffs need not set foot on every square inch of their titled lot to enjoy imprescriptibility when their title covers both the occupied and encroached portions.[^116] In [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822), the Supreme Court likewise held that the one-year indefeasibility period does not bar possessors whose claim partakes of an imprescriptible suit to quiet title.[^117]

Equitable defenses remain fact-sensitive. In [*Ocampo v. Ocampo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63159), where the petitioner judicially admitted the respondent's actual possession in his amended answer, laches was negated because the respondent had filed three separate criminal complaints for falsification, estafa through falsification and forgery against the petitioner.[^118] In [*Bank of Commerce*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215), estoppel by laches barred a mortgagee bank from belatedly questioning the Municipal Trial Court's jurisdiction after actively participating in the case for more than ten years and seeking affirmative relief.[^119]

## Case Discussions

### [*Aroc v. People's Homesite and Housing Corp.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1978/jan1978/gr_39674_1978.html): Prior Quieting Judgment Does Not Bar Annulment of Award

Urbana Velasco Aroc occupied half of Lot 6, Block E-144 in Quezon City and sued the People's Homesite and Housing Corporation and Spouses Cirilo Garcia and Feliciana Bito in Civil Case No. Q-11807 to annul the agency's award and sale and cancel TCT No. 106146 on grounds of charter violation and awardee disqualification.[^120] The Court of First Instance of Rizal dismissed the complaint on the ground of *res judicata* based on a prior final judgment in Civil Case No. Q-10442 for quieting of title and recovery of possession won by Garcia. The Court of Appeals certified the appeal to the Supreme Court on a pure question of law.[^121] Applying Article 476 to characterize the first suit (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that the two suits did not share identity of cause of action because the second suit required different evidence regarding the agency's authority, a presidential directive and the awardees' disqualification, while the agency had not been impleaded in the first case.[^122] The Supreme Court reversed the February 19, 1970 dismissal order and remanded the records for further proceedings with no costs.[^123] The ruling shows that a judgment in an action to quiet title is not conclusive on matters neither put in issue nor determined in that proceeding.[^124]

### [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html): Stale Notice of Embargo After Ten Years

Philippine National Bank obtained a March 31, 1959 money judgment against Eduardo Jalandoni and levied on his 17-hectare Silay City lot through a Notice of Embargo annotated on March 9, 1964, but held no execution sale for more than ten years.[^125] After Jalandoni filed Civil Case No. 685 to quiet title and cancel the notice of embargo, the Court of First Instance of Negros Occidental dismissed the complaint and Jalandoni's heirs appealed directly to the Supreme Court under Republic Act No. 5440.[^126] Sitting En Banc, the Supreme Court held under Article 1144 and Section 6, Rule 39 that levied property must be sold at public auction within the ten-year period for enforcing the judgment.[^127] Because more than ten years elapsed without an execution sale, the annotated notice of embargo became unenforceable and constituted a cloud removable under Articles 476 and 478.[^128] The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the trial court's June 15, 1977 decision and directed the Register of Deeds of Silay City to cancel the Notice of Embargo on TCT No. T-3202 with no costs.[^129] The case confirms that a judgment levy barred by extinctive prescription becomes a removable cloud under Article 476.[^130]

### [*Vda. de Aviles v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34239): Boundary Dispute Is Not a Cloud

The heirs of Eduardo Aviles filed an action for quieting of title in Branch 38 of the Regional Trial Court of Lingayen, Pangasinan against their uncle Camilo Aviles, alleging that he erected a bamboo fence and moved earthen dikes encroaching on their share under a 1957 partition agreement.[^131] The Regional Trial Court dismissed the complaint yet ordered a relocation survey. The Court of Appeals affirmed the dismissal while setting aside the survey order.[^132] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (and only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that quieting of title cannot be used to settle a boundary dispute where both parties admit the partition agreement and contest only boundary measurement.[^133] Neither the partition and redemption documents nor the bamboo fence and earthen dikes constituted clouds. The trial court could not fix boundaries in a quieting of title suit under then Rule 64 instead of an adversarial possessory proceeding.[^134] The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision with costs against the petitioners.[^135] The decision establishes that boundary disputes must be brought in forcible entry or possessory actions rather than under Article 476.[^136]

### [*Baricuatro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258): Equitable Origin and Double Sale Registration in Bad Faith

Severino Baricuatro, Jr. purchased Lots 9 and 10 in Minglanilla, Cebu on installment from Constantino Galeos in 1968 and took actual physical possession in 1970, leaving unpaid balances of P1,000.00 and P3,020.00.[^137] Galeos later sold the subdivision including the lots to Eugenio Amores, who registered titles in 1969 and resold them in 1974 to Spouses Mariano and Felisa Nemenio, who registered titles in 1976.[^138] Both the Regional Trial Court of Cebu and the Court of Appeals upheld the Nemenios' titles in Civil Case No. R-15442 for quieting of title.[^139] Applying Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court defined quieting of title as a common law remedy grounded in equity and held under Article 1544 that both Amores and the Nemenios registered in bad faith with notice of Baricuatro's prior purchase and possession.[^140] The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals decision, declared Baricuatro rightful owner subject to paying Galeos P1,000.00 for Lot 9 and P3,020.00 for Lot 10, nullified the 1968 and 1974 deeds as to both lots with purchase-price reimbursements plus legal interest from finality, directed the Register of Deeds of Cebu to cancel TCT Nos. 39002, 39003, 20016 and 20017 and issue new titles to Baricuatro upon payment of lawful fees and taxes and ordered the private respondents to pay P5,000.00 attorney's fees, P1,000.00 litigation expenses and costs.[^141] The decision sets out the foundational definition of Article 476 and removes titles registered in bad faith.[^142]

### [*Spouses Portic v. Cristobal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070): Non-Registered Vendor Under Contract to Sell

Spouses Ricardo and Ferma Portic entered into a 1984 memorandum of agreement selling an apartment property in Valenzuela City to Anastacia Cristobal while expressly reserving ownership until full payment of the P155,000.00 balance.[^143] Although TCT No. T-113299 was issued to Cristobal in 1984, she did not pay the full price and the Portics remained in possession before suing in 1996 to quiet title.[^144] The Regional Trial Court of Valenzuela City ordered quieting of title and reconveyance upon reimbursement of P145,025.89 and denied damages, but the Court of Appeals reversed on ten-year prescription and confirmed Cristobal's ownership subject to paying the P55,000.00 balance plus 6% interest.[^145] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that quieting of title is *quasi in rem*, that title under Article 476 does not necessarily mean a Torrens certificate of title, that registration under Article 1544 did not vest ownership without good faith and full payment under a contract to sell and that the Portics' continuous possession rendered the action imprescriptible.[^146] The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and reinstated the Regional Trial Court's September 23, 1999 decision with no pronouncement as to costs.[^147] The ruling confirms that a non-registered owner who retains ownership may sue under Article 476.[^148]

### [*Calacala v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772): Failure to Redeem Forecloses Both Requisites

Spouses Camilo and Conchita Calacala posted their Pangasinan lot under TCT No. T-21204 as a criminal bail bond that was forfeited and sold to the Republic at a 1982 sheriff's execution sale.[^149] Their heirs filed Civil Case No. 1239-R for quieting of title in the Regional Trial Court of Rosales, Pangasinan arguing that the Republic lost its claim by failing to obtain a final deed of sale, consolidation or writ of possession within ten years. The trial court dismissed the complaint.[^150] On direct Rule 45 review, the Supreme Court applied the first paragraph of Article 476 alongside Article 477 (while only quoting the second paragraph) and held that both indispensable requisites were absent.[^151] Under Section 33, Rule 39, the former owners lost all title upon expiration of the one-year redemption period, the final deed of sale was a mere formality, prescription does not run against the government and the heirs admitted the validity of the registered sheriff's certificate of sale.[^152] The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the trial court's October 31, 2001 resolution and July 2, 2002 order with costs against the petitioners.[^153] The case illustrates that a former owner divested by an unredeemed execution sale lacks both title and an invalid cloud.[^154]

### [*Spouses Ragasa v. Spouses Roa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559): Void Execution Sale and Imprescriptibility in Possession

Spouses Edesito and Consorcia Ragasa sued Spouses Gerardo and Rodriga Roa and the Ex-Officio Sheriff of Quezon City in Branch 220 of the Quezon City Regional Trial Court attacking a 1995 execution sale for lack of notice to occupants and gross inadequacy of the P511,000.00 bid for property estimated at P3,000,000.00.[^155] The Regional Trial Court dismissed Civil Case No. Q-99-37908 as prescribed under the four-year period of Article 1146 for actions upon an injury to rights. The Ragasas appealed directly to the Supreme Court on a pure question of law.[^156] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that the allegations of ownership by sale and delivery and an allegedly void execution sale stated an action to quiet title rather than an Article 1146 injury-to-rights suit.[^157] Because the Ragasas undisputedly alleged continuous and notorious possession in the concept of owners since May 1989, the action was imprescriptible under Article 480 and *Sapto v. Fabiana* (as cited in [*Spouses Ragasa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559)).[^158] The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the February 3, 2000 dismissal order and remanded the case for further proceedings.[^159] The ruling shows that challenging an allegedly void execution sale falls under Article 476 and does not prescribe while the plaintiff remains in possession.[^160]

### [*Bank of Commerce v. Spouses San Pablo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215): MTC Assessed-Value Jurisdiction and Forged Mortgage Cloud

After Melencio Santos obtained a P1,064,000.40 loan from Direct Funders Management and Consultancy Inc. secured by Natividad San Pablo's lot, Spouses Prudencio and Natividad San Pablo sued Santos and Bank of Commerce in Branch 2 of the Municipal Trial Court of Mandaue City to nullify a forged special power of attorney, real estate mortgage and foreclosure over the lot assessed at P4,900.00.[^161] The Municipal Trial Court and Branch 56 of the Mandaue City Regional Trial Court dismissed the complaint on the ground that the bank acted in good faith, but the Court of Appeals reversed, nullified the instruments and foreclosure and awarded P50,000.00 moral damages, P25,000.00 exemplary damages, attorney's fees and P10,000.00 litigation expenses.[^162] Quoting [*Baricuatro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258) on both paragraphs of Article 476, the Supreme Court held that the annotated mortgage cast a cloud making the suit an action for quieting of title, which is a real action within the Municipal Trial Court's exclusive original jurisdiction under Section 33(3) of Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 as amended by Republic Act No. 7691.[^163] The Supreme Court added that estoppel by laches barred the bank from raising jurisdiction after more than ten years of active participation and that the bank failed the highest degree of diligence required of banking institutions when not dealing directly with registered owners.[^164] The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals decision, declared the special power of attorney, deed of real estate mortgage and foreclosure proceedings void *ab initio*, directed the Register of Deeds of Mandaue City to cancel Entry Nos. 9089-V.9-D.B and 9084-V.9-D.B on TCT No. (26469)-7561 and ordered Bank of Commerce to pay P50,000.00 moral damages, P25,000.00 exemplary damages, P20,000.00 attorney's fees, P20,000.00 litigation expenses and costs.[^165] The case confirms that assessed value controls trial-court jurisdiction over a quieting of title suit involving a forged mortgage.[^166]

### [*San Pedro v. Ong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47626): Quasi in Rem Summons and Non-Indispensable Agent

Robert San Pedro sued Willy Ong, Normita Caballes and agent Adora Dela Peña in Branch 19 of the Regional Trial Court of Malolos, Bulacan to cancel real estate mortgages annotated on the titles of lots he bought from Spouses Narciso, alleging Dela Peña used forged special powers of attorney.[^167] The Regional Trial Court declared the mortgages void and ordered Dela Peña to pay Ong P245,000.00 plus legal interest, but the Court of Appeals reversed because substituted service of summons on Dela Peña was defective.[^168] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that the unauthorized annotated mortgages cast a removable cloud and that quieting of title is *quasi in rem*, so the trial court acquired jurisdiction over the *res* upon filing.[^169] Because Dela Peña claimed no title to the properties and was not an indispensable party, defective substituted service on her did not void the proceedings against Ong and only precluded personal money liability against her, while Ong failed the higher diligence required when dealing with a non-owner.[^170] The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals decision and reinstated the Regional Trial Court's February 21, 2003 decision with the modification deleting the order requiring Adora Dela Peña to pay Willy Ong P245,000.00 plus legal interest, without prejudice to Ong proceeding against the fraud perpetrators separately.[^171] The ruling clarifies the *quasi in rem* summons rule and party joinder in Article 476 actions.[^172]

### [*Malana v. Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941): Permissive Rule 63 and Motu Proprio Dismissal for Assessed Value

Carmen Danao Malana and co-heirs filed Civil Case No. 6868 for *reivindicacion*, quieting of title and damages in Branch 3 of the Regional Trial Court of Tuguegarao City over a lot with an assessed value of P410.00 after occupants refused a demand to vacate and asserted ownership before the barangay.[^173] Before any answer was filed, the Regional Trial Court dismissed the complaint without prejudice *motu proprio* for lack of jurisdiction under Republic Act No. 7691. The heirs filed a Rule 65 petition for certiorari directly with the Supreme Court.[^174] Applying Articles 476 to 481 together with Section 1, second paragraph of Rule 63 and Section 33(3) of Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 as amended, the Supreme Court held that Rule 63 is permissive and does not override the Municipal Trial Court's exclusive original jurisdiction where assessed value does not exceed P20,000.00 outside Metro Manila.[^175] Moreover, declaratory relief is unavailable once a breach or refusal to vacate has occurred, making *accion publiciana* or *accion reivindicatoria* proper and authorizing *motu proprio* dismissal for lack of jurisdiction.[^176] The Supreme Court dismissed the petition, affirmed the May 4, May 30 and October 31, 2007 dismissal orders without prejudice and ordered the Regional Trial Court to remand the records to the Municipal Trial Court with costs against the petitioners.[^177] The decision confirms that Rule 63 does not vest exclusive jurisdiction over quieting of title in the Regional Trial Court.[^178]

### [*Eland Philippines, Inc. v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53658): Summary Judgment and One-Year Review Remedy

Azucena Garcia, Elino Fajardo and Teresa Malabanan filed Civil Case No. TG-1784 for quieting of title in Branch 18 of the Regional Trial Court of Tagaytay City on March 5, 1998 to nullify Eland Philippines' OCT No. O-660 issued on August 29, 1997 pursuant to Decree No. N-217313 entered on August 20, 1997.[^179] The Regional Trial Court granted summary judgment cancelling the title without trial and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^180] Quoting [*Calacala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772) on both paragraphs of Article 476 and Article 477, the Supreme Court held that summary judgment was improper because Eland Philippines disputed the factual allegations underlying both requisites of quieting of title.[^181] Furthermore, because the one-year period from entry of the decree of registration under Section 32 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 had not yet elapsed when the suit was filed, quieting of title was not the proper remedy and the respondents should have filed a petition for review of the decree of registration grounded on actual fraud.[^182] The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the February 28, 2006 Court of Appeals decision and declared the Regional Trial Court's November 3, 1999 and June 28, 2006 summary judgment resolutions null and void.[^183] The case establishes that quieting of title cannot substitute for a Section 32 petition for review during the one-year period or be resolved by summary judgment amid genuine factual disputes.[^184]

### [*Phil-Ville Development and Housing Corp. v. Bonifacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462): No Cloud Without Prejudice but Declaratory Relief Available

Phil-Ville Development and Housing Corporation sued the surviving heirs of Eleuteria Rivera Vda. de Bonifacio for quieting of title in the Regional Trial Court of Caloocan City after receiving a notice to vacate based on Rivera's TCT No. C-314537 covering Maysilo Estate Lot 23.[^185] The Regional Trial Court upheld Phil-Ville's titles over Lot 23-A, but the Court of Appeals set aside the ruling and dismissed the complaint in CA-G.R. SP No. 62211.[^186] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court found that although Rivera's title traced to an inexistent April 19, 1917 OCT No. 994 was invalid, the fourth element of a cloud was missing because technical descriptions and a geodetic plan proved Lot 23 lay far west of Phil-Ville's Lot 23-A and did not prejudice its titles.[^187] Nevertheless, because Phil-Ville sued before actual dispossession seeking only judicial confirmation of its titles, the Supreme Court held it was entitled to declaratory relief under Section 1, Rule 63.[^188] The Supreme Court granted the petition, set aside the Court of Appeals decision and resolution and reinstated and upheld the Caloocan Regional Trial Court's March 24, 2000 decision with no pronouncement as to costs.[^189] The decision confirms that an invalid title covering a non-overlapping lot is not a cloud under Article 476.[^190]

### [*Green Acres Holdings, Inc. v. Cabral*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050): Final DARAB Decision as an Unenforceable Cloud

Green Acres Holdings bought five Bulacan lots from Filcon Ready Mixed on April 29, 1999, including three lots covered by transfer certificates of title with no notice of *lis pendens* or other adverse annotation. It then obtained TCT Nos. T-345660 (M), T-345661 (M) and T-345662 (M).[^191] On January 17, 2001, the Department of Agrarian Reform Adjudication Board ruled in a suit by Victoria Cabral and ordered the cancellation of the titles of Filcon and the Spouses Moraga. Green Acres filed a quieting of title complaint in the Regional Trial Court of Malolos on April 19, 2001. The board decision became final and executory on April 13, 2005.[^192] The Regional Trial Court granted Cabral's demurrer to evidence and the Court of Appeals dismissed Green Acres' appeal in CA-G.R. CV No. 85766, while in a separate execution proceeding the Provincial Agrarian Reform Adjudicator and the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 99651 denied Cabral's motion to execute against Green Acres.[^193] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court defined instrument, record, claim and proceeding and held that the final agrarian board decision satisfied all four elements of a cloud on title.[^194] Because Green Acres was an innocent purchaser for value not impleaded in the agrarian proceedings and Cabral neglected to annotate a notice of *lis pendens*, the decision was unenforceable against Green Acres and removable through quieting of title.[^195] The Supreme Court granted G.R. No. 175542, reversed the Court of Appeals decision in CA-G.R. CV No. 85766, declared Green Acres' three titles valid and removed any cloud created by the January 17, 2001 agrarian board decision, while denying Cabral's petition in G.R. No. 183205 and affirming CA-G.R. SP No. 99651 with costs against Cabral.[^196] The case defines every statutory category of cloud under Article 476.[^197]

### [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa v. Heirs of Jose Bacud*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822): Void Free Patent and Unproved Invalidity of Affidavit

Spouses Delfin and Maria Tappa filed a complaint for quieting of title and recovery of possession over Lot No. 3341 relying on Free Patent No. 021519-92-3194 and OCT No. P-69103 against occupants who relied on a notarized 1963 affidavit signed by Delfin and his relatives. The affidavit stated that Genaro originally owned the lot and that one-half belonged to Lorenzo.[^198] The trial court ruled in favor of the Tappas on July 6, 2007, but the Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the complaint.[^199] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 alongside Article 477 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that both requisites failed because the free patent and certificate of title were null and void over land already made private by open, continuous, exclusive and notorious possession. In addition, the notarized 1963 affidavit was not proved invalid by unsubstantiated testimony of force and intimidation.[^200] The Supreme Court further ruled that the occupants' affirmative defense disputing ownership was not a collateral attack on the certificate of title and that the one-year indefeasibility period did not bar possessors whose claim partook of an imprescriptible quieting of title action.[^201] The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision in CA-G.R. CV No. 90026.[^202] The ruling underscores that a void free patent confers no legal title under Article 476 and that a notarized instrument is not a cloud without proof of invalidity.[^203]

### [*Ocampo v. Ocampo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63159): Co-Owner in Possession, Forged Settlement and No Laches

Ricardo Ocampo sued his brother Jose Ocampo in Branch 55 of the Regional Trial Court of Manila for partition and annulment of TCT No. 102822 over their parents' Sampaloc lot, alleging Jose forged Ricardo's signature on a 1970 Extra-Judicial Settlement with Waiver.[^204] After an initial dismissal on prescription was nullified by the Court of Appeals, the Regional Trial Court ordered partition and cancellation of TCT No. 102822. The Court of Appeals affirmed.[^205] Because Jose judicially admitted Ricardo's actual residence on the property in his amended answer, the Supreme Court treated the suit as an action for quieting of title, quoting [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822) on both paragraphs of Article 476 and Article 477.[^206] Since the forgery of Ricardo's signature was established, the cloud had to be removed by cancelling TCT No. 102822. Laches did not bar the suit because Ricardo had filed three prior criminal complaints for falsification, estafa through falsification and forgery.[^207] The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the September 30, 2011 Regional Trial Court decision as affirmed by the Court of Appeals and directed the trial court to proceed with partition with dispatch.[^208] The case shows how actual possession and forged settlement deeds bring a co-ownership dispute within quieting of title principles.[^209]

### [*Desiderio Dalisay Investments, Inc. v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64119): Perfected Dacion en Pago Defeats Cloud Element

After initially offering its Davao City lots and building for P3,500,000.00 in 1977, Desiderio Dalisay Investments turned over the properties to the Social Security System in 1982 as a *dacion en pago* valued at P2,000,000.00 to partially offset unremitted premium liabilities, yet filed a complaint for quieting of title, recovery of possession and damages decades later.[^210] Branch 14 of the Regional Trial Court of Davao City granted the complaint finding no perfected *dacion*, but the Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the complaint for lack of merit.[^211] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that because the *dacion en pago* under Article 1245 was perfected upon acceptance and consummated by delivery and tacit ratification under Article 1910, the Social Security System held a valid and operative interest rather than a cloud.[^212] The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution, dismissed Civil Case No. 29,353-02, ordered the petitioner to execute the deed of sale and surrender the owner's duplicates of TCT Nos. T-18203, T-18204, T-255986 and T-255985 and tax declarations within ten days from finality (failing which the Clerk of Court shall execute the deed), directed the Register of Deeds of Davao City to cancel and issue new titles to the Social Security System and ordered the agency to recompute petitioner's obligations reckoned from June 17, 1982.[^213] The decision demonstrates that a valid, consummated transfer precludes an action under Article 476.[^214]

### [*Spouses Yu v. Topacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769): Non-Overlapping Valid Title Versus Physical Encroachment

Eulogio Topacio, Jr. (holding TCT No. T-348422) sued Spouses Ernesto and Elsie Yu (holding TCT No. T-490552) for quieting of title, recovery of possession and reconveyance after the spouses fenced and built a house on 450 square meters inside his Cavite lot.[^215] A court-ordered verification survey by the Community Environment and Natural Resources Office showed that the two certificates of title covered entirely different lots 1,526 meters apart while the spouses physically occupied 450 square meters of Topacio's land.[^216] Branch 90 of the Regional Trial Court of Dasmariñas, Cavite dismissed the complaint, while the Court of Appeals denied quieting of title and reconveyance but granted recovery of possession with monthly compensation and attorney's fees.[^217] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 alongside Article 477 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court affirmed that the spouses' valid, non-overlapping title was not a cloud prejudicing Topacio's title, yet upheld recovery of possession under Article 434 because of physical encroachment.[^218] Finding that the spouses built in good faith believing the area fell under their valid title, the Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision and resolution with modification deleting the P5,000.00 monthly compensation and attorney's fees and recognizing Topacio's right to exercise his option under Article 448, with no costs.[^219] The ruling sharply separates Article 476 quieting of title from Article 434 recovery of possession.[^220]

### [*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314): Thirty-Year Prescriptive Period When Out of Possession

Mercedes Gatmaytan and Erlinda Valdellon bought an Albay lot under a notarized December 9, 1991 deed of absolute sale duly annotated on TCT No. T-77703 in 1992, but lacked agrarian clearance to transfer title.[^221] In 2010 they discovered the lot had been resold to DAA Realty under a 1996 deed and consolidated into new titles by Misibis Land, prompting them to sue on December 10, 2014.[^222] Branch 15 of the Regional Trial Court of Tabaco City dismissed the complaint outright on ten-year implied-trust prescription and deficient docket fees. The buyers filed a direct Rule 45 petition on questions of law.[^223] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476, the Supreme Court held that the annotated 1991 deed sufficiently alleged equitable title and the 1996 deed and resulting title alleged a cloud.[^224] Because an action for quieting of title involving property not in the plaintiff's possession prescribes in thirty years from accrual (while remaining imprescriptible if the plaintiff is in possession), the complaint filed eighteen years after the February 22, 1996 issuance of DAA Realty's title had not prescribed. Belated payment of any docket fee deficiency could still be allowed.[^225] Stressing that its conclusions rested on hypothetical admissions and did not prejudge trial on the merits, the Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the October 22 and December 28, 2015 Regional Trial Court orders and remanded the case for trial on the merits with dispatch.[^226] The decision establishes the thirty-year prescriptive period for an Article 476 action when the plaintiff is out of possession.[^227]

### [*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617): Void Title Issued on Construction Permit and Imprescriptible Suit

The heirs of Fernando Tensuan sued Ma. Isabel Vasquez in Branch 256 of the Regional Trial Court of Muntinlupa for *accion reivindicatoria* and annulment of title after her rip-rapping pursuant to Special Work Order 13-000271 diverted the Magdaong River and resulted in TCT No. 144017 and seven derivative titles overlapping 1,680.92 square meters of their titled lot and 3,556.62 square meters of the river.[^228] After initially ruling for the heirs, the Regional Trial Court dismissed the case on prescription and the Court of Appeals affirmed the dismissal on reconsideration.[^229] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that regardless of caption, the complaint stated a cause of action for quieting of title, reconveyance and annulment of title.[^230] TCT No. 144017 was void *ab initio* because a special work order or construction permit cannot convey private or public river land. The heirs' action was imprescriptible because they remained in possession of their titled property without needing to set foot on every square inch.[^231] Finding Vasquez a builder in bad faith under Articles 449 and 450 while deleting P300,000.00 actual damages for lack of basis and non-prayer, the Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the July 4 and December 20, 2012 Court of Appeals resolutions, declared TCT No. 144017 and its derivative titles void and ordered Vasquez and her successors-in-interest Dr. Daniel Vasquez and Maria Luisa Vasquez to restore ownership and possession of the 1,680.92 square meters, pay P50,000.00 acceptance fees, P1,500.00 appearance fees and P100,000.00 attorney's fees with 6% interest per annum from finality and inform the trial court of the Article 450 election within fifteen days from finality, noting that the 3,556.62-square-meter river portion belongs to the State.[^232] The ruling confirms that an overlapping title issued on a mere construction permit is a void cloud removable in an imprescriptible action by a possessor.[^233]

### [*Almazan v. Bacolod*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67520): RTC Jurisdiction Over Quieting Suit Against Agrarian Decisions

Registered co-owner Eduviges Almazan filed a complaint for quieting of title, *accion reivindicatoria* and damages in Branch 25 of the Regional Trial Court of Biñan, Laguna against occupants claiming agricultural tenancy under Provincial Agrarian Reform Adjudicator and Department of Agrarian Reform Adjudication Board decisions rendered against strangers named Erana.[^234] The Regional Trial Court denied the occupants' motion to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction, but the Court of Appeals annulled those orders on Rule 65 certiorari finding agrarian board jurisdiction.[^235] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 alongside Article 477 (while only quoting the second paragraph) and citing [*Green Acres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050), the Supreme Court held that the complaint sufficiently stated an action for quieting of title over unenforceable agrarian decisions.[^236] Because no landlord-tenant relationship, consent or harvest sharing existed between the registered owners and the occupants, the controversy was not an agrarian dispute under Section 50 of Republic Act No. 6657 and fell within Regional Trial Court jurisdiction.[^237] The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the March 7, 2016 Court of Appeals decision and September 30, 2016 resolution and remanded the case to the Regional Trial Court for full resolution of the issues.[^238] The ruling confirms that regular courts hear Article 476 actions challenging agrarian rulings rendered against non-predecessors.[^239]

### [*Heirs of Manuel Eñano v. San Pedro Cineplex Properties, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68375): Co-Owner Standing and Unbroken Chain of Titles

Represented by Virgilio Bote through a special power of attorney from one heir, the heirs of Manuel Eñano sued San Pedro Cineplex Properties for quieting of title in Branch 93 of the Regional Trial Court of San Pedro, Laguna alleging that the company's TCT Nos. T-309608, T-309609 and T-309610 were fictitious clouds over Manuel's TCT No. T-35050.[^240] The Regional Trial Court ruled for the heirs, but the Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed the complaint for lack of merit.[^241] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 alongside Article 477 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court upheld Bote's standing to sue for all co-owners without impleading them because he did not repudiate the co-ownership, yet affirmed the dismissal because both requisites of quieting of title failed.[^242] Manuel's TCT No. T-35050 was a cancelled title for a different Calamba lot with no Land Registration Authority microfilm record, a tax declaration did not prove ownership and San Pedro Cineplex proved the unbroken genealogy of its valid titles from mother OCT No. O-217.[^243] The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the August 15, 2017 Court of Appeals decision and January 4, 2018 resolution in toto with costs on petitioners.[^244] The decision confirms that a verified chain of valid Torrens titles refutes the cloud requisite under Article 476.[^245]

### [*Du v. Ortile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68441): Notice of Lis Pendens Creates a Cloud Requiring Impleader

Malayan Savings and Mortgage Bank bought the Taft Avenue lot covered by TCT No. 194618 at a June 25, 2008 foreclosure sale. It agreed to sell the lot to Deanna Du and Primarosa Cuison for P20,500,000.00. Du paid P11,000,000.00 as downpayment. After Melissa Principe reacquired the lot through a compromise agreement with the bank, Du filed a petition for annulment of judgment against Malayan Bank, George Martinez and Melissa Principe without impleading the registered owners. She then sought to annotate a notice of *lis pendens* on the title.[^246] The Register of Deeds of Manila denied registration for failure to implead the registered owners, Land Registration Authority Deputy Administrator Ronald Ortile denied her appeal by *consulta* and the Court of Appeals denied her Rule 43 petition.[^247] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476, the Supreme Court held that annotating a notice of *lis pendens* necessarily creates a cloud on the registered owner's certificate of title that places the property in limbo and hinders lucrative disposition.[^248] Together with the protection of registered owners, prevention of fraud and due process, that cloud supported the implied requirement under Section 76 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 and Section 19, Rule 13 that registered owners be impleaded before a notice of *lis pendens* may be annotated.[^249] The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the September 11, 2020 Court of Appeals decision and February 10, 2021 resolution.[^250] The ruling establishes that a notice of *lis pendens* constitutes a cloud on title within the meaning of Article 476.[^251]

### [*Spouses Velarde v. Heirs of Candari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829): Physical Intrusion Is Accion Reivindicatoria, Not Quieting

The heirs of Isagani Velarde filed a complaint for quieting of title and damages in Branch 13 of the Regional Trial Court of Culasi, Antique after Concepcion Candari instituted tenants, collected rentals and appropriated produce from Aklan lots acquired through notarized deeds of sale with right of repurchase, quitclaim and absolute sale.[^252] The Regional Trial Court granted the complaint, but the Court of Appeals reversed finding hints of fraud including non-compliance with Article 1607 consolidation.[^253] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 alongside Article 477 and Rule 63, the Supreme Court held that physical intrusion is not a ground for quieting of title, so the complaint's allegations actually made out an *accion reivindicatoria*.[^254] Reviewing the merits under that action, the Supreme Court held that non-compliance with Article 1607 did not prove fraud, notarized deeds enjoyed full faith and credit absent clear and convincing proof of fraud and ownership vested automatically in the vendee upon expiration of the redemption period.[^255] The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the October 30, 2008 Court of Appeals decision and September 29, 2009 resolution and reinstated the July 5, 2001 Regional Trial Court decision declaring petitioners the true and rightful owners entitled to possession.[^256] The case confirms that intrusive acts of dominion fall outside Article 476 and must be resolved as *accion reivindicatoria*.[^257]

### [*Heirs of Deloy v. Basa-Joaquin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68698): Titles Derived from Void Reconstituted Title Fail Proof

Verna Basa-Joaquin, the Heirs of Spouses Mariano and Macaria Del Rosario and the Heirs of Maxima Guevarra filed petitions to quiet title over Lots 4012-J, 4012-K and 4012-L in Trece Martires City after their transfer certificates of title derived from a void reconstituted title were cancelled following an annulment judgment obtained by the Heirs of Spouses Deloy.[^258] Branch 23 of the Regional Trial Court of Trece Martires City granted the petitions and the Court of Appeals affirmed.[^259] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held that while an innocent purchaser for value may acquire ownership even from a void reconstituted title, the respondents' cancelled transfer certificates of title could not by themselves prove valid transfers.[^260] Verna presented no deed of sale and only a 2001 tax declaration and tax certification. The other respondents failed to place a copy of their alleged deeds in the record while relying on tax declarations that do not prove ownership, so all respondents failed to prove valid acquisition from Dionisio Deloy.[^261] The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the March 12, 2018 Court of Appeals decision and August 24, 2018 resolution and dismissed the respondents' petitions for quieting of title and damages.[^262] The ruling underscores that plaintiffs claiming under a void reconstituted title must prove valid acquisition or good-faith purchase to satisfy Article 476.[^263]

### [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation, Inc. v. Mabalacat Institute, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168): Demurrer Granted Where Simulated Donation Confers No Title

Mabalacat Institute sued Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation for quieting of title over a five-hectare portion of the foundation's Pampanga property under TCT No. 195826-R, relying on an unnotarized deed of absolute sale and an unnotarized special power of attorney executed by founder Don Teodoro Santos.[^264] Branch 57 of the Regional Trial Court of Angeles City granted the foundation's demurrer to evidence and dismissed the complaint, but the Court of Appeals reversed and in an Amended Decision treated the transfer as a sale conferring equitable title.[^265] Applying the first paragraph of Article 476 alongside Article 477 (while only quoting the second paragraph), the Supreme Court held under Articles 1345 and 1346 that the unnotarized deed of sale was a relatively simulated contract concealing a gratuitous donation.[^266] Because a donation of an immovable requires a public document under Article 749, the unnotarized instruments were void and Mabalacat Institute failed to prove equitable title or impugn the foundation's Torrens title.[^267] The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the July 30, 2021 Decision and April 14, 2023 Amended Decision of the Court of Appeals, reinstated the December 4, 2018 Regional Trial Court order granting the demurrer to evidence and dismissed the complaint for quieting of title for lack of evidence with no pronouncement as to costs.[^268] The decision confirms that a demurrer to evidence properly disposes of an Article 476 complaint when the plaintiff's own evidence fails to establish legal or equitable title.[^269]

- [Civil Code, Article 434](art-0434.md): Governs actions to recover property by requiring proof of identity and reliance on the strength of the plaintiff's title, distinguished from Article 476 in [*Spouses Yu v. Topacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769).[^280]
- [Civil Code, Article 448](art-0448.md): Sets the landowner's options when a builder in good faith introduces improvements, applied in [*Spouses Yu v. Topacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769) after quieting of title failed and physical encroachment was proved.[^281]
- [Civil Code, Articles 449](art-0449.md) and [450](art-0450.md): Govern forfeiture and removal or purchase options against a builder in bad faith, applied in [*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617) alongside quieting of title over an encroached lot.[^282]
- [Civil Code, Article 749](../book-3-title-03-donation/art-0749.md): Requires a public document for a valid donation of an immovable, applied in [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation, Inc. v. Mabalacat Institute, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168) to defeat a claim of equitable title.[^283]
- [Civil Code, Article 1144](../book-3-title-05-prescription/art-1144.md): Prescribes ten years for actions upon a judgment or obligation created by law, applied to judgment levies in [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html)[^284] and to implied constructive trusts in [*Gatmaytan v. - [Civil Code, Article 1146](../book-3-title-05-prescription/art-1146.md): Sets a four-year period for actions upon an injury to rights, held inapplicable to an Article 476 action to quiet title in [*Spouses Ragasa v. Spouses Roa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559).[^286]
- [Civil Code, Article 1245](../book-4-title-01-obligations/art-1245.md): Governs *dacion en pago* by the law of sales, applied in [*Desiderio Dalisay Investments, Inc. v. - [Civil Code, Articles 1345](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1345.md) and [1346](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1346.md): Define absolute and relative simulation of contracts, applied in [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation, Inc. v. Mabalacat Institute, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168) to bind the parties to their true agreement of donation.[^288]
- [Civil Code, Article 1456](../book-4-title-05-trusts/art-1456.md): Creates an implied constructive trust when property is acquired through mistake or fraud, contrasted with the thirty-year quieting of title period in [*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314).[^289]
- [Civil Code, Article 1498](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1498.md): Treats execution of a public instrument as constructive delivery of property, cited in [*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314) regarding a notarized prior deed of sale.[^290]
- [Civil Code, Article 1544](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1544.md): Requires good faith in prior registration of immovable property in double sales, applied in [*Baricuatro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258)[^291] and [*Spouses Portic v. Cristobal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070).[^292]

Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941)[^293] and [*Spouses Velarde v. Heirs of Candari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829).[^294]
- [Civil Code, Article 1910](../book-4-title-10-agency/art-1910.md): Binds the principal to obligations contracted by an agent within authority or tacitly ratified, applied in [*Desiderio Dalisay Investments, Inc. v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64119).[^295]

## Key Cases

- [*Aroc v. People's Homesite and Housing Corp.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1978/jan1978/gr_39674_1978.html) (1978): Occupant sued PHHC and awardee spouses to annul a housing award and cancel TCT No. 106146 after losing an earlier quieting of title suit. The CFI dismissed on *res judicata* and the CA certified the legal question. Applying Article 476 to distinguish the prior suit, the Supreme Court held that a quieting of title judgment does not bar a later annulment suit requiring different proof on grantor authority and awardee disqualification where PHHC was not impleaded, reversed the dismissal order and remanded with no costs.[^296]
- [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html) (1981): Judgment debtor sought cancellation of a 1964 Notice of Embargo after PNB held no execution sale for over ten years. The CFI dismissed the complaint. Sitting En Banc, the Supreme Court held that a judgment levy unenforced beyond the ten-year period under Article 1144 and Section 6, Rule 39 becomes an unenforceable cloud removable under Articles 476 and 478, reversed the dismissal and ordered the Register of Deeds of Silay City to cancel the Notice of Embargo on TCT No. T-3202 with no costs.[^297]
- [*Vda. de Aviles v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34239) (1996): Heirs of Eduardo Aviles sued their uncle for quieting of title over a bamboo fence and moved dikes under an undisputed 1957 partition. The RTC dismissed but ordered a relocation survey and the CA affirmed dismissal while setting aside the survey order. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court held that a boundary dispute is not a cloud cognizable in quieting of title and that courts cannot fix boundaries in that proceeding, denied the petition and affirmed the CA with costs against petitioners.[^298]
- [*Baricuatro v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258) (2000): Subsequent buyers who registered titles to two Minglanilla lots sued the installment buyer in possession. The RTC and CA upheld the later titles. Applying Article 476 and Article 1544, the Supreme Court defined quieting of title as a common law equitable remedy, held that subsequent buyers registered in bad faith with notice, reversed the CA, declared Baricuatro rightful owner upon paying P1,000.00 and P3,020.00 balances, voided the later sales with reimbursements plus legal interest, ordered cancellation and reissuance of titles and awarded P5,000.00 attorney's fees, P1,000.00 litigation expenses and costs.[^299]
- [*Spouses Portic v. Cristobal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070) (2005): Vendors who reserved ownership under a contract to sell sued to quiet title despite a TCT issued to an unpaid buyer. The RTC granted quieting of title and reconveyance upon reimbursement of P145,025.89, while the CA reversed on prescription. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court held that quieting of title is *quasi in rem*, that title does not require holding a Torrens certificate and that continuous possession makes the suit imprescriptible, granted the petition, reversed the CA and reinstated the RTC decision with no costs.[^300]
- [*Calacala v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772) (2005): Heirs sued to quiet title over bail-bond land sold to the Republic in 1982 because no final deed or consolidation occurred within ten years. The RTC dismissed the complaint. Applying Articles 476 and 477, the Supreme Court held that failure to redeem within one year divested ownership under Section 33, Rule 39 and that the heirs admitted the sheriff's certificate of sale, defeating both requisites, denied the petition and affirmed the RTC dismissal with costs against petitioners.[^301]
- [*Spouses Ragasa v. Spouses Roa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559) (2006): Possessors sued to void a sheriff's execution sale conducted without notice and for an allegedly inadequate P511,000.00 bid. The RTC dismissed the complaint under the four-year period of Article 1146. Applying Article 476 and Article 480, the Supreme Court held that the suit was for quieting of title and was imprescriptible while plaintiffs remained in continuous and notorious possession, granted the petition, reversed the dismissal order and remanded.[^302]
- [*Bank of Commerce v. Spouses San Pablo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215) (2007): Owners sued in the Mandaue City MTC to nullify a forged SPA, mortgage and foreclosure on land assessed at P4,900.00. The MTC and RTC dismissed while the CA reversed. Quoting [*Baricuatro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51258) on Article 476, the Supreme Court held that quieting of title is a real action governed by assessed value within MTC jurisdiction, applied estoppel by laches and highest bank diligence, denied the petition, affirmed the CA, declared the SPA, mortgage and foreclosure void *ab initio*, ordered cancellation of entries and ordered the bank to pay P50,000.00 moral damages, P25,000.00 exemplary damages, P20,000.00 attorney's fees, P20,000.00 litigation expenses and costs.[^303]
- [*San Pedro v. Ong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47626) (2008): Buyer sued to cancel mortgages annotated through forged SPAs executed by an agent. The RTC nullified the mortgages and held the agent liable for P245,000.00, while the CA reversed for defective substituted summons on the agent. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court held that quieting of title is *quasi in rem* where jurisdiction attaches over the *res* and the non-indispensable agent's defective service only bars her personal liability, granted the petition, reversed the CA and reinstated the RTC decision minus the P245,000.00 personal award against the agent.[^304]
- [*Malana v. Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941) (2009): Co-heirs sued in the Tuguegarao City RTC for *reivindicacion* and quieting of title over land assessed at P410.00 after occupants refused to vacate. The RTC dismissed without prejudice *motu proprio* for lack of jurisdiction. Applying Articles 476 to 481 with Rule 63 and Batas Pambansa Blg. 129, the Supreme Court held that Rule 63 is permissive, assessed value placed the case in the MTC and declaratory relief fails after breach, dismissed the Rule 65 petition, affirmed dismissal without prejudice and ordered remand to the MTC with costs against petitioners.[^305]
- [*Eland Philippines, Inc. v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53658) (2010): Claimants filed quieting of title within one year of Decree No. N-217313 and OCT No. O-660. The Tagaytay City RTC granted summary judgment cancelling the title and the CA affirmed. Quoting [*Calacala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772) on Articles 476 and 477, the Supreme Court held that summary judgment is improper amid genuine factual issues and that a Section 32 petition for review based on actual fraud is the proper remedy within one year, granted the petition, reversed the CA and declared the RTC summary judgment resolutions null and void.[^306]
- [*Phil-Ville Development and Housing Corp. v. Bonifacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462) (2011): Owner of Lot 23-A sued to quiet title after receiving a notice to vacate under an invalid TCT covering Lot 23. The Caloocan RTC upheld its titles while the CA dismissed the complaint. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court held that the fourth element of prejudice was absent because the titles did not overlap, yet granted declaratory relief under Rule 63 before dispossession, granted the petition, set aside the CA ruling and reinstated the RTC decision with no costs.[^307]
- [*Green Acres Holdings, Inc. v. Cabral*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050) (2013): Innocent purchaser for value sued to remove the cloud of a final DARAB decision cancelling predecessors' titles. The RTC granted a demurrer and the CA dismissed the appeal, while PARAD and the CA barred execution against Green Acres. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court defined instrument, record, claim and proceeding, held the DARAB decision was an unenforceable cloud, granted G.R. No. 175542 reversing CA-G.R. CV No. 85766 to validate Green Acres' titles and remove the cloud and denied G.R. No. 183205 with costs against Cabral.[^308]
- [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa v. Heirs of Jose Bacud*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822) (2016): Free-patent holders sued to quiet title against occupants relying on a notarized 1963 affidavit. The RTC ruled for plaintiffs while the CA reversed and dismissed. Applying Articles 476 and 477, the Supreme Court held the free patent over private land was void, the notarized affidavit was not proved invalid, disputing ownership in an answer was not a collateral attack and the one-year indefeasibility rule did not bar possessors, denied the petition and affirmed the CA.[^309]
- [*Ocampo v. Ocampo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63159) (2017): Co-owner brother sued for partition and cancellation of TCT No. 102822 procured via a forged 1970 extrajudicial settlement. The Manila RTC ordered cancellation and partition and the CA affirmed. Quoting [*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822) on Articles 476 and 477, the Supreme Court treated the suit as quieting of title based on judicially admitted actual possession, held prior criminal complaints negated laches, denied the petition, affirmed the lower courts and ordered partition with dispatch.[^310]
- [*Desiderio Dalisay Investments, Inc. v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64119) (2018): Debtor corporation sued to quiet title over Davao properties turned over to SSS in 1982 as a P2,000,000.00 *dacion en pago*. The RTC ruled for the debtor while the CA reversed and dismissed. Applying Article 476 with Articles 1245 and 1910, the Supreme Court held the perfected and consummated *dacion* gave SSS a valid interest rather than a cloud, denied the petition, affirmed dismissal, ordered execution of the deed of sale, surrender of titles and issuance of new titles to SSS and ordered SSS to recompute obligations from June 17, 1982.[^311]
- [*Spouses Yu v. Topacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769) (2019): Titled owner sued encroaching neighbors for quieting of title, recovery of possession and reconveyance. A survey showed the two TCTs lay 1,526 meters apart while defendants built in good faith on 450 square meters of plaintiff's lot. The RTC dismissed and the CA denied quieting and reconveyance but granted possession with damages. Applying Articles 476, 477, 434 and 448, the Supreme Court affirmed that the valid non-overlapping TCT was not a cloud while upholding recovery of possession, denied the petition and modified the CA ruling to delete P5,000.00 monthly compensation and attorney's fees and grant Topacio his Article 448 option with no costs.[^312]
- [*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314) (2020): Buyers with an annotated 1991 deed of sale sued eighteen years after a 1996 resale and new title. The Tabaco City RTC dismissed outright on ten-year implied-trust prescription and docket fees. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court held that quieting of title by a plaintiff out of possession prescribes in thirty years (while imprescriptible if in possession) and allowed payment of any docket fee deficiency, granted the petition, reversed the dismissal orders and remanded for trial on the merits.[^313]
- [*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617) (2020): Heirs sued to annul TCT No. 144017 issued on a special work order overlapping 1,680.92 square meters of their lot and part of a river. The RTC and CA dismissed on prescription. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court held the complaint stated an imprescriptible quieting of title action by possessors, declared TCT No. 144017 and derivative titles void, reversed the CA, ordered restoration of the 1,680.92 square meters, awarded P50,000.00 acceptance fees, P1,500.00 appearance fees and P100,000.00 attorney's fees with 6% annual interest from finality and directed notice of the Article 450 election within fifteen days from finality.[^314]
- [*Almazan v. Bacolod*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67520) (2021): Registered co-owner sued in the Biñan RTC to quiet title against PARAD and DARAB decisions issued against strangers. The RTC denied a motion to dismiss while the CA annulled those orders finding DARAB jurisdiction. Applying Articles 476 and 477 and citing [*Green Acres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050), the Supreme Court held that absent a tenancy relationship under Republic Act No. 6657 the RTC had jurisdiction over the quieting of title suit, reversed the CA and remanded for full resolution.[^315]
- [*Heirs of Manuel Eñano v. San Pedro Cineplex Properties, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68375) (2022): Heirs sued to quiet title claiming respondent's TCTs were fictitious. The San Pedro RTC ruled for the heirs while the CA reversed and dismissed. Applying Articles 476 and 477, the Supreme Court upheld co-owner standing without impleading other co-owners yet ruled both requisites failed because petitioners relied on a cancelled title and tax declaration while respondent proved an unbroken chain of valid titles from OCT No. O-217, denied the petition and affirmed the CA in toto with costs on petitioners.[^316]
- [*Du v. Ortile*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68441) (2022): Applicant sought to annotate a notice of *lis pendens* without impleading the registered owners in her annulment suit. The Manila Register of Deeds, LRA Deputy Administrator and CA denied registration. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court held that annotating a notice of *lis pendens* necessarily creates a cloud on title requiring that registered owners be impleaded, denied the petition and affirmed the CA.[^317]
- [*Spouses Velarde v. Heirs of Candari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829) (2022): Heirs holding notarized deeds sued for quieting of title after the former owner instituted tenants and collected rentals. The Culasi RTC granted the complaint while the CA reversed on suspected fraud. Applying Articles 476 and 477 and Rule 63, the Supreme Court held that physical intrusion is not a cloud and treated the complaint as *accion reivindicatoria*, found no clear proof of fraud against the notarized deeds, granted the petition, reversed the CA and reinstated the RTC decision declaring petitioners rightful owners entitled to possession.[^318]
- [*Heirs of Deloy v. Basa-Joaquin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68698) (2022): Claimants sued to quiet title over lots whose cancelled TCTs stemmed from a void reconstituted title. The Trece Martires RTC granted the petitions and the CA affirmed. Applying Article 476, the Supreme Court held that titles derived from a void reconstituted title and bare tax declarations did not prove valid purchase or good faith for value, granted the petition, reversed the CA and dismissed the petitions for quieting of title and damages.[^319]
- [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation, Inc. v. Mabalacat Institute, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168) (2025): School sued to quiet title over five hectares using an unnotarized deed of sale and SPA. The Angeles City RTC granted the owner's demurrer to evidence while the CA reversed and treated the deal as a sale. Applying Articles 476, 477, 749, 1345 and 1346, the Supreme Court held the unnotarized deed concealed a void donation conferring no equitable title, granted the petition, reversed the CA, reinstated the RTC demurrer order and dismissed the complaint for lack of evidence with no costs.[^320]

## Suggested Answers

**Question 1:** Maria purchased a parcel of land in 1995 from a corporation holding a clean transfer certificate of title free of any notice of *lis pendens* or encumbrance, registered the deed and obtained a transfer certificate of title in her name. Three years later, the Department of Agrarian Reform Adjudication Board issued a final decision in a case filed before Maria's purchase ordering the cancellation of the seller's title in favor of a former landowner. Maria was never impleaded in the agrarian proceedings. May Maria file an action for quieting of title before the Regional Trial Court to remove the agrarian board decision as a cloud on her title?

**Suggested Answer:** Yes. Under Article 476 of the Civil Code and [*Green Acres Holdings, Inc. v. Cabral*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050), an action to quiet title prospers when two requisites concur: the plaintiff holds legal or equitable title to the real property and the instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding casting a cloud on that title is shown to be invalid, ineffective, voidable or unenforceable despite its *prima facie* validity.[^321] Maria satisfies the first requisite as registered owner and innocent purchaser for value who relied on a clean title without any annotated notice of *lis pendens*. She also satisfies the second requisite because a final agrarian board decision constitutes an instrument, record, claim and proceeding that appears valid and prejudices her title yet is ineffective and unenforceable against her since she was not impleaded in those proceedings. Because Maria has no tenancy relationship with the former landowner and does not seek to modify the agrarian ruling between the original parties, the Regional Trial Court may hear her action to quiet title, as in [*Green Acres*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050)[^322] and [*Almazan v. Bacolod*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67520).[^323]

**Question 2:** Spouses Reyes and their neighbor Pedro signed an undisputed partition agreement dividing inherited land between them. Years later, Pedro erected a bamboo fence and shifted earthen dikes onto a strip that Spouses Reyes claim falls inside their share. Spouses Reyes filed a special civil action for quieting of title under Article 476 asking the Regional Trial Court to remove the cloud created by the fence and determine the true boundary line. Should the action for quieting of title prosper?

**Suggested Answer:** No. Under Article 476 of the Civil Code and [*Vda. de Aviles v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34239), an action to quiet title cannot be brought to settle a boundary dispute.[^324] Both parties admit the validity of the partition agreement and neither holds an invalid, ineffective, voidable or unenforceable instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding casting a cloud on the other's title. Constructing a bamboo fence and moving earthen dikes are physical acts rather than clouds removable under Article 476. A court in a quieting of title proceeding cannot determine boundaries through *evidence aliunde*. Spouses Reyes must pursue an adversarial possessory remedy such as forcible entry or recovery of possession where boundary measurement may properly be litigated.[^325]

**Question 3:** Elena bought a titled lot in 1991 under a notarized deed of absolute sale that she had annotated on the seller's transfer certificate of title, though she never took physical possession or secured a new certificate of title in her name. On February 22, 1996, the seller fraudulently resold the same lot to a third party who obtained a new transfer certificate of title. Elena filed an action for quieting of title eighteen years later on December 10, 2014. The defendant moved to dismiss the complaint on the ground that the action had already prescribed after ten years. Should the trial court dismiss the complaint on prescription?

**Suggested Answer:** No. Under Article 476 of the Civil Code and [*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314), an action for quieting of title is a real action that prescribes thirty years after the cause of action accrues when the plaintiff is not in possession of the property, whereas an action by a plaintiff in possession is imprescriptible.[^326] Elena sufficiently alleges equitable title through her notarized and annotated 1991 deed of absolute sale and alleges a cloud through the 1996 resale and resulting certificate of title. Counting from February 22, 1996 when the adverse Torrens title cast a cloud on her equitable title, Elena filed her complaint only eighteen years later on December 10, 2014, well within the thirty-year prescriptive period. The trial court therefore cannot dismiss the quieting of title claim on a ten-year prescriptive bar.[^327]

# Quotes

> "A cloud on title consists of (1) any instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding; (2) which is apparently valid or effective; (3) but is in truth and in fact invalid, ineffective, voidable, or unenforceable; and (4) may be prejudicial to the title sought to be quieted."[^328]

> "From the foregoing provisions, we reiterate the rule that for an action to quiet title to prosper, two indispensable requisites must concur, namely: (1) the plaintiff or complainant has a legal or an equitable title to or interest in the real property subject of the action; and (2) the deed, claim, encumbrance or proceeding claimed to be casting cloud on his title must be shown to be in fact invalid or inoperative despite its prima facie appearance of validity or legal efficacy."[^329]

> "Generally, the registered owner of a property is the proper party to bring an action to quiet title. However, it has been held that this remedy may also be availed of by a person other than the registered owner because, in the Article reproduced above, “title” does not necessarily refer to the original or transfer certificate of title. Thus, lack of an actual certificate of title to a property does not necessarily bar an action to quiet title."[^330]

> "Significantly, suits to quiet title are characterized as proceedings quasi in rem. Technically, they are neither in rem nor in personam. In an action quasi in rem, an individual is named as defendant. However, unlike suits in rem, a quasi in rem judgment is conclusive only between the parties. A proceeding quasi in rem is one brought against persons seeking to subject the property of such persons to the discharge of the claims assailed."[^331]

> "An action to quiet title or to remove cloud may not be brought for the purpose of settling a boundary dispute."[^332]

> "This action may be brought by one who has legal or equitable title to, or interest in the real property which is the subject matter of the action, whether or not such party is in possession. As a general rule, an action for quieting of title, being a real action, prescribes thirty (30) years after accrual. However, by way of exception, an action to quiet title involving property in the possession of the plaintiff is imprescriptible."[^333]

> "Since a cloud on the title is created when a notice of lis pendens is annotated, the registered owner should be given the opportunity to be able to protect his/her clean, or unblemished certificate of title. In the interregnum, while the cloud, i.e., notice of lis pendens, remains annotated in the certificate of title, the registered owner's right to dispose the property is unduly affected because a lucrative transfer may be hindered by the presence of the cloud. The very property is in limbo during such uncertain period."[^334]

> "We find that the "notice of embargo" annotated in 1964 on Jalandoni's title is no longer enforceable and has become a cloud upon his title. Following the rule in the Ansaldo case, he and his heirs have a good cause of action under article 476 of the Civil Code for the removal of that state encumbrance."[^335]

> "It is also a general rule that a judgment in an action to quiet title is not conclusive as to matters not in issue and determined"[^336]

# Tips

## Diagnostic Checklist

> [!TIP]
> **Five-Step Article 476 Case Check**
> 1. **Verify plaintiff's title:** Confirm legal title (registered ownership) or equitable title (beneficial ownership under a valid contract or relation). A void free patent ([*Heirs of Delfin and Maria Tappa v. Heirs of Jose Bacud*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61822)), an unnotarized simulated donation ([*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation, Inc. v. Mabalacat Institute, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168)) or bare tax declarations without proof of valid transfer ([*Heirs of Deloy v. Basa-Joaquin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68698)) will fail at step one.
> 2. **Isolate the specific cloud:** Identify the exact instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding and verify all four elements of a cloud under [*Green Acres Holdings, Inc. v. Cabral*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050). Mere boundary disagreements ([*Vda. de Aviles v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34239)) and physical intrusion ([*Spouses Velarde v. Heirs of Candari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829)) do not qualify.
> 3. **Confirm overlap and prejudice:** Check technical descriptions or a verification survey to ensure the adverse title actually covers the plaintiff's land. A non-overlapping title lacks prejudice under [*Phil-Ville Development and Housing Corp. v. Bonifacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36462) and [*Spouses Yu v. Topacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769).
> 4. **Check the proper court and timing:** Verify assessed value under Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 as amended by Republic Act No. 7691 ([*Malana v. Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941) and [*Bank of Commerce v. Spouses San Pablo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215)) and verify whether the one-year review period under Section 32 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 is still open ([*Eland Philippines, Inc. v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53658)).
> 5. **Determine possession for prescription:** If the plaintiff is in actual possession, the action is imprescriptible ([*Spouses Portic v. Cristobal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070), [*Spouses Ragasa v. Spouses Roa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559) and [*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617)). If the plaintiff is out of possession, apply the thirty-year real-action prescriptive period from accrual ([*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314)).

## Common Traps

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 1: Filing in the Regional Trial Court Automatically Under Rule 63**
> Do not assume Rule 63 gives the Regional Trial Court exclusive jurisdiction over every quieting of title complaint. In [*Bank of Commerce v. Spouses San Pablo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43215) and [*Malana v. Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941), the Supreme Court stressed that quieting of title is a real action governed by the property's assessed value under Batas Pambansa Blg. 129 as amended by Republic Act No. 7691. Filing in the wrong court risks *motu proprio* dismissal without prejudice as affirmed in [*Malana v. Tappa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49941).

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 2: Confusing Physical Encroachment With a Cloud on Title**
> In Bar examinations and trial practice, separate paper clouds from physical trespass. When neighbors build a fence across a disputed boundary line ([*Vda. de Aviles v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/34239)) or install tenants and collect rentals ([*Spouses Velarde v. Heirs of Candari*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68829)), there is no invalid instrument or proceeding to cancel. Even if both parties hold valid Torrens titles, a survey proving the titles cover different lots defeats quieting of title while leaving recovery of possession under Article 434 available ([*Spouses Yu v. Topacio*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65769)).

> [!NOTE]
> **Trap 3: Applying a Four-Year or Ten-Year Prescriptive Bar Reflexively**
> Defendants often move to dismiss quieting of title suits using the four-year period of Article 1146 or the ten-year period for written contracts and implied trusts. Watch for possession first. Continuous possession makes the action imprescriptible ([*Spouses Portic v. Cristobal*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43070) and [*Spouses Ragasa v. Spouses Roa*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43559)), and even partial occupation of a titled lot suffices ([*Tensuan v. Heirs of Vasquez*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66617)). When the plaintiff is out of possession, the thirty-year period governs the quieting of title cause of action ([*Gatmaytan v. Misibis Land, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/66314)).

## What Worked and What Did Not Work in Decided Cases

> [!TIP]
> **Winning Strategies in Supreme Court Decisions**
> - **Proving non-impleader and clean title reliance:** In [*Green Acres Holdings, Inc. v. Cabral*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56050) and [*Almazan v. Bacolod*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67520), registered owners overcame adverse agrarian rulings by proving they were strangers to the agrarian case with no tenancy relationship and no annotated notice of *lis pendens*.
> - **Documenting an unbroken chain of Torrens titles:** In [*Heirs of Manuel Eñano v. San Pedro Cineplex Properties, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68375), tracing every transfer from the mother original certificate of title defeated unsupported claims of spurious titles.
> - **Using a demurrer to evidence when plaintiff's documents are void:** In [*Santos Ventura Hocorma Foundation, Inc. v. Mabalacat Institute, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/70168), moving on demurrer succeeded because the plaintiff's own evidence, an unnotarized deed, showed a void donation under Article 749.
> - **Relying on judicial admissions and criminal complaints:** In [*Ocampo v. Ocampo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/63159), the defendant's admission of the plaintiff's residence anchored quieting of title while prior criminal complaints for forgery defeated laches.

> [!NOTE]
> **Arguments That Failed in Supreme Court Decisions**
> - **Moving for summary judgment on disputed title facts:** Reversed in [*Eland Philippines, Inc. v. Garcia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/53658), where genuine factual issues required trial and the one-year review period under Section 32 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 still applied.
> - **Claiming reversion from government inaction after an unredeemed sale:** Rejected in [*Calacala v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42772) because ownership passed automatically after the one-year redemption period under Section 33, Rule 39.
> - **Attacking a perfected *dacion en pago* decades after delivery:** Dismissed in [*Desiderio Dalisay Investments, Inc. v. Social Security System*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64119) because delivery and tacit ratification under Article 1910 perfected the transfer.
> - **Invoking defective summons on a non-indispensable co-defendant:** Rejected in [*San Pedro v. Ong*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/47626) because quieting of title is *quasi in rem* and defective service on the fraudulent agent only barred personal money liability against her.
