> ART. 478. There may also be an action to quiet title or remove a cloud therefrom when the contract, instrument or other obligation has been extinguished or has terminated, or has been barred by extinctive prescription.[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 478 supplies a ground for quieting title to real property when a contract, instrument or other obligation has ceased to be effective or enforceable. A notice may remain annotated on a certificate of title even after the right it records can no longer be enforced. In [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html), the Supreme Court treated an unenforceable notice of embargo as a cloud and cited Article 478 alongside Article 476.[^2]

**In brief**

1. Identify the plaintiff's legal or equitable title to or interest in the property.
2. Identify the instrument or claim that appears valid or effective and clouds that title or interest.
3. Establish the extinguishment, termination or extinctive prescription on which the action rests.
4. Identify the adverse parties whose rights would be affected by cancellation.

The inquiry starts with the plaintiff's interest and the alleged cloud, then turns to the event that supposedly extinguished the obligation and the relief the facts support.

## The plaintiff's interest and the apparent cloud

Article 476 describes the cloud as an apparently valid or effective instrument, record, claim, encumbrance or proceeding that is actually invalid, ineffective, voidable or unenforceable and may prejudice title. Article 478 addresses the case in which the contract, instrument or other obligation has been extinguished, has terminated or is barred by extinctive prescription. Article 477 requires the plaintiff to have legal or equitable title to or interest in the affected property. Possession is unnecessary under Article 477.[^3]

The plaintiff must allege facts supporting both the plaintiff's interest and the invalidity or inoperativeness of the alleged cloud. In [*De Guzman v. Tabangao Realty Incorporated*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59826), the allegations of succession and continuing registration in the spouses' names failed to establish the heirs' interest because the complaint alleged no timely redemption after the execution sale. The allegations of invalidity also fell short. The Supreme Court treated conclusions of law, unsupported inferences and bare charges of fraud as outside what the motion to dismiss hypothetically admitted. The heirs' inference that the levy and sale occurred on the same day did not overcome the certificate's validity. Their allegation of fraud in delaying consolidation lacked a factual basis and concerned events after the certificate was issued.[^4]

## Extinguishment or termination by agreement

If the plaintiff relies on a later agreement, its terms and legal effect must support the claimed extinguishment or termination. In [*Spouses Ramos v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41543), the heirs alleged that a compromise ended their father's obligation under the deed of sale. The Supreme Court held that the father was outside the agreement and that any benefit to him was merely incidental. The reference to him described the land and conferred no enforceable right on which the heirs could rely.[^5]

## Prescription of enforcement and expiration of redemption

The relevant period depends on the right the plaintiff claims has ceased. In [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html), the bank obtained a levy within five years of entry of judgment and never caused an execution sale. The Supreme Court followed *Ansaldo v. Fidelity and Surety Co. of the P.I.* Under that rule, levied property must be sold at public auction within the ten-year period for enforcing the judgment. An execution is accomplished by levy and sale, not by levy alone. The timely levy therefore did not make a later sale permissible after prescription. The annotation consequently became an unenforceable cloud.[^6]

In [*De Guzman v. Tabangao Realty Incorporated*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59826), an execution sale had taken place and the one-year redemption period expired without redemption. The Supreme Court described redemption within that period as a condition precedent to exercising the right. Under Rule 39, Section 33 of the 1997 Rules of Court as applied in the case, expiration of that period vested the spouses' rights in the purchaser. The final deed would confirm the title already vested. No prescriptive period to compel the sheriff to execute the deed had begun to run. If the sheriff refused to execute it, the purchaser's cause of action to compel execution and the period for that action would begin then.[^7]

## Cancellation and notice to affected parties

When cancellation would defeat the rights of identified judgment creditors, look to the relief sought and the persons against whom it is directed. In [*Rico v. Rufon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43917), the Supreme Court classified the petition to cancel a notice of embargo as an action to quiet title or remove a cloud under Articles 476, 478 and 481. It treated the petition as a real action and an action in personam directed at the plaintiffs in the execution case. Posting alone did not supply the required personal service. The judge also failed to require evidence on prescription and issued a final order without the supporting facts and law.[^8]

## Case Discussions

### [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html): Levy Without an Execution Sale

On March 31, 1959, the Court of First Instance of Manila ordered Eduardo Jalandoni to pay the Philippine National Bank P63,297.53 with interest, attorney's fees and costs. The judgment became final and executory. On March 9, 1964, within five years from its entry, the sheriff of Silay City levied on Lot No. 657-C of the Silay cadastre under an alias writ of execution. The lot measured seventeen hectares. The Notice of Embargo was annotated on TCT No. T-1827, which TCT No. T-3202 replaced in 1969. The bank made no effort to have the land sold at public auction.

On April 22, 1974, Jalandoni petitioned the Court of First Instance of Negros Occidental at Silay City to cancel the levy on the ground of prescription. On October 31, 1974, that court directed him to ask the Manila court to quash the writ and then to refile. On May 20, 1975, he filed Civil Case No. 685 to quiet title. The trial court dismissed it on June 15, 1977. Jalandoni had died on January 20, 1977. His heirs appealed directly under Republic Act No. 5440.

The Supreme Court held that the trial court erred in not applying *Ansaldo v. Fidelity and Surety Co. of the P.I.* Under that rule, levied property must be sold at public auction within the ten-year period for enforcing the judgment. The Supreme Court also faulted the bank's employees for failing to require the sheriff to sell the land. The notice of embargo was no longer enforceable and had become a cloud on the title. The heirs therefore had a good cause of action under Article 476. The Supreme Court added that Article 478 allows the same action when an obligation has been barred by extinctive prescription.[^9]

The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the dismissal. It directed the register of deeds of Silay City to cancel the Notice of Embargo on TCT No. T-3202. No costs were awarded.

### [*Spouses Ramos v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41543): Effect of a Compromise Agreement

In 1948, Gregorio Valdez sold a parcel of 3,036 square meters to the spouses Segundo Ramos and Felisa Valdez. The deed was annotated on OCT No. 48824. Valdez died in 1991. His children then sued the spouses for quieting of title, ownership and possession. They alleged that the spouses had renounced their rights in a 1977 compromise agreement. They argued that the compromise extinguished or terminated their father's obligation under the deed. The spouses answered that the compromise referred to another parcel and was itself a cloud on their title.

The Regional Trial Court of Urdaneta, Pangasinan, Branch 48 dismissed the complaint and declared the spouses owners. The Court of Appeals reversed. It declared the children owners and ordered the spouses to vacate with costs against them.

The Supreme Court held that the compromise bound only its parties. Those parties were the spouses and Felipe Cabero, who had opposed their land registration application. Valdez was keenly interested in the agreement but was not a party to it. His signature appeared without any stated capacity. His children therefore could not sue for its performance. The Supreme Court also rejected the claim that a clause naming Valdez conferred a right on him. The clause only described the land being renounced. Any benefit to him was merely incidental, so there was no stipulation in favor of a third person.[^10]

The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals decision. It affirmed the trial court decision only insofar as it dismissed the complaint. No costs were awarded.

### [*Rico v. Rufon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43917): Notice to Judgment Creditors and Proof of Prescription

Atty. Rex G. Rico was counsel for the plaintiffs in Civil Case No. 32482 before the Regional Trial Court, Branch 155, Pasig City. That court ruled for his clients in a decision dated April 26, 1983. The Court of Appeals affirmed. The Bacolod City sheriff later levied on properties of the defendant's spouse and annotated a Notice of Embargo dated May 16, 1989 on the titles.

On September 7, 2000, the spouse petitioned the Regional Trial Court, Branch 51, Bacolod City to cancel the notice. Judge Anastacio C. Rufon sat as acting presiding judge. He required only posting in three conspicuous places for three weeks before the hearing set for October 27, 2000. On that date he granted the petition in a brief order because no one opposed it. Rico then charged him with gross ignorance of the law.

The Supreme Court sustained the charge. The petition was an action to quiet title or remove a cloud under Articles 476, 478 and 481 and was not a cadastral matter. It was a real action but also an action in personam because it was directed only at the plaintiffs in Civil Case No. 32482. Posting did not give them the personal service they were owed. The judge also did not require evidence on prescription. His order was in the nature of a final judgment but stated no facts or law. The Supreme Court noted that this was his first infraction in six years of service. It compared fines of P10,000 imposed in similar cases.[^11]

The Supreme Court found Judge Rufon guilty of gross ignorance of the law. It fined him P10,000 with a stern warning that repetition would be dealt with more severely. No costs were awarded.

### [*De Guzman v. Tabangao Realty Incorporated*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59826): Failure to State a Cause of Action After an Execution Sale

Serafin and Josefino de Guzman failed to pay credit purchases of oil and lubricating products from Filipinas Shell Petroleum Corporation. The Regional Trial Court of Manila ruled against them in Civil Case No. 120680. The judgment became final and executory. A writ of execution issued on May 3, 1983. On June 30, 1983, the creditor levied on a parcel of 74,415 square meters in Trece Martires City covered by TCT No. 3531 in the names of the spouses Serafin and Amelia de Guzman. A Sheriff's Certificate of Sale dated February 4, 1988 recorded the sale to Tabangao Realty Incorporated for P70,000. It was annotated on April 13, 1988. The spouses did not redeem within one year.

On October 19, 2001, the 11 children of the spouses sued in the Regional Trial Court, Branch 23, Trece Martires City to quiet title. They alleged that the entries on the title had been terminated or extinguished by prescription, laches and estoppel. They also alleged that no sale could have occurred on the same day as the levy. They added that the buyer had waited more than 13 years to consolidate title. Tabangao moved to dismiss for a defective certification against forum shopping and for failure to state a cause of action. The trial court found the certificate of sale valid and the buyer's right not yet prescribed. It dismissed the complaint without costs and denied reconsideration. The heirs went directly to the Supreme Court on pure questions of law.

The Supreme Court restated the two requisites of an action to quiet title. The plaintiff must have legal or equitable title or interest. The adverse instrument must be shown to be invalid or inoperative despite its apparent validity. The complaint alleged neither. Succession and registration in the parents' names did not show title. The one-year redemption period ended on April 13, 1989 with no alleged redemption. Tabangao then acquired the spouses' rights under Rule 39, Section 33. At their deaths on January 1, 1997 and April 23, 2001, the parents had no rights left to pass on. The Supreme Court applied the 1997 rule retroactively in line with *Calacala v. Republic of the Philippines*. The delay in obtaining a final deed changed nothing because the deed only confirmed vested title. No prescriptive period had begun to run.[^12]

The claims of invalidity were conclusions of law, inferences and bare charges of fraud that the motion to dismiss did not admit. The annotations showed that the levy came on June 30, 1983. The certificate of sale came almost five years later. The Supreme Court presumed that the sheriff who executed it and the judge who approved it had regularly performed their duties.[^13]

The Supreme Court denied the petition. It affirmed the orders of March 4, 2002 and May 21, 2002.

## Key Cases

No costs were awarded.
- [*Spouses Ramos v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41543)[^19] concerned an alleged extinguishment by compromise. ## Suggested Answers

### 1. Levy left on the title after the judgment prescribed

**Question.** A bank timely levies on the debtor's land. No public auction takes place within the ten-year period for enforcing the judgment. May the heirs seek cancellation of the notice of embargo?

**Answer.** Yes. If the judgment can no longer be enforced and the notice remains on the title, the heirs may seek removal of the notice as a cloud. In [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html), the Supreme Court directed cancellation because the timely levy did not permit a sale beyond the enforcement period.[^22]

### 2. A compromise mentions the original seller

**Question.** Heirs allege that a later compromise ended their father's obligation under a deed of sale. Their father was outside the compromise, which named him only to identify the land. Can the heirs rely on that compromise to defeat the buyers' claim?

**Answer.** No. If their father was outside the agreement and any benefit to him was merely incidental, his heirs cannot enforce it. In [*Spouses Ramos v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41543), such a compromise could not defeat the buyers' claim.[^23]

### 3. No final deed after the redemption period

**Question.** Land was sold at a valid execution sale under Rule 39, Section 33. No redemption occurred before expiration of the redemption period. The heirs claim that delay in obtaining a final deed returned title to their predecessors. Does the delay supply a cause of action to quiet title?

**Answer.** No. If the sale was valid and no timely redemption was made, the purchaser acquired the judgment obligor's rights upon expiration of the redemption period. In [*De Guzman v. Tabangao Realty Incorporated*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59826), the final deed would merely confirm title already vested and the heirs' complaint failed to state a cause of action.[^24]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Checklist for a claim under Article 478**
> Identify the property and state the plaintiff's legal or equitable interest. Identify the entry or instrument that appears to burden that interest. Specify the event that allegedly extinguished or terminated the obligation, or the facts showing prescription. Identify the parties whose rights would be affected by cancellation.

### Check What Happened After the Levy

For a levy on execution, find out whether the sheriff ever held a public auction. Then check whether the sale occurred within the relevant enforcement period. The bank in [*Jalandoni v. Philippine National Bank*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1981/oct1981/gr_47579_1981.html) relied on a timely levy and took no steps to have the land sold. The annotation could remain on the title even though the levy had become unenforceable.

### Check the Interest Claimed by Heirs

If there was an execution sale, examine the allegations about redemption and when the redemption period expired. If the heirs rely on succession, ask what interest their predecessors still had to transmit. In [*De Guzman v. Tabangao Realty Incorporated*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59826), the heirs' reliance on the absence of a final deed failed because title had already vested on expiration of the redemption period without redemption.

### Do Not Treat the Redemption Period as Prescription

The Supreme Court in [*De Guzman v. Tabangao Realty Incorporated*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/59826) called the redemption period a condition precedent and not a prescriptive period. Count one year from registration of the certificate of sale. If no redemption is alleged, the purchaser already holds the judgment obligor's rights. A delayed final deed does not return them.

### Plead Facts and Not Conclusions

A motion to dismiss admits only well-pleaded facts. In *De Guzman*, charges of fraud, bad faith and invalidity stood on conclusions and inferences. Plead the dates, the entries on the title and the facts that make the sale or the levy ineffective.

### Read the Compromise for Its Effect on the Claimed Obligation

Check who agreed to the compromise and whom the contracting parties meant to benefit. In [*Spouses Ramos v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41543), the reference to the original seller identified the land. The Supreme Court found that any benefit to him was incidental. That left his heirs unable to use the compromise to defeat the buyers' claim.

> [!NOTE]
> **Notice and proof in a cancellation proceeding**
> Identify the creditors named by the annotation if cancellation would affect their rights. In [*Rico v. Rufon*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43917), the creditors were entitled to personal service. The Supreme Court also faulted the judge for failing to require evidence on prescription and to state the factual and legal basis of the final order.
