> ART. 539. Every possessor has a right to be respected in his possession; and should he be disturbed therein he shall be protected in or restored to said possession by the means established by the laws and the Rules of Court.
>
> A possessor deprived of his possession through forcible entry may within ten days from the filing of the complaint present a motion to secure from the competent court, in the action for forcible entry, a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore him in his possession. The court shall decide the motion within thirty (30) days from the filing thereof. (446a)[^civil-code]

# Explanation

## Concept and Scope of Possessory Protection

Article 539 of the Civil Code establishes the substantive guarantee that every possessor has the right to be respected in his possession and, if disturbed, to be protected in or restored to that possession through the remedies provided by law and the Rules of Court. Its second paragraph pairs this substantive right with an urgent provisional remedy by authorizing a possessor deprived of possession through forcible entry to move within ten days from the filing of the complaint for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction restoring possession during the pendency of the action, which the court must decide within thirty days from filing.

### All Kinds of Possession Protected

The statutory phrase every possessor in Article 539(1) encompasses all kinds of possession, from that of the owner to that of a mere holder, except possession that constitutes a crime, as held in [*Philippine Trust Company v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36989).[^2] In [*Philippine Trust Company*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36989), the Supreme Court held that a corporation remained a real party in interest with a cause of action to sue for disturbance of its enjoyment when a sheriff improperly implemented a writ of possession against its lot covered by Original Certificate of Title No. R-165, even though the one-year redemption period had already expired following a separate mortgage foreclosure by the Land Bank of the Philippines.[^3] Because the Land Bank of the Philippines had deferred consolidation of title on account of the land being submerged in water and had given the corporation an option to reacquire the property, the corporation was in lawful possession when disturbed and was entitled to institute an action under Article 539(1).[^4]

The same rule protects lessees and agricultural tenants against both their lessors and third-party intruders. In [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863), the Supreme Court ruled under Article 1654(3) and Article 539(1) of the Civil Code that every possessor includes a mere holder and that possession is not protection against right, but against the exercise of a right by one's own authority.[^5] Similarly, in [*Bacar v. Del Rosario*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/mar1989/gr_51208_1989.html), the Supreme Court explained that ownership is distinct from possession, which may legally reside in a lessee or tenant under Article 525 of the Civil Code.[^6] Because the tenant in [*Bacar*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/mar1989/gr_51208_1989.html) had the right under Article 539(1) to be respected and protected in his actual possession against third parties who burned his palay straw and plowed his landholding, his landlords were not indispensable parties under Section 7, Rule 3 of the Rules of Court in his action for injunction and damages.[^7]

### Protection Based on Priority in Time

The right to be respected in possession applies regardless of the character of a party's possession, provided that the occupant has priority in time, as recognized in [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602)[^8] and [*Painaga v. Cortes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_53837_1991.html).[^9] In [*Mabalo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), the Supreme Court affirmed that regardless of the actual condition of title, a party in peaceable and quiet possession cannot be turned out by a strong hand, violence or terror and a party who proves prior physical possession can recover possession even against the owner.[^10] Likewise, in [*Montañez v. Mendoza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51691), the Supreme Court held that once a plaintiff in a forcible entry action establishes by a preponderance of evidence his prior physical possession of a six-hectare parcel and fixes its identity as distinct from the parcel covered by Original Certificate of Title No. P-658 invoked by the defendant, his action for possession cannot fail and he must be restored to possession pursuant to Article 539 of the Civil Code.[^11]

## Operation Against Owners, Co-Owners and Self-Help

### Prohibition Against Taking the Law into One's Own Hands

Article 539(1) operates together with Articles 429, 433 and 536 of the Civil Code to forbid self-help by an owner or rival claimant against an objecting possessor. Under Article 536, as applied in [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602),[^12] [*Bacar v. Del Rosario*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/mar1989/gr_51208_1989.html)[^13] and [*Buayan Cattle Co., Inc. v. Quintillan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/mar1984/gr_l-26970_1984.html),[^14] possession cannot be acquired through force or intimidation as long as there is a possessor who objects thereto and anyone who believes that he has an action or right to deprive another of the holding of a thing must invoke the aid of a competent court if the holder refuses delivery. Under Article 433, as enforced in [*Spouses Castro v. Spouses Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35087)[^15] and [*Busilac Builders, Inc. v. Aguilar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41960),[^16] actual possession under claim of ownership raises a disputable presumption of ownership and the true owner must resort to judicial process for the recovery of the property. Conversely, under Article 429, it is the owner or lawful possessor already in possession who may use such force as may be reasonably necessary to repel or prevent an actual or threatened unlawful physical invasion or usurpation of his property.[^17]

The Supreme Court applied Articles 433 and 539(1) in [*Busilac Builders, Inc. v. Aguilar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41960) to discipline a Regional Trial Court judge who had purchased a 100-square-meter portion of a 426-square-meter lot in Laoag City while still a prosecutor and obtained Transfer Certificate of Title No. T-32810.[^18] Because the complainants were in prior physical possession of the lot and had enclosed it with concrete posts and barbed wires, the respondent judge practically took the law into his own hands when he removed the fences and spearheaded the leveling of the lot with a rented payloader instead of resorting to judicial remedies.[^19] The Supreme Court found him guilty of impropriety under Canon 2 and Rule 2.01 of the Code of Judicial Conduct and fined him P11,000.00 for that charge.[^20]

### Contractual Possessors Versus the Owner's *Jus Possidendi*

Even where a party is the undisputed owner of property, the right of possession as an incident of ownership (*jus possidendi*) cannot be exercised to the prejudice of a party whose possession is predicated on a contract such as agency, trust, pledge or lease.[^21] In [*Metro Manila Transit Corporation v. D.M. Consortium, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41302), the Supreme Court held under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code that a party vested with a contractual right of possession may set up that right even against the owner.[^22] Because the trial court and the Court of Appeals found no default for three consecutive months under the lease-purchase agreement covering 228 buses and the trial court found that the lessee-purchaser had already paid about P106 million (more than its original obligation of P71 million), the owner's seizure of the buses was not justified.[^23] Since eleven years had elapsed and the 228 buses could no longer be returned in their original state and considering that the lessee had substantially performed its obligation in good faith under Article 1234 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals' directives ordering the owner to pay the value of the buses at the time of repossession in December 1989 and P2,000,000.00 for the appropriation and use of the lessee's furniture, fixtures and other equipment.[^24]

A lessor who forcibly evicts a lessee during the lease term without awaiting a final judicial judgment likewise violates Article 1654(3) and Article 539(1) of the Civil Code.[^25] In [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863), the lessor unilaterally rescinded a four-year lease Memorandum of Agreement covering seminary advertising spaces, filed an unlawful detainer complaint before the Metropolitan Trial Court of Makati and then dismantled the lessee's billboards while the ejectment suit was still pending.[^26] Rejecting the Court of Appeals' view that the expiration of the four-year lease on July 31, 1997 barred restoration of possession, the Supreme Court held that a lessee unlawfully evicted by the lessor is entitled to be restored to possession for the unused and unexpired portion of the lease contract counted from eviction.[^27] Because the lessee had occupied the premises for only three years, two months and two days of the agreed four-year term, restoring possession for the remaining period (except as to areas already leased to a non-party corporation before the ejectment suit) merely enforced the contract rather than extending it.[^28]

### Ejectment Between Co-Owners

In [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), the Supreme Court re-examined the rules governing ejectment suits between co-owners under Articles 487, 536 and 539(1) of the Civil Code.[^29] Under Articles 486 and 493, a buyer of a specific portion of an unpartitioned parcel steps into the shoes of the selling co-owner as a co-owner of the undivided property and co-owners hold the common property in a fiduciary relationship akin to trustees for one another.[^30] However, a co-owner's right of possession carries a corresponding obligation to respect the prior possession of the other co-owners and not to wrest exclusive possession by force.[^31] The Supreme Court laid down four rules governing ejectment suits between co-owners:

1. If a co-owner takes possession of a definite portion of the common property in the exercise of his or her right to possession as a co-owner, he or she may not be ejected as long as he or she recognizes the co-ownership, because he or she possesses it as a trustee for the co-ownership.[^32]
2. If a co-owner takes exclusive possession of a specific portion of the common property, resulting in the exclusion or deprivation of another co-owner in prior possession, any co-owner may file an action for ejectment to evict the co-owner who wrested possession by force.[^33]
3. To evict a co-owner from the common property, the plaintiff co-owner bears the burden of proving that the defendant co-owner employed force, intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth when coming into possession of the common property.[^34]
4. Failing to meet that requirement, the plaintiff co-owner can neither exclude the defendant co-owner nor recover a determinate part of the property.[^35]

Because the buyer in [*Mabalo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602) entered an undivided 5,599-square-meter parcel in Misamis Oriental the day after her purchase, ordered the co-heirs' laborers to desist from trimming a tree, built a perimeter fence with a no-trespassing sign and demolished two houses on the portion she claimed, she excluded the co-owners who were in prior physical possession by force.[^36] The Supreme Court affirmed her eviction and the order to remove her improvements, while deleting the lower court's award of P300.00 monthly rent (both for lack of supporting evidence of fair rental value and because it is absurd to require a co-owner to pay rent on common property) and P5,000.00 attorney's fees (because no legal reason was stated in the body of the trial court decisions).[^37]

## Protection of Movable Property and Third-Party Possessors

### Good-Faith Possessors of Movables Under Articles 527, 539 and 559

For movable property, as applied in [*BA Finance Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33853)[^38] and [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html),[^39] Article 539(1) operates in conjunction with Article 527 (which presumes good faith and places the burden of proving bad faith on the person alleging it) and Article 559 (which provides that the possession of movable property acquired in good faith is equivalent to a title). An acquirer and possessor in good faith of a chattel is entitled to be respected and protected in his possession as if he were the true owner until a competent court rules otherwise.[^40]

In [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html), a buyer purchased cast iron scrap engine blocks from a steel corporation using a check for P61,808.25 that was dishonored for insufficient funds and two days later sold 50,000 kilos of the scrap engine blocks for P44,000.00 to the proprietors of a junk shop under a sales invoice in the ordinary course of business.[^41] The steel corporation filed an *estafa* complaint and procured a search warrant under which police officers seized 42.8 metric tons of the engine blocks from the junk shop and delivered them to the steel corporation's premises for safekeeping.[^42] Applying *Chua Hai v. Kapunan, Jr.*, as cited in [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html), along with Articles 539 and 559 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that although the search warrant was validly issued upon probable cause, the filing of an *estafa* information against the transferor did not entitle the original seller to retain the goods in the absence of a final judgment of restitution in the criminal case under Article 105 of the Revised Penal Code.[^43] Finding cogency in the view of Justice Felix in his concurring opinion in *Chua Hai*, as cited in [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html), the Supreme Court distinguished theft and robbery, where the owner is involuntarily deprived of property, from a case like this one where the owner voluntarily delivered the goods in the expectation of being paid.[^44] Applying by analogy the rule of *Eliason v. Wilborn*, as cited in [*Yu*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html), it added that as between two innocent persons, the one who made the loss possible by its act of confidence must bear the loss.[^45]

### Replevin by a Chattel Mortgagee Against a Third-Party Possessor

Article 539(1) also protects a third-party possessor of a movable against a chattel mortgagee who fails to implead and serve summons upon the principal debtor-mortgagor. In [*BA Finance Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33853), the assignee of a promissory note and chattel mortgage over a Ford Cortina motor vehicle filed a replevin suit against the mortgagor spouses and a John Doe, seized the vehicle under a writ of replevin from a third-party possessor in Sorsogon and then failed to serve summons on the mortgagor spouses.[^46] Quoting with approval the Court of Appeals' reliance on Articles 539, 527 and 559 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that a chattel mortgagee's right of possession is conditioned upon the actual fact of the mortgagor's default.[^47] Where a replevin suit is brought to foreclose a chattel mortgage and a third-party possessor asserts an adverse and independent claim, the mortgagor is an indispensable party without whom no final determination can be had.[^48] An adverse possessor who is not the mortgagor cannot be deprived of possession simply because the mortgagee files a replevin action and the Supreme Court affirmed the dismissal of the complaint and the order to return the vehicle with all its accessories to the third-party possessor.[^49]

### Protection Against *Ex Parte* Writs of Possession and Receivership

In real property foreclosure and partition suits, Article 539(1) bars procedural shortcuts that dispossess actual occupants without an opportunity to be heard or without extreme necessity:

- **Improperly enforced *ex parte* writs of possession:** In [*Spouses Castro v. Spouses Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35087), the mortgagors sold an unregistered 417-square-meter parcel of land in Hagonoy, Bulacan in 1997 to third-party buyers who took possession, allegedly built a house and obtained a new tax declaration in their name prior to the mortgagees' extrajudicial foreclosure purchase in February 1999.[^50] While the mortgagees' civil action to annul the sale and tax declaration was pending before Branch 7 of the Malolos Regional Trial Court, the mortgagees obtained an *ex parte* writ of possession from Branch 16 of the same court and evicted the third-party buyers.[^51] Affirming Branch 7's issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction anchored on Article 539(1), the Supreme Court held that enforcing an *ex parte* writ of possession against third parties who bought and possessed the property before foreclosure and who did not take part in the foreclosure proceedings disturbed the *status quo ante litem* and was contrary to Article 433 of the Civil Code, which contemplates a judicial process where the third-party possessor is given an opportunity to be heard.[^52]
- **Receivership over disputed properties in a party's possession:** In [*Vivares v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44835), an executor and an heir suing for partition of an estate moved to place the disputed properties under receivership, alleging unapproved transfers by the respondent co-heir.[^53] Invoking Article 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that between the actual possessor of disputed properties and a party asserting contrary rights, the possessor is accorded better rights and in litigation ought not to be deprived of possession except in exceptional and extreme cases.[^54] Citing *Descallar v. Court of Appeals*, as cited in [*Vivares v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44835), it added that the appointment of a receiver is not proper where the rights of the parties, one of whom is in possession of the property, are still to be determined by the trial court.[^55] Because the petitioners failed to adduce clear and convincing evidence of the alleged fraud, notices of *lis pendens* were already annotated on the properties, the respondent was in actual possession of some of the disputed properties and the respondent offered to post a counterbond under Section 3, Rule 59 of the Rules of Court, the Supreme Court affirmed the discharge of the court-appointed receiver upon the posting of a PhP 100,000 counterbond while maintaining the notice of *lis pendens* on Tax Declaration No. 112 pending trial.[^56]

## Judicial Means to Protect or Restore Possession

### The Three Actions to Recover Possession of Real Property and *In Personam* Jurisdiction

In [*Emilia v. Bado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html), the Supreme Court summarized the three ordinary and summary judicial remedies available to recover possession of real property under Philippine law:

1. **Forcible entry or unlawful detainer:** The summary action brought within one year in the municipal court to recover physical possession only, in which a preliminary mandatory injunction may be sought within ten days from the filing of the forcible entry complaint under Article 539(2) of the Civil Code.[^57]
2. ***Accion publiciana*:** The plenary action in an ordinary civil proceeding in the Court of First Instance to recover the right to possess, as described in [*Emilia v. Bado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html),[^58] [*Gabriel, Jr. v. Crisologo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57063)[^59] and [*Aguilon v. Bohol*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_27169_1977.html).[^60]
3. ***Acción de reivindicación*:** The plenary action to recover ownership, including the *jus utendi* and *jus fruendi*.[^61]

Because these ordinary and summary actions provide a plain, speedy and adequate remedy at law, an independent action for injunction cannot be used to take property out of the possession or control of one party and place it into that of another whose title has not been clearly established.[^62] In [*Emilia*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html), both the plaintiff and the defendant held subsisting Torrens titles (Torrens Title O-267 over Lot 1131 and Torrens Title O-275 over adjoining Lot 2894) and disputed whether a house under construction encroached on a 48-square-meter border area that the plaintiff said the defendants had entered on or about December 1, 1962.[^63] The Supreme Court affirmed the dissolution of the *ex parte* preliminary injunction and the dismissal of the injunction complaint because the dispute presented a legitimate issue of ownership that should have been brought as an *acción de reivindicación*.[^64]

In [*Gabriel, Jr. v. Crisologo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57063), the Supreme Court described *accion publiciana* as an ordinary civil proceeding to determine the better right of possession of realty independently of title, filed after the expiration of one year from the accrual of the cause of action or from the unlawful withholding of possession.[^65] The Supreme Court then applied Article 539(1) of the Civil Code in an *accion publiciana* to restore possession of two Baguio City lots covered by Transfer Certificate of Title Nos. T-13935 and T-13936 to the registered owner, who had purchased the lots in 1967, paid realty taxes since 1969, appointed an administrator and offered to sell the occupied portions to the defendants.[^66] While a court in an *accion publiciana* may pass upon ownership provisionally when raised by the parties to determine who has the better right of possession, the defendants' contention that the titles were void under Presidential Decree No. 1271 constituted an impermissible collateral attack barred by Section 48 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 and under Section 6 of Presidential Decree No. 1271 only the Solicitor General may institute actions to recover possession of lands covered by void titles not validated under that decree.[^67]

Furthermore, in [*Domagas v. Jensen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43231), the Supreme Court clarified the jurisdictional nature of ejectment suits under Article 539(1).[^68] Correcting the Court of Appeals' characterization of ejectment as an action *quasi in rem*, the Supreme Court held that an action for forcible entry or unlawful detainer is a real action and an action *in personam* because the plaintiff seeks to enforce a personal obligation or liability on the defendant under Article 539 of the Civil Code to vacate the property, restore physical possession to the plaintiff and pay actual damages by way of reasonable compensation for use or occupation.[^69] Consequently, the court must acquire jurisdiction over the person of the defendant through valid service of summons.[^70] Where the defendant was in Oslo, Norway and the sheriff left the summons with her brother who was merely visiting her house in Calasiao, Pangasinan to collect rent from a lessee, the substituted service failed to comply with Section 7, Rule 14 of the Rules of Court, rendering the Municipal Trial Court's ejectment judgment null and void.[^71]

### Possessory Actions Over Public Lands and Administrative Exhaustion

When a dispute involves public agricultural, homestead or pasture land, the applicability of the doctrine of exhaustion of administrative remedies depends on whether the plaintiff is a prior peaceful possessor seeking protection against forcible intrusion or a usurper seeking to enjoin administrative authorities and the prior occupant:

| Case | Plaintiff's Status & Action | Administrative Proceeding | Supreme Court Ruling under Art. 539 |

| [*Aguilon v. Filed *accion publiciana* in CFI against an alleged 1957 intruder. | Defendant's 1958 protest against plaintiff's Homestead Application No. 20-1846 pending before the Director of Lands. | Regular court has jurisdiction under Art. 539(1) to restore possession and forestall breaches of peace. The Director of Lands' authority over public land disposition does not exclude judicial jurisdiction over possessory actions. |
| [*Painaga v. Cortes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_53837_1991.html)[^73] | Prior possessor since 1962 purchase. ## Writ of Preliminary Mandatory Injunction to Restore Possession

### Statutory Purpose, Exception to General Rule and Requisites

Under the general rule governing injunctions, as stated in [*Mara, Inc. v. Estrella*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html)[^75] and [*Emilia v. Bado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html),[^76] a writ of preliminary injunction is not proper where its purpose is to take property out of the possession or control of one person and place it in the hands of another whose title has not been clearly established by law. Prior to the promulgation of the New Civil Code, it was deemed improper to issue a writ of preliminary injunction where the party to be enjoined had already taken complete material possession of the property (*Torre v. Querubin*, as cited in [*Semirara Coal Corporation v. HGL Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40420)).[^77]

As the Supreme Court explained in [*Mara, Inc. v. Estrella*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html), the Code Commission noted that prolonged litigations between the owner and the usurper frequently deprived the owner of possession even when he had an immediate right thereto.[^78] To remedy that mischief, Article 539(2) of the Civil Code created an exception to the general rule by allowing a possessor deprived of possession through forcible entry to move within ten days from the filing of the complaint for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore him in his possession and by directing the court to decide the motion within thirty days from its filing.[^79]

In [*Mara, Inc.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html), the registered owner of four Quezon City lots covered by Torrens titles derived from Original Certificate of Title No. 735 filed a motion for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction four days after filing its forcible entry complaint.[^80] The City Court delayed resolving the motion for more than nineteen months (which the Supreme Court condemned as deplorable given the thirty-day statutory mandate in Article 539(2)) before finally issuing the writ upon a P10,000.00 bond, only to dissolve the writ two months later when the defendant offered a counterbond and submitted an unserved check for P10,000.00 instead of a surety bond.[^81] The Supreme Court held that where the petitioner is the registered owner under unassailable Torrens titles and the oppositor is an interloper or squatter who shows no indubitable right to possess, a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction may issue *pendente lite* and the City Court committed grave abuse of discretion in dissolving the writ on the basis of an insufficient, unserved bond in violation of Section 8, Rule 58 of the Rules of Court.[^82]

### Availability in *Accion Publiciana* and Recovery of Possession Suits

Although the text of Article 539(2) refers to an action for forcible entry, the Supreme Court held in [*Semirara Coal Corporation v. HGL Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40420) that under Article 539 of the Civil Code, any disturbance of a lawful possessor's possession is a ground for the issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore possession during the pendency of an action to recover possession, including a case alleged to be an *accion publiciana*.[^83] In [*Semirara Coal Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40420), the grantee of a 25-year Forest Land Grazing Lease Agreement (FLGLA No. 184) over 367 hectares in Semirara Island, Antique permitted a coal mining operator in 1999 to pass its trucks through the grazing land, but the mining company constructed administrative offices, residences, an access road and a coal stockyard and conducted blasting and excavation that decimated the lessee's cattle.[^84] In the lessee's 2003 action for recovery of possession before the Regional Trial Court of Culasi, Antique, Branch 13, the Supreme Court affirmed the issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction restoring possession to the grazing lessee upon showing the three requisites for mandatory injunction:

1. The invasion of the right is material and substantial.[^85]
2. The right of the complainant is clear and unmistakable.[^86]
3. There is an urgent and permanent necessity for the writ to prevent serious damage.[^87]

The grazing lessee possessed a clear and unmistakable right under FLGLA No. 184 (whose unilateral cancellation by the Department of Environment and Natural Resources was enjoined by the Regional Trial Court of Caloocan City in a separate action), whereas the mining operator's entry was merely by the lessee's tolerance.[^88]

### Extension to Unlawful Detainer Appeals Under Article 1674

In original ejectment proceedings before the municipal or city court, the Supreme Court noted in [*Dayao v. Shell Company of the Philippines, Ltd.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/apr1980/gr_32475_1980.html) that Article 539(2) of the Civil Code refers to the issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction in forcible entry cases in the original court, whereas the older rule in *Piit v. De Lara & Velez* and *Dikit v. Ycasiano*, as cited in [*Dayao*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/apr1980/gr_32475_1980.html), that a Justice of the Peace Court cannot issue a writ of preliminary injunction in unlawful detainer cases had not been superseded.[^89] (Later, in [*Domagas v. Jensen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43231)[^90] and [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863),[^91] the Supreme Court quoted or cited Section 15, Rule 70 of the 1997 Rules of Civil Procedure, under which a possessor deprived of possession through forcible entry or unlawful detainer may move within five days from the filing of the complaint for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction.)

When an ejectment or unlawful detainer case is appealed, as held in [*Dayao v. Shell Company of the Philippines, Ltd.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/apr1980/gr_32475_1980.html)[^92] and [*De Laureano v. Adil*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1976/jul1976/gr_43345_1976.html),[^93] Article 1674 of the Civil Code (reproduced in Section 9, Rule 70 of the Rules of Court) expressly extends the remedy granted in Article 539(2) to the appellate court upon motion of the lessor within ten days from the perfection of the appeal, if the higher court is satisfied that the lessee's appeal is frivolous or dilatory or that the lessor's appeal is *prima facie* meritorious.

- **Lessor's *prima facie* meritorious appeal from a dismissal:** In [*Dayao*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/apr1980/gr_32475_1980.html), the City Court of Quezon City dismissed a sublessor's unlawful detainer complaint against a gasoline station dealer and the sublessor appealed to the Court of First Instance of Rizal, moved to admit an amended complaint conforming to evidence of contract violations tried in the City Court and moved for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction.[^94] The Supreme Court upheld the Court of First Instance's issuance of the writ of preliminary mandatory injunction upon a P5,000.00 bond under Article 1674 in relation to Article 539(2) because the appellate judge found the lessor's appeal *prima facie* meritorious.[^95]
- **Lessee's frivolous and dilatory appeal and effect of a supersedeas bond:** In [*De Laureano v. Adil*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1976/jul1976/gr_43345_1976.html), the City Court of Iloilo City rendered judgment ordering an overstaying lessee whose fifteen-year lease had expired to vacate two lots, remove his buildings and pay P12,428.00 monthly compensation, but erroneously allowed the lessee *ex parte* to post an inadequate P22,000.00 supersedeas bond and deposit only P1,200.00 monthly.[^96] Within nine days from notice of the appellate docketing, the lessor moved in the Court of First Instance for immediate execution and for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction under Article 1674 and Section 9, Rule 70.[^97] Reversing the trial judge's denial of the mandatory injunction, the Supreme Court held that there is no inconsistency between allowing a defendant to stay execution of monetary liability by filing a sufficient supersedeas bond and making monthly deposits under Section 8, Rule 70 and simultaneously issuing a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction under Articles 539(2) and 1674 to restore physical possession to the lessor because the lessee's appeal is manifestly frivolous and dilatory.[^98] Under Article 1671, an overstaying lessee is a possessor in bad faith governed by Article 1678 (not Articles 448 and 546) and has no right of retention, although immediate possession under the mandatory injunction covers only the portions of the lots not occupied by the lessee's buildings while removal of the buildings requires a special order of demolition after notice and hearing under Section 14, Rule 39 of the Rules of Court.[^99]

### [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602): Forcible Entry Between Co-Owners

In [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), the children of the late Roman Babuyo took physical possession of and introduced improvements on an undivided 5,599-square-meter parcel of land (Lot 943-Cad-361-D, covered by Original Certificate of Title No. P-10402, Free Patent No. 575915) in Kauswagan, Lagonglong, Misamis Oriental.[^100] Segundina Taranza Baboyo, claiming to be the daughter of another heir named Rufino Babuyo, sold a specific 364-square-meter portion of the unpartitioned lot to Perlita Mabalo on June 2, 2014.[^101] On June 3, 2014, Mabalo ordered the laborers hired by the heirs of Roman to desist from trimming a tree on the lot, constructed a fence with a no-trespassing sign around the portion, pruned plants and caused the demolition of two houses built thereon.[^102] On July 10, 2014, the heirs of Roman filed a complaint for forcible entry (Civil Case No. 04-2014) before the 4th Municipal Circuit Trial Court of Balingasag-Lagonglong, which rendered a Decision on February 24, 2015 ordering Mabalo and all persons claiming rights under her to immediately vacate the portion, remove her improvements, pay P300.00 per month as rental from June 3, 2014 until surrender of the portion, pay P5,000.00 as attorney's fees with costs and dismissing her counterclaim.[^103] Branch 20 of the Regional Trial Court of Cagayan de Oro City affirmed in a May 6, 2016 Resolution and the Court of Appeals affirmed in a March 15, 2018 Decision in CA-G.R. SP No. 07450-MIN.[^104]

On a Petition for Review on Certiorari, the Supreme Court applied Articles 487, 493, 536 and 539(1) of the Civil Code and held that although the sale of a specific portion of an unpartitioned property is valid to the extent of transferring the vendor's *pro indiviso* share and making the buyer a co-owner, every possessor has a right to be respected in possession under Article 539(1) and a co-owner cannot wrest exclusive possession of a specific portion by force from co-owners who were in prior physical possession.[^105] The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the March 15, 2018 Decision of the Court of Appeals with modification, ordering Mabalo and all persons claiming rights under her to immediately vacate the portion of land covered by Original Certificate of Title No. P-10402, Free Patent No. 575915 and to remove the improvements she introduced thereon, while deleting the awards of P300.00 monthly rent and P5,000.00 attorney's fees.[^106]

### [*Gabriel, Jr. v. Crisologo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57063): *Accion Publiciana* by a Registered Torrens Titleholder

In [*Gabriel, Jr. v. Crisologo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57063), Carmeling Crisologo, represented by her attorney-in-fact Pedro Isican, filed a complaint for recovery of possession and/or ownership with damages (Civil Case No. 13209) before the Municipal Trial Court in Cities, Branch 1, Baguio City against Juliet B. Pulkera, Paul P. Gabriel, Ireneo C. Calwag and Thomas L. Tingga-an over two parcels of land with a total area of approximately 2,000 square meters covered by Transfer Certificate of Title Nos. T-13935 and T-13936 registered on August 24, 1967.[^107] Crisologo alleged that in 2006 she discovered that the petitioners had entered her lots without her consent, built houses thereon, reneged on a promise to buy the lots at P3,500.00 per square meter and refused demands to vacate.[^108] The petitioners countered that Crisologo's titles were void under *Republic v. Marcos* and Presidential Decree No. 1271 for lack of validation.[^109] On September 15, 2009, the Municipal Trial Court in Cities rendered judgment directing the defendants and all persons acting on their behalf to immediately vacate the properties and demolish or dismantle their houses (or allow the plaintiff to demolish them at the defendants' expense), to pay reasonable rentals of Php4,000.00 per month from January 2006 for each of the defendants and to pay Php20, 000.00 as attorney's fees and costs of litigation.[^110] The Regional Trial Court, Branch 6, Baguio City reversed on April 18, 2011, but the Court of Appeals in its June 14, 2012 Decision and November 14, 2012 Resolution set aside the Regional Trial Court decision and reinstated the Municipal Trial Court in Cities decision.[^111]

On a petition for review on certiorari under Rule 45, the Supreme Court held that the action was an *accion publiciana* in which the issue is possession rather than conclusive ownership, that the petitioners' challenge to Crisologo's Torrens titles was a prohibited collateral attack under Section 48 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 (and only the Solicitor General can sue to recover lands under unvalidated titles pursuant to Section 6 of Presidential Decree No. 1271) and that Crisologo established a preferred claim of possession entitling her to be respected and restored to her lawful possession under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code.[^112] The Supreme Court denied the petition, thereby affirming the Court of Appeals' reinstatement of the Municipal Trial Court in Cities judgment and all its vacatur, demolition, Php4,000.00 monthly rental per defendant, Php20, 000.00 attorney's fees and costs directives.[^113]

### [*Spouses Castro v. Spouses Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35087): Mandatory Injunction Against an *Ex Parte* Writ of Possession

In [*Spouses Castro v. Spouses Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35087), Spouses Eduardo and Charito Perez obtained a P250,000.00 loan from petitioners Spouses Isagani and Diosdada Castro on November 15, 1996 secured by a real estate mortgage over an unregistered 417-square-meter parcel of land in San Isidro, Hagonoy, Bulacan covered by Tax Declaration No. 01844.[^114] Before the petitioners extrajudicially foreclosed the mortgage and bought the property on February 4, 1999, Spouses Perez sold the land in 1997 to respondents Spouses Regino Se and Violeta dela Cruz, who took possession, built a house (by their own allegation) and caused the cancellation of Tax Declaration No. 01844 and the issuance of Tax Declaration No. 01892 in their name on August 15, 1997.[^115] On April 8, 1999, the petitioners filed a complaint for annulment of the deed of sale and Tax Declaration No. 01892 and damages before Branch 7 of the Malolos Regional Trial Court and during its pendency obtained an *ex parte* writ of possession dated August 2, 2001 from Branch 16 of the Malolos Regional Trial Court, evicting Spouses dela Cruz.[^116] In their Answer to the Amended Complaint before Branch 7, Spouses dela Cruz prayed for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction, which Branch 7 granted by Order of October 29, 2004 (reconsideration denied March 5, 2007) anchored on Article 539 of the Civil Code and the Court of Appeals denied the petitioners' certiorari petition on September 14, 2009.[^117]

The Supreme Court held under Articles 539(1) and 433 of the Civil Code that because Spouses dela Cruz bought and took actual possession of the unregistered property in 1997 long before the 1999 foreclosure and were not parties to the foreclosure proceedings, enforcing an *ex parte* writ of possession against them disturbed the *status quo ante litem* and amounted to taking real property without proper judicial process where they could be heard.[^118] The Supreme Court denied the petition, upholding the Court of Appeals' decision and the trial court's writ of preliminary mandatory injunction restoring Spouses dela Cruz to physical possession pending trial on the merits of the principal action.[^119]

### [*Vivares v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44835): Protection of Actual Possessor Against Receivership

In [*Vivares v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44835), petitioners Julio A. Vivares (executor of the will of Torcuato Reyes) and Mila G. Ignaling (declared heir of Torcuato) filed an action for partition and recovery of real estate (Civil Case No. 517) before the Camiguin Regional Trial Court, Branch 28 against Torcuato's brother, respondent Engr. Jose J. Reyes, contesting properties remaining in the name of their late father Severino Reyes.[^120] After causing notices of *lis pendens* to be annotated on the disputed properties, the petitioners moved on March 15, 2000 to place the disputed estate under receivership, claiming that the respondent had fraudulently antedated transfers of common properties prior to Torcuato's death on May 12, 1992.[^121] The Regional Trial Court appointed Lope Salantin as receiver upon a PhP 50,000 bond on May 24, 2000 (reinstated September 5, 2000) and issued a Resolution on May 22, 2001 (reconsideration denied October 19, 2001) denying the respondent's motions to discharge the receiver upon a counterbond and to cancel the notice of *lis pendens* on Tax Declaration No. 112 covering Lot No. 33.[^122] On certiorari in CA-G.R. SP No. 67492, the Court of Appeals rendered a Decision on June 18, 2002 (reconsideration denied September 24, 2002) reversing the May 22, 2001 Resolution, discharging receiver Lope Salantin upon the respondent's posting of a P100,000.00 counterbond, cancelling the notice of *lis pendens* on Tax Declaration No. 112 insofar as it covered a portion allegedly owned by Elena Unchuan and remanding the case for further proceedings.[^123]

On review under Rule 45, the Supreme Court applied Article 539(1) of the Civil Code and held that because the petitioners failed to prove the alleged fraud, notices of *lis pendens* already safeguarded the properties, the respondent offered a counterbond under Section 3, Rule 59 of the Rules of Court and the respondent was in actual possession of some of the disputed properties, receivership was unwarranted where the rights of the parties remained to be determined by the trial court.[^124] However, the Supreme Court held that the Court of Appeals lacked factual basis to cancel the notice of *lis pendens* on Tax Declaration No. 112 because the trial court must determine whether Elena Unchuan's property was part of Lot No. 33.[^125] The Supreme Court partly granted the petition and affirmed the June 18, 2002 Court of Appeals Decision with modification, reversing and setting aside the May 22, 2001 Resolution of the Regional Trial Court of Camiguin, Branch 28 in Civil Case No. 517, discharging receiver Lope Salantin upon the posting of a PhP 100,000 counterbond, declaring that the notice of *lis pendens* in Tax Declaration No. 112 (including the portion allegedly belonging to Elena Unchuan) remains valid and effective and remanding the case to the trial court for further proceedings without costs.[^126]

### [*Metro Manila Transit Corporation v. D.M. Consortium, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41302): Lessee's Contractual Possession Against the Owner

In [*Metro Manila Transit Corporation v. D.M. Consortium, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41302), respondent D.M. Consortium, Inc. entered into a 1981 lease-purchase agreement with petitioner Metro Manila Transit Corporation to acquire 228 buses, under which the lessee could be deprived of possession only if it defaulted in paying rent for three consecutive months.[^127] Alleging default, the petitioner repossessed the buses and occupied the respondent's premises and offices on December 9, 1989 and scheduled a public auction.[^128] The respondent filed a petition for injunction in the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 93, which issued a preliminary injunction and, after trial, rendered a Decision on September 11, 1995 finding that the respondent was not in default and had already paid about P106 million (exceeding its original P71 million obligation) and ordering the petitioner to return all the buses and to pay P200,000.00 as moral damages, P100,000.00 for the use of furniture, fixtures and equipment, P500,000.00 as reasonable rent for the use of the buses from December 9, 1989, P100,000.00 as attorney's fees and costs.[^129] In CA-G.R. CV No. 54294, the Court of Appeals in its December 6, 1999 Decision affirmed the return of the buses while reversing and setting aside the money awards and in its March 16, 2001 Resolution modified its decision (because eleven years had passed and the buses could no longer be returned in their original condition) by adjudging the petitioner liable to pay the value of the 228 buses as of December 1989 (remanding the case to the lower court to determine that value) and requiring the petitioner to pay P2,000,000.00 for the appropriation and use of the respondent's furniture, fixtures and other equipment.[^130]

On appeal by certiorari under Rule 45, the Supreme Court held under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code that an owner cannot exercise *jus possidendi* to the prejudice of a lessee whose contractual right of possession had not been forfeited by a three-month default, that a party vested with the right of possession may set up that right even against the owner and that because the respondent had substantially performed its obligation under Article 1234 and the buses could no longer be returned in their original state, reimbursement of their value at the time of repossession was proper.[^131] Declining to review the factual basis of the P2,000,000.00 award for furniture, fixtures and equipment under Rule 45, the Supreme Court affirmed the December 6, 1999 Decision and March 16, 2001 Resolution (printed as March 6, 2001 in the dispositive paragraph) of the Court of Appeals and remanded the records to the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 93 to determine the value of the buses to be paid by the petitioner to the respondent, with costs against the petitioner.[^132]

### [*Semirara Coal Corporation v. HGL Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40420): Preliminary Mandatory Injunction in a Recovery of Possession Action

In [*Semirara Coal Corporation v. HGL Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40420), respondent HGL Development Corporation held Forest Land Grazing Lease Agreement No. 184 issued on September 28, 1984 for a 25-year term ending December 31, 2009 over 367 hectares of cattle-grazing land in Bobog and Pontod, Semirara, Caluya, Antique.[^133] In 1999, HGL Development Corporation granted petitioner Semirara Coal Corporation (now Semirara Mining Corporation), holder of a Coal Operating Contract over Semirara Island, conditional permission to pass its trucks and equipment through the property, but the petitioner built administrative offices and employee residences, conducted blasting and excavation, built an access road and maintained a coal stockyard without permission, damaging the land and decimating the cattle.[^134] After the Department of Environment and Natural Resources unilaterally cancelled FLGLA No. 184 on December 6, 2000, HGL Development Corporation filed Civil Case No. 20675 before the Regional Trial Court of Caloocan City (which issued a writ of preliminary injunction on December 22, 2003 enjoining the enforcement of the cancellation order) and simultaneously filed Civil Case No. C-146 on November 17, 2003 against the petitioner for recovery of possession and damages with prayer for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction before the Regional Trial Court of Culasi, Antique, Branch 13.[^135] On September 16, 2004, the Culasi trial court granted the application and issued the Writ of Preliminary Mandatory Injunction on October 6, 2004 commanding the petitioner to cease and desist from encroaching and to restore possession of the subject land to HGL Development Corporation, which the Court of Appeals affirmed on January 31, 2005 in CA G.R. CEB SP No. 00035.[^136]

On a petition for review on certiorari, the Supreme Court noted that the petitioner's certiorari petition before the Court of Appeals was defective for failure to move for reconsideration of the trial court's September 16, 2004 Resolution and held on the merits that under Article 539 of the Civil Code a plaintiff in an action to recover possession (even if alleged to be an *accion publiciana*) may avail of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore possession *pendente lite* where the invasion is material and substantial, the complainant's right under its subsisting grazing lease is clear and unmistakable as against an occupant by mere tolerance and urgent necessity exists to prevent irreparable injury to its cattle-grazing business.[^137] The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the January 31, 2005 Decision of the Court of Appeals and the September 16, 2004 Resolution and October 6, 2004 Writ of Preliminary Mandatory Injunction of the Regional Trial Court of Culasi, Antique, Branch 13 and lifted its own temporary restraining order without pronouncement as to costs.[^138]

### [*Busilac Builders, Inc. v. Aguilar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41960): Judicial Discipline for Self-Help Dispossession

In [*Busilac Builders, Inc. v. Aguilar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41960), complainant Romeo M. Camarillo, president of Busilac Builders, Inc., agreed in 1993 to purchase Lots 1, 2 and 3 in Laoag City from Spouses Anatalio and Pacita Ramos, took physical possession of the 426-square-meter Lot 1, fenced it with concrete posts and barbed wires and filed Civil Case No. 12310 for specific performance on July 16, 2001 before the Regional Trial Court of Laoag City, Branch 12.[^139] Respondent Judge Charles A. Aguilar had purchased a 100-square-meter portion of Lot 1 on February 14, 2001 while still a prosecutor. After his appointment as Presiding Judge of Branch 12 on August 21, 2001, he failed to inhibit himself from Civil Case No. 12310, dismissed the case without prejudice on April 26, 2002, obtained Transfer Certificate of Title No. T-32810 over his 100-square-meter portion on July 1, 2002 and on October 12, 2002, together with the other co-owners of Lot 1, removed Camarillo's concrete posts and barbed wires and spearheaded the leveling of the lot with a payloader rented from the Municipality of Bacarra.[^140] He also appeared twice as his own counsel in Civil Case No. 12635 before Branch 16 of the same court on September 23 and 29, 2003 without prior written permission from the Supreme Court and issued a search warrant against Camarillo on Sunday, November 10, 2002.[^141]

In the administrative complaint filed by Busilac Builders, Inc. and Camarillo, the Supreme Court invoked Articles 433 and 539(1) of the Civil Code and held that even a registered lot owner must resort to judicial process to recover possession from an actual possessor and cannot take the law into his own hands by removing fences and leveling the land himself.[^142] The Supreme Court found Judge Aguilar: (1) guilty of violating Section 1, Rule 137 of the Rules of Court and Rule 3.12, Canon 3 of the Code of Judicial Conduct for failing to disqualify and inhibit himself in Civil Case No. 12310 and ordering its dismissal, suspending him for three months without pay under Section 11, Rule 140 of the Rules of Court, (2) guilty of impropriety for spearheading the leveling of Lot 1, fining him P11,000.00 and (3) guilty of violating Section 12, Rule XVIII of the Revised Civil Service Rules for appearing as counsel in two hearings of Civil Case No. 12635 without prior Supreme Court permission, reprimanding him with a warning, while dismissing for lack of merit the charge of oppression and evident bad faith regarding the issuance of the search warrant.[^143]

### [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863): Restoration of Unlawfully Evicted Lessee for the Unused Lease Period

In [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863), the Roman Catholic Archbishop of Manila leased advertising spaces in the Our Lady of Guadalupe Minor Seminary and San Carlos a stipulation that ASTRO would remit rentals directly to the church and subsequently executed a Memorandum of Agreement on September 28, 1993 fixing a four-year lease over the advertising areas from August 1, 1993 to July 31, 1997.[^144] After ASTRO's sublease expired in February 1995, the lessor leased those sublet spaces to Macgraphics Carranz International Corporation, notified Limitless Potentials, Inc. that it considered the Memorandum of Agreement rescinded as of October 31, 1995, demanded back rentals and damages totaling P1,021,219.15 after Limitless Potentials, Inc. had filed Civil Case No. 95-1559 in the Regional Trial Court of Makati to consign P300,000.00 and recover P990,462.90 plus P1,000,000.00 each as moral damages and P100,000.00 attorney's fees, filed an unlawful detainer suit (Civil Case No. 50450) in the Metropolitan Trial Court of Makati on November 13, 1995 praying for rentals plus P50,000.00 attorney's fees and P2,000.00 per hearing appearance fees and dismantled the billboards of Limitless Potentials, Inc. on October 5, 1996 while the ejectment suit was pending.[^145] In Civil Case No. 50450, Limitless Potentials, Inc. claimed that ASTRO's direct rental payments of P832,920.00 during the first period should be deducted from its P553,134.00 accountability to yield a P279,786.00 overpayment and on May 25, 2000 the Metropolitan Trial Court of Makati rendered judgment declaring the lessee's possession lawful, ordering the lessee to pay monthly rentals from September 1995 until it stopped occupying the premises, ordering the lessor to pay the lessee P100,000.00 as damages and finding from its account reconciliation (P1,584,185.82 paid less P1,239,635.00 due) a rental overpayment of P344,550.33 without crediting ASTRO's P832,920.00 SP No. 71261, where the lessor argued among other things that non-party Macgraphics Carranz International Corporation had introduced P20,000,000.00 worth of improvements, the Court of Appeals on June 30, 2003 set aside the Regional Trial Court rulings and affirmed the Metropolitan Trial Court decision with the modification ordering the lessor to return P344,550.33 with legal interest from finality.[^148]

No. 157391, partially granted the petitions in G.R. ### [*Domagas v. Jensen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43231): Forcible Entry as an Action *In Personam*

In [*Domagas v. Jensen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43231), petitioner Filomena Domagas filed a complaint for forcible entry on February 19, 1999 (Civil Case No. 879) before the Municipal Trial Court of Calasiao, Pangasinan against respondent Vivian Layno Jensen, alleging that on January 9, 1999 the respondent excavated and fenced a 68-square-meter portion of the petitioner's 827-square-meter land covered by Original Certificate of Title No. P-30980 in Barangay Buenlag, Calasiao, Pangasinan.[^151] On April 5, 1999, while the respondent was in Oslo, Norway (she had leased her Buenlag house to Eduardo Gonzales since November 24, 1997), the sheriff served the summons and complaint on the respondent's brother Oscar Layno, who was merely visiting the leased house to collect rentals.[^152] On May 17, 1999, the Municipal Trial Court rendered judgment ordering the respondent to vacate the 68-square-meter area and to pay P1,000.00 monthly rental, P20,000.00 actual damages, P15,000.00 attorney's fees, P20,000.00 exemplary damages and costs.[^153] The respondent filed an action (Civil Case No. 2000-0244-D) before the Regional Trial Court of Dagupan City, Branch 44 to annul the Municipal Trial Court judgment. The Regional Trial Court declared the Municipal Trial Court decision null and void for lack of jurisdiction and ordered Domagas to pay Jensen P50,000.00 actual damages, P50,000.00 attorney's fees, P50,000.00 moral damages, P50,000.00 exemplary damages and costs, which the Court of Appeals affirmed with modifications on May 6, 2003 in CA-G.R. CV No. 73995 on the ground that ejectment is an action *quasi in rem* requiring extraterritorial service.[^154]

On review under Rule 45, the Supreme Court held that although the Court of Appeals erred in classifying ejectment as an action *quasi in rem* (because an action for forcible entry or unlawful detainer is a real action and an action *in personam* seeking to enforce a personal obligation or liability on the defendant under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code to vacate, restore physical possession and pay reasonable compensation), jurisdiction over the defendant's person is mandatory in an action *in personam*.[^155] Because the respondent remained a resident of Barangay Buenlag who had left for Oslo, Norway on February 17, 1999, the summons could be validly served on her only through substituted service under Section 7, Rule 14 of the Rules of Court.[^156] Because Oscar Layno was merely a visitor collecting rent at a house leased to a tenant and the sheriff's return failed to show strict compliance with Section 7, Rule 14 of the Rules of Court, substituted service was ineffective and the Municipal Trial Court decision in Civil Case No. 879 was null and void.[^157] The Supreme Court denied the petition for lack of merit without costs.[^158]

### [*Montañez v. Mendoza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51691): Proof of Prior Possession and Identity of Land in Forcible Entry

In [*Montañez v. Mendoza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51691), petitioner Cesar Montañez filed a complaint for forcible entry with damages in April 1995 (Civil Case No. 1163) before the Municipal Trial Court of San Mateo, Rizal against respondent Nestor Mendoza, alleging that he and his family had possessed and cultivated a six-hectare parcel of land in Sitio Lumbangan, Barangay Pintong Bukawe (formerly Maarat), San Mateo, Rizal since 1970 (as certified by the Community Environment and Natural Resources Office) until the respondent entered in mid-May 1994 and dispossessed him by force and intimidation.[^159] The respondent answered that he built his house with the administrator's authority on land titled in the name of Ramon Mendoza under Original Certificate of Title No. P-658.[^160] On August 1, 1996, the Municipal Trial Court of San Mateo, Rizal rendered judgment in favor of the petitioner, ordering the respondent and all persons claiming rights under him to vacate the property and surrender possession to the petitioner, remove the improvements he introduced and pay P5,000.00 as attorney's fees and costs, while dismissing the respondent's counterclaims.[^161] The Regional Trial Court of San Mateo, Rizal, Branch 77 affirmed on September 16, 1998, but the Court of Appeals in its April 17, 2000 Decision and July 13, 2000 Resolution in CA-GR SP No. 49368 reversed the lower courts and awarded the respondent P10,000.00 attorney's fees on the ground that the petitioner failed to prove the identity of the land.[^162]

Reviewing the factual findings under the exception to Rule 45 where the Court of Appeals reverses the uniform findings of the trial courts, the Supreme Court found that the petitioner established by a preponderance of evidence both his prior physical possession and the distinct identity of the land claimed in his complaint as different from the parcel covered by Original Certificate of Title No. P-658, entitling him to be restored to possession pursuant to Article 539(1) of the Civil Code.[^163] The Supreme Court granted the petition, annulled and set aside the Court of Appeals decision and reinstated the August 1, 1996 Decision of the Municipal Trial Court of San Mateo, Rizal as affirmed by the Regional Trial Court, without costs.[^164]

### [*Philippine Trust Company v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36989): Lawful Possessor as Real Party in Interest After Expiration of Redemption Period

In [*Philippine Trust Company v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36989), petitioner Philippine Trust Company foreclosed a mortgage executed by the Policarpio spouses over lands covered by Transfer Certificate of Title No. 4144 (now 51668) and Transfer Certificate of Title No. 24182, consolidated ownership in 1971 and after protracted litigation had a second alias writ of possession implemented on November 14, 1990.[^165] In November 1992, respondent Simeon Policarpio Shipyard and Shipbuilding Corporation filed a complaint for damages, injunction and mandamus before the Regional Trial Court of Malabon, Metro Manila, Branch 170 against Philippine Trust Company and the implementing sheriffs, alleging improper implementation of the alias writ of possession over two parcels covered by Transfer Certificate of Title No. 234088 and Original Certificate of Title No. R-165.[^166] The trial court denied the bank's motion to dismiss as to Original Certificate of Title No. R-165 and on July 30, 1995 the Court of Appeals in C.A. G.R. SP No. 39342 dismissed the bank's petition for certiorari, holding that *res judicata* barred the claim as to Transfer Certificate of Title No. 24182 (which had been consolidated into Transfer Certificate of Title No. 234088) but did not apply to Original Certificate of Title No. R-165, which was never mortgaged to Philippine Trust Company.[^167]

Before the Supreme Court, Philippine Trust Company argued that the respondent corporation was no longer the owner and had no cause of action because Original Certificate of Title No. R-165 had been mortgaged to the Land Bank of the Philippines for P4,529,000.00, foreclosed on April 27, 1987 and not redeemed when the one-year redemption period expired on September 21, 1988.[^168] Applying Article 539(1) of the Civil Code and Section 2, Rule 3 of the 1997 Rules of Civil Procedure, the Supreme Court held that every possessor includes all kinds of possession from the owner to a mere holder (except that which constitutes a crime) and because the Land Bank of the Philippines had deferred consolidation of title and given the respondent an option to reacquire the submerged property, the respondent was in lawful possession when disturbed by the sheriff and could sue for damages.[^169] The Supreme Court affirmed *in toto* the decision of the Court of Appeals dismissing the petition for certiorari.[^170]

### [*BA Finance Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33853): Protection of Third-Party Chattel Possessor in Replevin

In [*BA Finance Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33853), Spouses Reynaldo and Florencia Manahan executed a promissory note for P83,080.00 on May 15, 1980 payable in thirty-six monthly installments and secured it with a chattel mortgage over a Ford Cortina 1.6 GL motor vehicle in favor of Carmasters, Inc., which assigned the note and mortgage to petitioner BA Finance Corporation with the conformity of the Manahans.[^171] Alleging default, the petitioner filed a complaint for replevin with damages on October 2, 1987 (Civil Case No. 87-42270) in the Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch XX against the Manahan spouses and a John Doe. Upon the filing of a P169,161.00 bond, the trial court issued a writ of replevin but warned that the case would be dismissed for failure to prosecute if summons were not served on the defendants within thirty days.[^172] The vehicle was seized in Sorsogon from respondent Roberto M. Reyes, the John Doe in the complaint, who then alleged possession in good faith.[^173] On February 18, 1988, the trial court dismissed the case for failure to prosecute because the principal defendants had not been served with summons after four months and ordered the return of the vehicle to Reyes. On April 20, 1988, it granted the petitioner's motion for reconsideration, set aside the dismissal and directed the petitioner to serve summons on the principal defendants within five days.[^174] After Reyes was declared in default and the petitioner presented its evidence *ex parte*, the trial court rendered a Decision on February 27, 1989 dismissing the case against the Manahan spouses without prejudice for failure to prosecute, dismissing the case against Reyes for failure to show his liability and directing the petitioner to return the seized vehicle with all its accessories to Reyes.[^175] The Court of Appeals affirmed in CA-G.R. CV No. 23605. It rejected the petitioner's theory that replevin to foreclose a chattel is an action *quasi in rem* that does not need the principal obligors and quoted Articles 539, 527 and 559 of the Civil Code.[^176]

On appeal by certiorari, the Supreme Court recognized that a chattel mortgagee may ordinarily maintain replevin against any person in whose hands the chattel is found when its right to possess is not disputed.[^177] Where that right is put in great doubt or an adverse and independent claim of possession is raised, however, other persons may have to be impleaded. Following *Servicewide Specialists, Inc. v. Court of Appeals*, as cited in [*BA Finance*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33853), the mortgagor is an indispensable party when the mortgagee seeks replevin to foreclose the mortgage.[^178] The Supreme Court approved the Court of Appeals' ruling and held that because a chattel mortgagee's right to possess the mortgaged chattel depends on the actual fact of the mortgagor's default, the mortgagor is an indispensable party when the mortgagee seeks replevin to foreclose the mortgage against an adverse third-party possessor, who cannot be deprived of his possession without establishing the mortgage and default in a proceeding where the mortgagor is brought under the court's jurisdiction.[^179] The Supreme Court affirmed the decision of the Court of Appeals without costs.[^180]

### [*Painaga v. Cortes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_53837_1991.html): Judicial Jurisdiction Over Possessory Injunction Pending Free Patent Protest

In [*Painaga v. Cortes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_53837_1991.html), petitioner Felix Painaga purchased a 60,000-square-meter parcel of corn and pasture land in Sitio Igbolo, Barangay Cabiawan, San Remigio, Antique from Bonifacio Merendad on December 29, 1962 and remained in possession thereof.[^181] On April 21, 1977, respondent Venancio V. Mostacho claimed that the northern portion of the land was included in his Original Certificate of Title No. N-1889 (Free Patent No. 319059) and caused a technical survey, prompting the petitioner to file a sworn protest with the Bureau of Lands in San Jose, Antique to annul the title on the ground of fraud.[^182] On September 20, 1978, alleging that the respondent and his agents had repeatedly attempted to oust him by force, violence and intimidation, the petitioner filed an action for injunction with preliminary prohibitory injunction and damages (Civil Case No. 1539) before the Court of First Instance of Antique, Branch I (also referred to in the body as Capiz).[^183] After denying the preliminary prohibitory injunction on December 17, 1978, respondent Judge Noli Ma. Cortes issued an Order on January 22, 1979 dismissing Civil Case No. 1539 on the ground of prematurity for failure to exhaust administrative remedies before the Bureau of Lands.[^184]

On a petition for review on certiorari, the Supreme Court held under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code that Civil Case No. 1539 was an original action to protect and preserve the petitioner's prior possession rather than to annul the free patent and that the Bureau of Lands' authority over public land disposition and the pending administrative protest regarding ownership does not divest regular courts of jurisdiction to protect actual physical possessors and quell social unrest.[^185] The Supreme Court granted the petition, annulled and set aside the January 22, 1979 Order, reinstated Civil Case No. 1539 of the former Court of First Instance of Antique and directed the successor Regional Trial Court to proceed with the disposition of the case with deliberate dispatch.[^186]

### [*Bacar v. Del Rosario*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/mar1989/gr_51208_1989.html): Tenant's Suit to Protect Possession Without Joining Landlords

In [*Bacar v. Del Rosario*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/mar1989/gr_51208_1989.html), petitioner Godofredo Bacar filed a complaint for injunction and damages with preliminary prohibitory injunction on May 11, 1978 (Civil Case No. 12415) before the Court of First Instance of Iloilo, 11th Judicial District, Branch IV against respondents Valeriano Bacabac and Fidela Bacabac, alleging that he was the leasehold tenant in actual possession of a two-hectare palay landholding in Barrio Lanjagan, Ajuy, Iloilo owned by Guadalupe Bacabac Batapa and Faustino Bacabac and that the respondents had hired a person to set fire to his compost pile of palay straw on April 26, 1978 (causing P 500.00 damage) and caused a hired tractor to plow the landholding against his will on April 28,1978.[^187] Because the respondents alleged in their answer that they owned the land, respondent Judge Amelia Del Rosario issued an Order on May 3, 1979 (also printed as May 3, 1978 in the narrative) directing the petitioner to amend his complaint to join his landlords Guadalupe Bacabac Batapa and Faustino Bacabac as indispensable parties under Section 7, Rule 3 of the Revised Rules of Court and denied reconsideration on June 18, 1979.[^188]

On certiorari, the Supreme Court held under Articles 525, 536 and 539(1) of the Civil Code that ownership is separate from possession, that the petitioner as a tenant in actual possession had the right to be respected and protected in his possession against third-party intruders without impleading his landlords (who were not indispensable parties to the injunction suit) and that if the respondents claimed ownership they could not take the law into their own hands by force or intimidation but had to file a separate action to resolve ownership.[^189] The Supreme Court granted the petition, set aside the Orders dated May 3, 1979 and June 18, 1979 and remanded the case to the court of origin for further proceedings, with costs against the private respondents.[^190]

### [*Buayan Cattle Co., Inc. v. Quintillan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/mar1984/gr_l-26970_1984.html): Invalidity of Preliminary Injunction Protecting a Usurper

In [*Buayan Cattle Co., Inc. v. Quintillan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/mar1984/gr_l-26970_1984.html), petitioner Buayan Cattle Co., Inc. held Pasture Lease Agreement No. 8 (originally issued on April 18, 1952 to its predecessor-in-interest and approved on November 20, 1958) over 1,000 hectares of fenced pasture land in Southern Cotabato.[^191] Respondent Adan de las Marias obtained Pasture Lease Agreement No. 2510 on September 11, 1964 (which the Secretary of Agriculture and Natural Resources suspended on October 20, 1965 for *prima facie* forgery in the inspection report), had a forest guard conduct a relocation survey overlapping 580 hectares of the petitioner's ranch, unilaterally removed the petitioner's fence and erected his own fence 580 hectares inside the petitioner's pasture area.[^192] After the petitioner complained to the Bureau of Forestry and the Director of Forestry ordered the respondent to stop fencing and refrain from disturbance pending a boundary investigation, the respondent filed Civil Case No. 631 for injunction on September 25, 1965 before the Court of First Instance of Cotabato, 16th Judicial District, Second Branch, General Santos, where respondent Judge Jesus Quintillan issued a writ of preliminary injunction on September 29, 1965 restraining the petitioner, forestry officials and the military commander from driving cattle into or enforcing forestry orders over the 580-hectare area and subsequently denied the petitioner's motions to dissolve the writ and to dismiss the complaint.[^193]

In a petition for prohibition, certiorari and mandamus, the Supreme Court held under Articles 429, 536 and 539(1) of the Civil Code that the petitioner as prior peaceful lessee was entitled to be respected in its possession and to repel unlawful invasion, whereas the respondent was a usurper who took the law into his own hands and had no cause of action for injunction because his lease right was doubtful and he failed to exhaust pending administrative remedies before the Bureau of Forestry under the Revised Administrative Code and Forestry Administrative Order No. 6-2.[^194] Holding that the preliminary injunction perversely restrained the vigilant lawful possessor rather than the wrongdoer and that mandamus lies to compel the ministerial dismissal of a complaint decidedly devoid of a cause of action, the Supreme Court granted the petition, set aside as null and void the order for the issuance of the writ of injunction and the order denying the motion to dismiss in Civil Case No. 631 and remanded the case to the lower court only for the purpose of hearing the petitioner on its claim for damages against private respondent Adan de las Marias, with costs against the private respondent.[^195]

### [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html): Return of Movables Seized by Search Warrant from Good-Faith Purchasers

In [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html), Carlito Refuerzo purchased scrap cast iron engine blocks on June 10, 1978 from Marcelo Steel Corporation using a check for P61,808.25 that was dishonored for insufficient funds and on June 12, 1978 sold 50,000 kilos of the engine blocks for P44,000.00 to petitioners Spouses Alfonso Yu and Soledad Yu of Soledad Junk Shop in Caloocan, Metro Manila under a sales invoice in the ordinary course of business.[^196] Upon the application of Makati Police Detective Carlos C. Nuestro, respondent Judge Reynaldo P. Honrado of the Court of First Instance of Rizal, Branch XXV-Pasig issued a search warrant on June 27, 1978 under which police seized 42.8 metric tons of engine blocks from Soledad Junk Shop on June 28, 1978 and brought them to Marcelo Steel Corporation's premises for safekeeping.[^197] On November 9, 1978, Judge Honrado denied the Yu spouses' motion to set aside the search warrant and return the engine blocks, while the fiscal absolved Soledad Yu as an innocent purchaser for value and filed an *estafa* information (Criminal Case No. 32394) against Refuerzo and his confederates, which was temporarily archived because the accused had not been arrested.[^198]

In a certiorari, prohibition and mandamus action, the Supreme Court held that although the search warrant was lawfully issued upon probable cause, under *Chua Hai v. Kapunan, Jr.*, as cited in [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html) and Articles 539(1) and 559 of the Civil Code, a possessor of movable property acquired in good faith is entitled to be respected and protected in his possession as if he were the true owner until a competent court rules otherwise.[^199] Because there was no adjudication of civil liability in the criminal case under Article 105 of the Revised Penal Code and Marcelo Steel Corporation had voluntarily delivered the goods to the alleged swindler (a situation the Supreme Court distinguished from theft or robbery), the Supreme Court ordered respondent Marcelo Steel Corporation to return and deliver the 42.8 tons of scrap engine blocks to the Yu spouses within ten days from notice of entry of judgment, without costs.[^200]

### [*Dayao v. Shell Company of the Philippines, Ltd.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/apr1980/gr_32475_1980.html): Preliminary Mandatory Injunction on Appeal in Unlawful Detainer

In [*Dayao v. Shell Company of the Philippines, Ltd.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/apr1980/gr_32475_1980.html), respondent Shell Company of the Philippines, Ltd. filed an unlawful detainer complaint on October 10, 1966 before the City Court of Quezon City, Branch IV to eject sublessee-dealer petitioner Jesus Dayao from a gasoline service station at the corner of Aurora Boulevard Extension and Illinois Street, Quezon City.[^201] After trial where evidence of contract violations was presented, the City Court on November 7, 1967 dismissed the complaint and ordered Shell to pay Dayao P300.00 attorney's fees.[^202] On appeal to the Court of First Instance of Rizal, Branch IV, Quezon City (CC No. Q-11921), respondent Judge Walfrido de los Angeles issued an Order on March 30, 1968 admitting Shell's Amended Complaint (conforming to the evidence of contract violations presented at trial) and an Order on April 1, 1968 granting a Writ of Preliminary Mandatory Injunction upon a P5,000.00 bond turning over possession of the station to Shell and denied reconsideration on December 18, 1969.[^203]

In a special civil action for certiorari and prohibition, Dayao argued under Article 539(2) of the Civil Code and Section 3, Rule 70 of the Rules of Court that a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction may issue only in forcible entry cases.[^204] The Supreme Court held that while Article 539(2) refers to the issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction in forcible entry cases in the original court, Article 1674 of the Civil Code and Section 9, Rule 70 of the Rules of Court apply the same remedy in unlawful detainer or ejectment cases in the appellate court upon motion within ten days from perfection of the appeal when the appellate court is satisfied that the lessor's appeal is *prima facie* meritorious and that the admission of the amended complaint to conform to evidence tried by consent was proper under Section 5, Rule 10 of the Rules of Court and Article 1673 of the Civil Code.[^205] The Supreme Court denied the writ of certiorari and remanded the case to the respondent Court of First Instance for determination of the appeal on the merits and rendition of judgment.[^206]

### [*Aguilon v. Bohol*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_27169_1977.html): *Accion Publiciana* by a Homestead Applicant Pending Administrative Protest

In [*Aguilon v. Bohol*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_27169_1977.html), plaintiff-appellant Purita S. Aguilon, represented by her attorney-in-fact Ignacio S. Aguilon, sued defendant Montano Bohol on October 21, 1964 in the Court of First Instance of Davao (Civil Case No. 4464) to recover possession of a portion of Lot No. 3046 in Barrio Kinabilangan, Baganga, Davao Oriental with an area of more than twenty-one hectares.[^207] She alleged that through her father she had cleared, cultivated and improved the land immediately after the war, declared it for realty tax purposes and paid the taxes up to 1964 and filed Homestead Application No. 20-1846 for it.[^208] She further alleged that in 1957 Bohol, through his son-in-law Crisanto Garay, usurped a portion of the land, that her forcible entry case against Garay was dismissed by the justice of the peace court on February 18, 1958 because her remedy was with the Director of Lands and that Bohol refused the district land officer's directive to vacate and instead filed a formal protest against her application on July 23, 1958.[^209] Bohol answered that he had occupied about six hectares of the lot since 1951. He pleaded prescription because the action was not brought within ten years from 1951 and lack of jurisdiction because the land was public land under the control of the Director of Lands.[^210] In 1966, long after the pre-trial and upon motion of Bohol, the Court of First Instance of Davao issued a minute order dismissing Civil Case No. 4464 for lack of jurisdiction.[^211]

On appeal, the Supreme Court characterized the suit as an *accion publiciana*, the plenary action in an ordinary civil proceeding to determine the better right of possession independently of title. It held that Courts of First Instance have jurisdiction over all actions involving possession of land except ejectment cases filed within one year from the unlawful deprivation of possession.[^212] Applying Article 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that even pending the resolution of a homestead application and protest before the Director of Lands, a *bona fide* applicant and occupant acquires by priority of application and entry a possessory right over the public land applied for as against any other public land applicant. That right may be protected by forcible entry or by *accion publiciana*.[^213] The authority of the Director of Lands over the disposition of public lands does not exclude the courts from their jurisdiction over possessory actions. The Supreme Court found the case similar to *Bohayang v. Maceren*, as cited in [*Aguilon*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_27169_1977.html), which the trial court arbitrarily ignored and which treated an action to recover possession as an urgent matter to be decided promptly to forestall breaches of peace.[^214] The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the trial court's order of dismissal and directed the trial court to set the case for trial on the merits, with costs against the defendant-appellee.[^215]

### [*De Laureano v. Adil*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1976/jul1976/gr_43345_1976.html): Mandatory Injunction and Supersedeas Bond in an Unlawful Detainer Appeal

In [*De Laureano v. Adil*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1976/jul1976/gr_43345_1976.html), petitioner Josefina S. de Laureano, registered owner of Lots 996 and 1004-B (3,107 square meters) at Iznart and Solis Streets, Iloilo City, filed an unlawful detainer suit (Civil Case No. 10370) in October 1974 before the City Court of Iloilo City against lessee Ong Cu after their fifteen-year lease expired on August 31, 1974.[^216] On September 23, 1975, the City Court rendered judgment ordering Ong Cu to vacate the lots, restore possession to Laureano, remove his buildings and improvements and pay P12,428.00 monthly compensation from September 1, 1974 until he vacates with 12% per annum interest plus P10,000.00 as moral and exemplary damages and attorney's fees, but on October 8, 1975 granted Ong Cu's *ex parte* motion approving a P22,000.00 supersedeas bond and fixing the monthly rental deposit at P1,200.00.[^217] On appeal, respondent Judge Midpantao L. Adil of the Court of First Instance of Iloilo, Branch II issued Orders on December 9, 1975 and February 12, 1976 upholding the reduced bond and deposit, treating Ong Cu as a possessor in good faith with a right of retention and denying Laureano's timely motion (filed within nine days of notice of appellate docketing) for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction under Article 1674 of the Civil Code and Section 9, Rule 70 of the Rules of Court.[^218]

In a special civil action of certiorari, the Supreme Court held that: (1) under Section 8, Rule 70, the supersedeas bond and monthly deposits must match the P12,428.00 monthly rate fixed in the City Court judgment, though Ong Cu should be given thirty days to file a new P161,564.00 supersedeas bond and deposit the deficiency because his mistake was induced by the City Court's erroneous October 8, 1975 order, (2) under Article 1674 in relation to Article 539(2) of the Civil Code, a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction restores physical possession to the lessor on appeal when the lessee's appeal is manifestly frivolous and dilatory, while the supersedeas bond and monthly deposits subsist to stay execution as to the accrued and ongoing rentals and (3) an overstaying lessee is a possessor in bad faith under Article 1671 governed by Article 1678 (not Articles 448 and 546) with no right of retention.[^219] The Supreme Court set aside the Orders dated December 9, 1975 and February 12, 1976 and directed the lower court: (1) to require Ong Cu within thirty days from notice (a) to file a new supersedeas bond of P161,564.00 for the period from September 1, 1974 to September 1975 and (b) to deposit compensation from October 1, 1975 to June 1976 at P12,428.00 a month less the P1,200.00 monthly amounts already deposited, (2) if after June 1976 he had not restored the portions not occupied by his buildings, to require him to deposit P12,428.00 on or before the tenth day of each month until compliance, (3) to issue a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction requiring Ong Cu to vacate the two lots, deliver possession to Laureano and remove his buildings and improvements after the court passes upon a petition for demolition under Section 14, Rule 39 and (4) after complying with the mandatory injunction as to unoccupied portions, to require him to deposit the value of the use and occupation of the portions occupied by his buildings at four pesos a square meter, with costs against Ong Cu.[^220]

### [*Mara, Inc. v. Estrella*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html): Mandatory Injunction Against an Interloper and Invalid Dissolution by Check

In [*Mara, Inc. v. Estrella*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html), petitioner Mara, Inc., registered owner of Lots 7, 9, 11 and 13 (four thousand square meters covered by Transfer Certificates of Title Nos. 127719, 127720, 127721 and 127722 derived from Original Certificate of Title No. 735) in Barrio Santol, Quezon City, filed a forcible entry complaint on May 4, 1973 (Civil Case No. V-23366) in the City Court of Quezon City, Branch V against Jose de Leon, who allegedly occupied the lots on April 5, 1973 and moved four days later on May 8, 1973 for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction.[^221] Respondent Judge Justiniano C. Estrella deferred resolution of the motion for over nineteen months until November 20, 1974, when (after the Supreme Court upheld the validity of Original Certificate of Title No. 735 in *Benin v. Tuason*, as cited in [*Mara, Inc. v. Estrella*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html)) he granted the writ upon a P10,000.00 bond and issued the writ on February 10, 1975.[^222] On April 1, 1975, however, Judge Estrella granted De Leon's motion to dissolve the injunction upon a P10,000.00 surety counterbond, for which De Leon merely submitted a check without serving a copy on Mara, Inc.[^223]

In a special civil action of certiorari, the Supreme Court held that Article 539(2) of the Civil Code (incorporated in the second paragraph of Section 3, Rule 70 of the Rules of Court) is an exception to the general rule against using an injunction to transfer possession, that the thirty-day decision period underscores the urgency of restoring a registered Torrens titleholder against an interloper or squatter who has shown no indubitable right to possess and that the City Court gravely abused its discretion in dissolving the injunction on the basis of an unserved check in violation of Section 8, Rule 58 of the Rules of Court.[^224] The Supreme Court granted the petition for certiorari, set aside the City Court's Order of April 1, 1975 and affirmed its Order of November 20, 1974 granting the writ of preliminary mandatory injunction, with costs against respondent De Leon.[^225]

### [*Emilia v. Bado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html): Inappropriateness of Injunction Where Title Is Disputed Between Torrens Owners

In [*Emilia v. Bado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html), plaintiff-appellant Cirila Emilia filed a complaint on December 12, 1963 for preliminary and final injunction and damages against Epifanio Bado, Glicerio Bado and others. She alleged that on or about December 1, 1962 the defendants entered her Lot 1131 in Iligan City bordering Salabao Creek (covered by her Torrens Title O-267) and began constructing a house of light materials on a 48-square-meter area on her northern boundary.[^226] A preliminary injunction was issued *ex parte*. The defendants moved to dismiss for lack of cause of action and, at a summary hearing called by the trial court, relied on the sketch, affidavit and testimony of surveyor Flordelito Aragon (also a deputy public land surveyor) showing that the house was inside defendant Glicerio Bado's adjoining Lot 2894 covered by his Torrens Title O-275.[^227] On February 27, 1964, the trial court sustained the motion to dismiss. It reasoned that stopping the construction would deprive Glicerio Bado of his lawful dominical rights and that his title subsists until declared void by a competent court even if, as the plaintiff claimed, it was obtained through fraud. It then dissolved the preliminary injunction and dismissed the complaint.[^228]

On appeal, the Supreme Court held that unless the plaintiff succeeds in annulling the decree of registration in the defendant's favor, which she had sought in the cadastral proceedings, that title subsists.[^229] Where legal title is disputed and the possessor asserts ownership, no injunction can issue to dispossess him because the parties must be kept in their *status quo* until ownership is determined by evidence. The recognized exceptions, such as where the defendant is clearly a mere intruder, are generally applied upon hearing and not upon an *ex parte* application.[^230] Nor does a principal action for injunction lie where the plaintiff has an adequate remedy under the three actions to recover real property: (a) forcible entry (where a preliminary mandatory injunction may be sought within ten days from filing the complaint under Article 539(2) of the Civil Code) or illegal detainer within one year in the municipal court, (b) *accion publiciana* in the Court of First Instance and (c) *acción de reivindicación* in the Court of First Instance.[^231] Because both parties claimed the 48-square-meter portion under Torrens titles, a legitimate issue of ownership emerged and the plaintiff should have brought an *acción de reivindicación*.[^232] The Supreme Court declined to return the record for amendment of the complaint because the many pleadings could confuse the issues and a final decision in the cadastral proceeding on the validity of Glicerio Bado's title could prevent further controversy. It affirmed the February 27, 1964 Order dismissing the complaint, with costs against the plaintiff-appellant.[^233]

## Key Cases

- [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602)[^247] (G.R. No. 238468, July 6, 2022): After buying a 364-square-meter portion of an unpartitioned 5,599-square-meter inherited parcel in Misamis Oriental (OCT No. P-10402), the buyer ordered the co-heirs' laborers to desist, built a fence and demolished two houses. The Municipal Circuit Trial Court, Regional Trial Court and Court of Appeals ruled for the co-heirs in forcible entry. Applying Articles 487, 536 and 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that a co-owner who wrests exclusive possession of a specific portion by force from co-owners in prior physical possession may be evicted by forcible entry (though a co-owner who enters without force while recognizing the co-ownership cannot be evicted) and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision with modification by ordering the buyer to vacate and remove her improvements while deleting the P300.00 monthly rent and P5,000.00 attorney's fees awards.
- [*Gabriel, Jr. v. Crisologo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57063)[^248] (G.R. No. 204626, June 9, 2014): A registered owner of two Baguio City lots under TCT Nos. T-13935 and T-13936 who bought the land in 1967 and paid realty taxes since 1969 filed an *accion publiciana* against occupants who entered in 2006. The Municipal Trial Court in Cities ruled for the owner, the Regional Trial Court reversed under Presidential Decree No. 1271 and the Court of Appeals reinstated the Municipal Trial Court in Cities decision. The Supreme Court held under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code and Section 48 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 that the registered owner had a preferred claim of possession and her Torrens titles were immune from collateral attack in an *accion publiciana* (where any ownership ruling is provisional), denying the petition and affirming the orders to vacate, demolish structures and pay Php4,000.00 monthly rental per defendant from January 2006, Php20, 000.00 attorney's fees and costs.
- [*Spouses Castro v. Spouses Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35087)[^249] (G.R. No. 190122, January 10, 2011): Third-party buyers bought and took actual possession of an unregistered 417-square-meter lot in Hagonoy, Bulacan in 1997 before the mortgagees extrajudicially foreclosed the mortgage in 1999. After the mortgagees evicted them under an *ex parte* writ of possession from Branch 16 of the Malolos Regional Trial Court, Branch 7 granted a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction restoring the buyers, which the Court of Appeals upheld. The Supreme Court held under Articles 539(1) and 433 of the Civil Code that enforcing an *ex parte* writ of possession against non-party buyers in prior actual possession under claim of ownership disturbed the *status quo ante litem* without due judicial process and denied the petition without closing the principal action on the merits.
- [*Vivares v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44835)[^250] (G.R. No. 155408, February 13, 2008): In an estate partition suit, the Camiguin Regional Trial Court, Branch 28 appointed a receiver over disputed properties in the respondent co-heir's possession and refused to cancel a notice of *lis pendens* on Tax Declaration No. 112. The Court of Appeals discharged the receiver upon a P100,000.00 counterbond and cancelled the *lis pendens* as to a portion allegedly owned by a third person. Applying Article 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that an actual possessor ought not to be deprived of possession in litigation except in exceptional and extreme cases, found the alleged fraud unproven and partly granted the petition by affirming the discharge of receiver Lope Salantin upon a PhP 100,000 counterbond while modifying the judgment to keep the notice of *lis pendens* on Tax Declaration No. 112 valid and remanding the case for trial.
- [*Metro Manila Transit Corporation v. D.M. Consortium, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41302)[^251] (G.R. No. 147594, March 7, 2007): A lessor-owner extrajudicially repossessed 228 buses and occupied the lessee-purchaser's offices despite the absence of a three-month default and after the lessee had paid P106 million on a P71 million obligation. The Regional Trial Court of Quezon City, Branch 93 ordered the return of the buses plus damages and the Court of Appeals modified the judgment after eleven years to order payment of the December 1989 value of the buses and P2,000,000.00 for furniture, fixtures and equipment. The Supreme Court held under Article 539(1) and Article 1234 of the Civil Code that an owner cannot exercise *jus possidendi* against a lessee vested with contractual possession and that a possessor may set up his right even against the owner, affirming the Court of Appeals and remanding to the trial court to determine the value of the 228 buses.
- [*Semirara Coal Corporation v. HGL Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40420)[^252] (G.R. No. 166854, December 6, 2006): A grazing lessee under 25-year FLGLA No. 184 over 367 hectares in Semirara Island permitted a coal mining company to pass trucks through the land, but the mining company built structures and conducted blasting. The Regional Trial Court of Culasi, Antique, Branch 13 issued a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction in the lessee's recovery of possession suit and the Court of Appeals affirmed. The Supreme Court held that Article 539 of the Civil Code allows a plaintiff to avail of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore possession *pendente lite* in an action to recover possession (even if alleged to be an *accion publiciana*) upon showing a clear right, material invasion and urgent necessity and denied the petition while lifting its temporary restraining order.
- [*Busilac Builders, Inc. v. Aguilar*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41960)[^253] (A.M. No. RTJ-03-1809, October 17, 2006): A Regional Trial Court judge who had bought a 100-square-meter portion of a 426-square-meter lot in Laoag City failed to inhibit himself from the prior possessor's specific performance suit, dismissed it without prejudice, removed the prior possessor's concrete posts and barbed wires and spearheaded the leveling of the lot with a payloader. Applying Articles 433 and 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that the judge took the law into his own hands instead of resorting to judicial remedies, fining him P11,000.00 for impropriety in leveling the lot, suspending him for three months without pay for failing to inhibit, reprimanding him for appearing twice as his own counsel without prior permission and dismissing the search warrant charge.
- [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863)[^254] (G.R. Nos. 157391, 160749 & 160816, July 15, 2005): A lessor unilaterally rescinded a four-year lease Memorandum of Agreement over seminary advertising spaces, filed an unlawful detainer suit in the Metropolitan Trial Court of Makati and dismantled the lessee's billboards while the suit was pending. The Metropolitan Trial Court declared the lessee's possession lawful and awarded P100,000.00 damages, the Regional Trial Court ordered restoration of possession for 21 months and a P796,421.44 refund (while denying immediate execution) and the Court of Appeals denied restoration because the four-year term had lapsed. Applying Articles 1654(3) and 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that a lessee forcibly evicted by the lessor is entitled to be restored for the unused portion of the lease term (except areas already leased to a non-party), affirmed the denial of immediate execution in favor of a defendant under Section 19, Rule 70 and remanded to the Metropolitan Trial Court to compute the exact rental refund, if any.
- [*Domagas v. Jensen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43231)[^255] (G.R. No. 158407, January 17, 2005): In a forcible entry suit before the Municipal Trial Court of Calasiao, Pangasinan over a 68-square-meter encroachment, summons was left with the defendant's brother who was merely visiting the defendant's leased house to collect rent while the defendant was in Norway. The Regional Trial Court of Dagupan City, Branch 44 and the Court of Appeals annulled the resulting ejectment judgment. The Supreme Court held that forcible entry or unlawful detainer is a real action and an action *in personam* (not *quasi in rem*) because it enforces a personal liability under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code to vacate, restore possession and pay reasonable compensation and denied the petition because invalid substituted service failed to confer jurisdiction over the defendant's person.
- [*Montañez v. Mendoza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51691)[^256] (G.R. No. 144116, November 22, 2002): An occupant who had cultivated a six-hectare parcel in San Mateo, Rizal since 1970 won a forcible entry judgment in the Municipal Trial Court of San Mateo, Rizal (affirmed by the Regional Trial Court, Branch 77) against an intruder claiming under a title (OCT No. P-658), but the Court of Appeals reversed for alleged failure to prove the identity of the land. The Supreme Court held under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code that because the plaintiff proved by a preponderance of evidence his prior physical possession and the distinct identity of the land from OCT No. P-658, he was entitled to restoration of possession, granting the petition and reinstating the Municipal Trial Court decision ordering vacatur, removal of improvements, P5,000.00 attorney's fees and costs.
- [*Philippine Trust Company v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36989)[^257] (G.R. No. 124658, December 15, 1999): A shipyard corporation sued a bank and sheriffs in the Regional Trial Court of Malabon, Branch 170 for damages after an alias writ of possession was implemented against its lot covered by OCT-R-165, which was never mortgaged to the bank. The trial court and Court of Appeals denied dismissal as to OCT-R-165 (while applying *res judicata* to a separate foreclosed lot under TCT 24182). The Supreme Court held under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code that every possessor covers all kinds of possession except a crime and because the separate mortgagee (Land Bank of the Philippines) had deferred consolidation of title after expiration of the redemption period and granted an option to reacquire, the corporation was a lawful possessor and real party in interest, affirming the Court of Appeals *in toto*.
- [*BA Finance Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33853)[^258] (G.R. No. 102998, July 5, 1996): A chattel mortgagee seized a Ford Cortina motor vehicle under a writ of replevin from a third-party possessor in Sorsogon but failed to serve summons on the principal debtor-mortgagor spouses. The Regional Trial Court of Manila, Branch XX and the Court of Appeals dismissed the complaint and ordered the return of the vehicle to the third-party possessor. Approving the Court of Appeals' application of Articles 539(1), 527 and 559 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that an adverse third-party possessor cannot be deprived of possession in a replevin suit for foreclosure without impleading and serving the indispensable mortgagor to prove default and affirmed the Court of Appeals decision.
- [*Painaga v. Cortes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_53837_1991.html)[^259] (G.R. No. 53837, October 3, 1991): A possessor of 60,000 square meters of land in San Remigio, Antique since 1962 filed an administrative protest with the Bureau of Lands to annul a rival claimant's free patent (OCT No. N-1889) and then filed Civil Case No. 1539 in the Court of First Instance of Antique, Branch I for injunction against the patentee's forcible attempts to oust him. The trial court dismissed the suit for non-exhaustion of administrative remedies. Applying Article 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that regular courts retain jurisdiction to protect prior physical possession while the Bureau of Lands determines ownership in the patent protest, setting aside the dismissal and reinstating Civil Case No. 1539.
- [*Bacar v. Del Rosario*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1989/mar1989/gr_51208_1989.html)[^260] (G.R. No. 51208, March 29, 1989): A leasehold tenant of a two-hectare palay landholding in Ajuy, Iloilo sued third parties in the Court of First Instance of Iloilo, Branch IV for injunction and damages after they burned his palay straw and plowed his land and the trial judge ordered him to join his landlords as indispensable parties because the defendants claimed ownership. Applying Articles 525, 536 and 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that a tenant in actual possession is entitled to be protected in his possession without joining his landlords and that ownership claims must be litigated in a separate action, setting aside the trial court's orders and remanding the case.
- [*Buayan Cattle Co., Inc. v. Quintillan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/mar1984/gr_l-26970_1984.html)[^261] (G.R. No. L-26970, March 19, 1984): A subsequent pasture lessee removed the prior pasture lessee's fences over 580 hectares in Southern Cotabato and, after being ordered by the Director of Forestry to stop fencing, obtained a writ of preliminary injunction from the Court of First Instance of Cotabato, Second Branch, General Santos restraining the prior lessee and forestry and military officials. Applying Articles 429, 536 and 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that the subsequent lessee was a usurper who failed to exhaust forestry administrative remedies and had no cause of action, setting aside the injunction and denial of dismissal as null and void and remanding the case solely to hear the prior lessee's claim for damages.
- [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html)[^262] (G.R. No. 50025, August 21, 1980): Junk shop proprietors bought 50,000 kilos of scrap cast iron engine blocks in good faith for P44,000.00 in the ordinary course of business from a buyer who had paid Marcelo Steel Corporation with a dishonored check and police seized 42.8 metric tons from the junk shop under a search warrant issued by the Court of First Instance of Rizal, Branch XXV-Pasig and delivered them to Marcelo Steel Corporation. Applying Articles 539(1) and 559 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that although the search warrant was valid, a good-faith purchaser of movables voluntarily delivered by the original seller cannot be deprived of possession absent a final criminal judgment of restitution under Article 105 of the Revised Penal Code and ordered Marcelo Steel Corporation to return the 42.8 tons of scrap engine blocks within ten days.
- [*Dayao v. Shell Company of the Philippines, Ltd.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/apr1980/gr_32475_1980.html)[^263] (G.R. No. L-32475, April 30, 1980): After the City Court of Quezon City dismissed a sublessor's unlawful detainer complaint against a gasoline station dealer, the Court of First Instance of Rizal, Branch IV, Quezon City on appeal admitted an amended complaint conforming to evidence tried at the City Court and issued a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction upon a P5,000.00 bond restoring possession to the sublessor. The Supreme Court held that while Article 539(2) of the Civil Code refers to forcible entry in the original court, Article 1674 and Section 9, Rule 70 apply the preliminary mandatory injunction remedy to unlawful detainer appeals where the lessor's appeal is *prima facie* meritorious, denying certiorari and remanding the appeal for judgment on the merits.
- [*Aguilon v. Bohol*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_27169_1977.html)[^264] (G.R. No. L-27169, October 20, 1977): A homestead applicant who alleged that she had cleared and cultivated over twenty-one hectares in Baganga, Davao Oriental since immediately after the war filed an *accion publiciana* in the Court of First Instance of Davao against an alleged 1957 intruder who had protested her homestead application and the trial court dismissed the suit for lack of jurisdiction. Applying Article 539(1) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that a *bona fide* homestead applicant acquires a prior possessory right enforceable in regular courts by *accion publiciana* even while an administrative protest is pending before the Director of Lands, reversing the dismissal and directing trial on the merits.
- [*De Laureano v. Adil*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1976/jul1976/gr_43345_1976.html)[^265] (G.R. No. L-43345, July 29, 1976): After winning an unlawful detainer judgment in the City Court of Iloilo City ordering an overstaying fifteen-year lessee to vacate two lots and pay P12,428.00 monthly compensation, the lessor moved on appeal in the Court of First Instance of Iloilo, Branch II for execution and a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction, which the judge denied after upholding an inadequate P22,000.00 supersedeas bond and P1,200.00 monthly deposit. Applying Article 1674 in relation to Article 539(2) and Articles 1671 and 1678 of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court set aside the trial court's orders, directed the lessee to post a new P161,564.00 supersedeas bond and deposit the P12,428.00 monthly compensation deficiency (which stay monetary execution only) and ordered the issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction restoring possession of unoccupied portions immediately and occupied portions after a demolition hearing.
- [*Mara, Inc. v. Estrella*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html)[^266] (G.R. No. L-40511, July 25, 1975): A registered Torrens owner of four Quezon City lots moved for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction four days after filing a forcible entry complaint. The City Court of Quezon City, Branch V delayed nineteen months before granting the writ upon a P10,000.00 bond and then dissolved the writ when the defendant submitted an unserved check for P10,000.00. The Supreme Court held that Article 539(2) of the Civil Code is an exception to the general rule against transferring possession by injunction and must be decided within thirty days to protect a registered owner against an interloper, setting aside the dissolution order and affirming the order granting the writ of preliminary mandatory injunction.
- [*Emilia v. Bado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html)[^267] (G.R. No. L-23685, April 25, 1968): On December 12, 1963, an Iligan City lot owner sued for preliminary and final injunction to stop the defendants from building a house on a disputed 48-square-meter boundary area that they allegedly entered on or about December 1, 1962. The trial court dismissed the complaint after a surveyor testified that the house stood inside the defendant's titled lot. The Supreme Court held that an independent injunction suit cannot be used to dispossess a possessor claiming under a subsisting Torrens title where ordinary and summary remedies exist (forcible entry with preliminary mandatory injunction under Article 539(2), *accion publiciana* or *acción de reivindicación*) and affirmed the dismissal because the ownership dispute required an *acción de reivindicación*.

## Suggested Answers

### Hypothetical 1: Ejectment Between Co-Owners After Purchase of a Specific Portion

The children of a deceased registered owner took physical possession of an undivided 5,599-square-meter parcel of land covered by a free patent and original certificate of title and built two houses thereon. Without any judicial or extrajudicial partition having been made, a granddaughter of the decedent from another deceased child sold a specific 364-square-meter portion of the lot to a buyer. The day after the sale, the buyer entered the 364-square-meter portion, ordered the heirs' laborers to stop trimming a tree, enclosed the portion with a perimeter fence and a no-trespassing sign and demolished the two houses standing on that section. Within one month, the heirs filed a complaint for forcible entry praying that the buyer be evicted, ordered to remove her fence and required to pay monthly rentals and attorney's fees. The buyer argued that she cannot be ejected because she is now a co-owner of the property. Decide the claims for eviction, removal of improvements and monthly rentals.

**Suggested Answer:**

The buyer must be ordered to vacate the 364-square-meter portion and remove the improvements she introduced thereon, but she cannot be held liable to pay monthly rentals on the common property.[^268]

In [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), the Supreme Court held under Article 493 of the Civil Code that when a co-owner sells a specific portion of an unpartitioned property without the consent of the other co-owners, the sale is valid only to the extent of the vendor's undivided *pro indiviso* share, making the buyer a co-owner who holds the common property in a fiduciary capacity as a trustee for the other co-owners.[^269] Under Article 539(1) of the Civil Code, every possessor has a right to be respected in his or her possession regardless of the character of possession, provided he or she has priority in time and under Article 536 possession cannot be acquired through force or intimidation against an objecting possessor.[^270] Consequently, under Article 487 in relation to Articles 536 and 539(1), while a co-owner who takes possession of a definite portion while recognizing the co-ownership and without employing force cannot be ejected, a co-owner who takes exclusive possession of a specific portion by force, intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth and thereby excludes or deprives other co-owners who were in prior physical possession may be evicted through an action for forcible entry.[^271]

Here, the buyer's acts of ordering the prior-possessing co-heirs' laborers to desist, building a perimeter fence with a no-trespassing sign and demolishing two houses excluded the co-heirs by force.[^272] She must therefore vacate the portion and remove her improvements to restore the co-heirs to their prior peaceful possession.[^273] However, as the Supreme Court ruled in [*Mabalo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), the buyer cannot be ordered to pay monthly rentals on a property that she is entitled to enjoy as a co-owner.[^274]

### Hypothetical 2: Forcible Dispossession of a Lessee and Expiration of the Lease *Pendente Lite*

A religious corporation leased advertising spaces in its compound to an advertising company under a Memorandum of Agreement for a four-year period from August 1, 1993 to July 31, 1997. In October 1995, the lessor unilaterally declared the lease rescinded for alleged violations and filed a complaint for unlawful detainer before the Metropolitan Trial Court in November 1995. On October 5, 1996, while the unlawful detainer suit was still pending trial and after the lessee had occupied the spaces for three years, two months and two days, the lessor entered the premises and dismantled the lessee's billboards. When the case reached the appellate courts after July 31, 1997, the trial courts found that the lessor's unilateral rescission was unlawful, but the lessor contended that the lessee could no longer be restored to possession because the stipulated four-year lease period had already expired on July 31, 1997 during the pendency of the litigation. Is the lessee entitled to be restored to possession of the leased spaces?

**Suggested Answer:**

Yes. The lessee is entitled to be restored to the possession of the leased spaces (except any spaces already leased to a third party who was not impleaded in the action) for the unused and unexpired portion of the four-year lease term counted from its unlawful eviction.[^275]

In [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863), the Supreme Court held under Article 1654(3) and Article 539(1) of the Civil Code that a lessor is obliged to maintain the lessee in the peaceful and adequate enjoyment of the leased premises for the entire duration of the contract and that the lessee has the right to be respected in his possession and restored thereto if disturbed.[^276] Every possessor under Article 539(1) includes a mere holder and possession is protection against the exercise of a right by one's own authority.[^277] When a lessor forcibly dispossesses a lessee by dismantling its billboards without waiting for the final resolution of the ejectment suit, the lessee is entitled to be restored to possession for the unused period of the lease contract counted from eviction and that unexpired portion is not lost by the lapse of the stipulated calendar dates while the ejectment suit filed by the lessor was pending.[^278]

Because the lease period in a reciprocal contract is fixed for the benefit of both parties, restoring the lessee to possession for the remaining unused balance of the four-year term does not extend the lease beyond the contract, but merely enforces the agreed four-year duration and prevents the lessor from profiting from its own unlawful act.[^279]

### Hypothetical 3: Preliminary Mandatory Injunction and Dissolution by Counterbond Check in Forcible Entry

A corporation holding Transfer Certificates of Title over four subdivision lots in Quezon City derived from an Original Certificate of Title whose validity had already been upheld by the Supreme Court filed a forcible entry complaint in the City Court against an occupant who entered the lots one month earlier. Four days after filing the complaint, the corporation filed a motion for the issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction. In his answer, the occupant claimed lack of knowledge of the corporation's titles and invoked a different unvalidated title. The City Judge delayed nineteen months before granting the writ of preliminary mandatory injunction upon a P10,000.00 bond and then two months later dissolved the writ of preliminary mandatory injunction after the occupant offered a counterbond and deposited a P10,000.00 check in court without serving a copy of any bond on the corporation. Did the City Judge act properly in delaying the resolution and subsequently dissolving the writ of preliminary mandatory injunction?

**Suggested Answer:**

No. The City Judge violated the thirty-day decision period mandated by Article 539(2) of the Civil Code and committed grave abuse of discretion in dissolving the writ of preliminary mandatory injunction.[^280]

In [*Mara, Inc. v. Estrella*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html), the Supreme Court held that the second paragraph of Article 539 of the Civil Code provides an urgent exception to the general rule that an injunction cannot be used to transfer possession of property from one party to another.[^281] Under Article 539(2), a possessor deprived of possession through forcible entry may move within ten days from the filing of the complaint for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore him in his possession and the court is required to decide the motion within thirty days from its filing.[^282] Where the plaintiff asking for the preliminary mandatory injunction is the registered owner under unassailable Torrens titles and the oppositor is an interloper or squatter who shows no indubitable right to possess the lots, a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction properly issues *pendente lite* to prevent prolonged deprivation of the owner's immediate right to possession.[^283]

Furthermore, under Section 8, Rule 58 of the Rules of Court, as applied in [*Mara, Inc.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html), a copy of the counterbond for the dissolution of an injunction must be served on the adverse party so that it may object to the sufficiency of the bond.[^284] Dissolving the writ of preliminary mandatory injunction after finding the plaintiff's root title incontestable and allowing the defendant to submit an unserved check instead of a proper surety bond constitutes a capricious and whimsical exercise of discretion that must be set aside on certiorari.[^285]

# Quotes

> "The phrase "every possessor" in the article indicates that all kinds of possession, from that of the owner to that of a mere holder, except that which constitutes a crime, should be respected and protected by the means established and the laws of procedure. Consequently, private respondent having been in lawful possession of the property covered by OCT-R-165 at the time the writ of possession was implemented, may institute an action for having been disturbed in its enjoyment."[^286]

> "Under Article 539 of the New Civil Code, a lawful possessor is entitled to be respected in his possession and any disturbance of possession is a ground for the issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore the possession. Thus, petitioner's claim that the issuance of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction is improper because the instant case is allegedly one for accion publiciana deserves no consideration. This Court has already ruled in Torre, et al. v. Hon. J. Querubin, et al. that prior to the promulgation of the New Civil Code, it was deemed improper to issue a writ of preliminary injunction where the party to be enjoined had already taken complete material possession of the property involved. However, with the enactment of Article 539, the plaintiff is now allowed to avail of a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore him in his possession during the pendency of his action to recover possession."[^287]

> "From the aforementioned provisions of the Rules of Court and by its very nature and purpose, an action for unlawful detainer or forcible entry is a real action and in personam because the plaintiff seeks to enforce a personal obligation or liability on the defendant under Article 539 of the New Civil Code, for the latter to vacate the property subject of the action, restore physical possession thereof to the plaintiff, and pay actual damages by way of reasonable compensation for his use or occupation of the property."[^288]

> "Article 1674 is reproduced in section 9 of Rule 70. Article 539 of the Civil Code in its second paragraph grants to the possessor, who was deprived of the possession of his real property through forcible entry, the right to secure from an inferior court in the action for forcible entry a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction to restore him in his possession."[^289]

> "Article 1674 gives to the plaintiff in an unlawful detainer case originating in the inferior court and appealed to the Court of First Instance the remedy which article 539 gives to the plaintiff in a forcible entry case. It is designed to eliminate the injustice of the old rule which allowed the lessee to continue in possession during an appeal even if the owner or plaintiff has an immediate right to the premises in litigation (pp. 98,143, Report of Code Commission)."[^290]

> "An adverse possessor, who is not the mortgagor, cannot just be deprived of his possession, let alone be bound by the terms of the chattel mortgage contract, simply because the mortgagee brings up an action for replevin."[^291]

> "Long divorced from doubt is the doctrine that where legal title is disputed and the possessor asserts ownership over the land in controversy, no injunction can issue to dispossess him."[^292]

# Tips

## Diagnostic Checklist for Article 539 Claims and Defenses

1. **Identify the Claimant's Possessory Status and Priority in Time:**
   - Does the claimant hold possession as an owner, co-owner, lessee, agricultural tenant, homestead or pasture lease applicant, good-faith chattel buyer or mere lawful holder? Under [*Philippine Trust Company v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/36989), Article 539(1) protects all kinds of possession except that which constitutes a crime. [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863) adds that every possessor includes a mere holder.
   - Did the claimant have prior physical possession before the disturbance? Under [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602) and [*Montañez v. Mendoza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51691), priority in time entitles the peaceable occupant to protection and restoration even against an owner or co-owner who resorts to force.

2. **Select the Proper Judicial Action and Verify Service of Summons:**
   - Check when and how dispossession occurred to choose among forcible entry or unlawful detainer (within one year), *accion publiciana* (after one year to determine the better right of possession independently of title) or *acción de reivindicación* (to recover ownership), as delineated in [*Emilia v. Bado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html) and [*Gabriel, Jr. v. Crisologo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57063).
   - Ensure personal or strictly compliant substituted service of summons on the defendant. Under [*Domagas v. Jensen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43231), forcible entry and unlawful detainer are real actions *in personam* enforcing a personal liability under Article 539(1), so leaving summons with a mere visitor renders the ejectment judgment void for lack of jurisdiction over the defendant's person.

3. **Check the Procedural Requirements for a Writ of Preliminary Mandatory Injunction:**
   - In an original forcible entry action under the codal text of Article 539(2), verify that the motion for a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction is filed within ten days from the filing of the complaint (noting that Section 15, Rule 70 of the 1997 Rules of Civil Procedure, as quoted in [*Domagas v. Jensen*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/43231) and cited in [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863), sets a five-day filing period for forcible entry or unlawful detainer) and that the court resolves it within thirty days from filing, as enforced in [*Mara, Inc. v. Estrella*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1975/jul1975/gr_l_40511_1975.html).
   - In an *accion publiciana* or recovery of possession suit, establish under [*Semirara Coal Corporation v. HGL Development Corporation*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40420) that: (a) the invasion of the right is material and substantial, (b) the complainant's right is clear and unmistakable and (c) there is an urgent and permanent necessity for the writ to prevent serious damage.
   - In an unlawful detainer or ejectment appeal, invoke Article 1674 of the Civil Code and Section 9, Rule 70 of the Rules of Court within ten days from the perfection of the appeal by showing either that the lessee's appeal is frivolous or dilatory, as in [*De Laureano v. Adil*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1976/jul1976/gr_43345_1976.html), or that the lessor's appeal is *prima facie* meritorious, as in [*Dayao v. Shell Company of the Philippines, Ltd.*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/apr1980/gr_32475_1980.html).

## Common Traps to Avoid

> [!TIP]
> **Distinguish Co-Owner Entry by Force from Peaceful Entry Under Claim of Co-Ownership:** When a co-owner or a buyer of an unpartitioned share enters common property, check how the entry was made. Under [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), if the co-owner takes possession of a definite portion without force and while recognizing the co-ownership, ejectment will not lie because he or she possesses as a trustee for all. If the entering co-owner fences off a specific portion and demolishes structures to exclude co-owners who were in prior physical possession, forcible entry lies to evict the intruding co-owner, though no monthly rentals can be charged against a co-owner.

> [!NOTE]
> **Do Not Confuse Supersedeas Bonds with Mandatory Injunctions on Appeal:** In an unlawful detainer appeal under [*De Laureano v. Adil*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1976/jul1976/gr_43345_1976.html), a lessee's filing of a supersedeas bond and making of periodic monthly rental deposits at the rate fixed in the inferior court judgment stays execution only as to the accrued and ongoing monetary liability for rentals or reasonable compensation. It does not prevent the appellate court from issuing a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction under Article 1674 in relation to Article 539(2) to restore physical possession to the lessor when the lessee's appeal is manifestly frivolous and dilatory.

> [!TIP]
> **Check Administrative Exhaustion Against Who Holds Prior Possession of Public Land:** When public land is subject to a pending administrative protest or boundary investigation before the Bureau of Lands or Bureau of Forestry, check who had prior peaceful possession. Under [*Aguilon v. Bohol*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1977/oct1977/gr_27169_1977.html) and [*Painaga v. Cortes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1991/oct1991/gr_53837_1991.html), a prior peaceful possessor may immediately sue in the regular courts under Article 539(1) for *accion publiciana* or prohibitory injunction without waiting for the administrative agency to resolve ownership or patent validity. Conversely, under [*Buayan Cattle Co., Inc. v. Quintillan*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1984/mar1984/gr_l-26970_1984.html), a subsequent claimant who forcibly removes the prior lessee's fences and skips pending forestry administrative remedies has no cause of action for an injunction.

## What Worked and What Failed in Decided Cases

- **What Worked for Possessors Seeking Protection or Restoration:**
  - Proving prior physical possession and fixing the distinct identity of the land against a defendant invoking a Torrens title that covered a different parcel in [*Montañez v. Mendoza*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/51691).
  - Presenting registered Torrens titles, tax payments since 1969, appointment of an administrator and an offer to sell the occupied portions to establish a preferred claim of possession in an *accion publiciana* in [*Gabriel, Jr. v. Crisologo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57063).
  - Securing a writ of preliminary mandatory injunction under Article 539(1) and Article 433 to restore third-party buyers who had purchased and occupied unregistered land prior to a mortgage foreclosure and were evicted by an *ex parte* writ of possession in [*Spouses Castro v. Spouses Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35087).
  - Enforcing a lessee's contractual possession against the owner to recover the value of 228 repossessed buses after eleven years and P2,000,000.00 for office furniture and equipment in [*Metro Manila Transit Corporation v. D.M. Consortium, Inc.*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41302) and restoring a forcibly evicted lessee for the unused balance of a four-year lease term despite the lapse of the contract dates *pendente lite* in [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863).
  - Recovering 42.8 metric tons of scrap engine blocks seized under a search warrant by showing good-faith purchase for value in the ordinary course of business under Articles 539(1) and 559 where the original owner had voluntarily delivered the goods to an alleged swindler and no criminal judgment of restitution existed in [*Yu v. Honrado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1980/aug1980/gr_50025_1980.html).

- **What Failed for Litigants and Movants:**
  - Filing a principal action for injunction over an alleged boundary encroachment where the defendant possessed the area under a subsisting Torrens title, instead of bringing an *acción de reivindicación*, in [*Emilia v. Bado*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1968/apr1968/gr_l-23685_1968.html).
  - Attempting to collaterally attack the validity of a plaintiff's Torrens certificates of title under Presidential Decree No. 1271 as a defense in an *accion publiciana* in [*Gabriel, Jr. v. Crisologo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57063).
  - Using a replevin suit to seize a mortgaged motor vehicle from a third-party possessor without serving summons on the indispensable debtor-mortgagors to establish their default in [*BA Finance Corporation v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/33853).
  - Seeking receivership over estate properties in the respondent co-heir's actual possession without clear and convincing proof of fraud where notices of *lis pendens* and the respondent's offer of a counterbond protected the claimants in [*Vivares v. Reyes*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/44835).
  - Moving as a defendant-lessee for immediate execution pending appeal of a Regional Trial Court decision ordering the plaintiff-lessor to restore possession, which failed in [*Limitless Potentials, Inc. v. Quilala*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42863) because Section 19, Rule 70 authorizes immediate execution pending appeal only when judgment is rendered in favor of the plaintiff against the defendant.
