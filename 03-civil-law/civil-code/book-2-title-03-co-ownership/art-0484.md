> ART. 484. There is co-ownership whenever the ownership of an undivided thing or right belongs to different persons.
>
> In default of contracts, or of special provisions, co-ownership shall be governed by the provisions of this Title. (392)[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 484 opens Title III of Book II of the Civil Code by laying down two foundational rules. Its first paragraph defines co-ownership as the state in which ownership of an undivided thing or right belongs to different persons. Its second paragraph sets the hierarchy of governing norms: contracts and special legal provisions govern first, while Title III applies in their default.

**In brief**, Article 484 operates through four core rules:
1. **Undivided ownership is essential.** Each co-owner exercises dominion over the whole property while owning only an abstract, ideal or spiritual share prior to partition.
2. **Concrete physical division ends co-ownership.** Once portions are concretely determined and separately identifiable, co-ownership ceases even if the portions lack technical descriptions or remain under one Torrens title.
3. **Co-ownership must be proved by preponderance of evidence.** Mere construction of a house on another's land, administrative management or payment of realty taxes does not create co-ownership.
4. **Special provisions and contracts prevail over Title III.** Where a special regime such as Family Code Article 148 or Civil Code succession rules governs, Title III yields. Where no contract or special rule conflicts, Title III applies suppletorily, as when [Article 487](art-0487.md) allows one co-owner spouse to recover conjugal vehicles.

This commentary explains the legal nature of undivided co-ownership, when co-ownership terminates and how Article 484 interacts with possession, partition, legal redemption, accession and the governing-law hierarchy under paragraph 2.

## Meaning and Characteristics of Co-Ownership (Paragraph 1)

Under the first paragraph of Article 484, co-ownership exists whenever the ownership of an undivided thing or right belongs to different persons. A co-owner of an undivided parcel of land is an owner of the whole who exercises the right of dominion over the whole while simultaneously owning a portion that is truly abstract. The Supreme Court stated this doctrine in [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152)[^2] and [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649).[^3]

A co-owner cannot point to a particular physical segment of the common property as exclusively belonging to that co-owner because the share is intangible and spiritual rather than physically localized. The Supreme Court applied this rule in [*Salatandol v. Retes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/jun1988/gr_l-38120_1988.html)[^4] and [*Hernandez v. Quitain*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/nov1988/gr_l-48457_1988.html).[^5] Read alongside [Article 485](art-0485.md), [Article 486](art-0486.md) and [Article 493](art-0493.md), Article 484 confirms that co-owners hold a pro indiviso, pro rata and pari passu right proportional to their respective shares and on equal footing with one another, as explained in [*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492).[^6]

### Co-Ownership Arising from Succession

Co-ownership commonly arises upon the death of a property owner when two or more heirs inherit an unpartitioned estate under [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md). In [*Spouses Bolaños v. Bernarte*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35479), the Supreme Court applied Article 484 alongside [Article 979](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-0979.md) and [Article 980](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-0980.md) to hold that an unregistered lot acquired by a widower passed in equal one-eleventh undivided shares to all eleven legitimate children from his first and second marriages. Consequently, two sisters who sold the entire lot validly transferred only their combined two-elevenths ideal shares.[^7]

In [*Teodoro v. Espino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56498), where an ancestral lot remained unpartitioned in the common grandfather's name, the Supreme Court held under Article 484 and [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md) that co-ownership alone gave the nephew a right of possession against collateral relatives who barricaded the lot.[^8] Likewise, in [*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492), heirs descended from the original owner's excluded children were provisionally recognized as co-owners by intestate succession who could not be ejected by a fellow co-owner prior to partition.[^9]

### When No Co-Ownership Exists

Two recurring rules mark the outer boundaries of Article 484 paragraph 1.

First, **separately identifiable properties belonging to different owners do not form a co-ownership**. In [*Victoria v. Pidlaoan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61799), the Supreme Court squarely held that constructing a house on another person's land does not create co-ownership regardless of whether the house is worth more than the lot. Because a house and a lot are separately identifiable properties that can belong to different owners, the builder's remedy lies under [Article 448](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0448.md) rather than Article 484.[^10]

Second, **a claimant alleging co-ownership bears the burden of proving it by preponderance of evidence**. In [*Co Giok Lun v. Co*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26015), the Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of a partition suit because a brother's payment of realty taxes and utilities, his designation as administrator in insurance records, hearsay testimony about start-up capital and an unproven Chinese custom under [Article 12](../preliminary-title/art-0012.md) failed to prove co-ownership against notarized deeds of sale exclusively in the other brother's name.[^11] Similarly, while a Torrens certificate issued in one person's name does not foreclose proof of co-ownership, failure to prove actual contribution toward purchasing the land defeats a co-ownership claim, as held in [*Victoria v. Pidlaoan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61799)[^12] and [*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293).[^13]

## Concrete Segregation Terminates Co-Ownership

Co-ownership ceases to exist once the portions belonging to different owners are concretely determined and separately identifiable, even if those portions are not yet technically described or remain covered by a single certificate of title. This principle governs two major legal consequences.

### Extinguishment of Legal Redemption Under Articles 1620 and 1623

The right of legal redemption granted to a co-owner under [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md) and [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md) presupposes a subsisting co-ownership under Article 484. Once the land is divided into concrete, localized shares, legal redemption is no longer available:
- In [*Salatandol v. Retes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/jun1988/gr_l-38120_1988.html), actual partition had already identified and localized the vendor's one-third share despite remaining under one Original Certificate of Title (OCT), extinguishing co-ownership and barring pre-emption under [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md) (in addition to lack of written vendor notice under [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md)).[^14]
- In [*Hernandez v. Quitain*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/nov1988/gr_l-48457_1988.html), a 1974 extrajudicial partition adjudicating a 46.40-square-meter parcel to one heir ended co-ownership among the siblings, barring another sister from redeeming the lot from its buyer under [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md) or [Article 1622](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1622.md).[^15]
- In [*Spouses Si v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37419), three 1954 deeds of sale technically described each brother's portion and the portions were separately tax-declared in 1970, ending co-ownership and making legal redemption under [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md) inapplicable.[^16]

### Bar Against a Second Partition Suit

Once a final judgment or approved compromise segregates the co-owners' specific portions, no co-ownership remains to partition. In [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649), a 1971 compromise judgment incorporating a subdivision survey plan concretely segregated three-fifths and two-fifths shares of Lot No. 252. Even though the old TCT remained uncancelled, co-ownership had ended and a new partition action filed seventeen years later was barred by res judicata and lack of cause of action, though Section 81 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 did not bar belated registration of the 1971 compromise judgment.[^17]

By contrast, when co-ownership still subsists because no final adjudication has segregated specific shares, [Article 494](art-0494.md) preserves every co-owner's imprescriptible right to demand partition at any time. In [*Quintos v. Nicolas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57149), quoting [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649) on Article 484, the Supreme Court held that a prior dismissal for failure to prosecute under Section 3, Rule 17 is deemed without prejudice in partition cases so long as the thirty-to-seventy co-ownership still subsists.[^18]

## Possession, Ejectment and Improvements Among Co-Owners

Because every co-owner holds dominion over the undivided whole under Article 484, co-owners share equal possessory rights until partition.

In [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152), the Supreme Court held that one co-owner may file an action for recovery of possession under [Article 487](art-0487.md) against another co-owner who takes exclusive possession of a co-owned fishpond. However, before partition, the sole effect of that suit is judicial recognition of co-ownership and of both co-owners' equal right to possess, use and enjoy the entire property. The court cannot deliver a concrete one-half physical portion prior to partition. Even before partition, a co-owner who appropriates exclusive use without the other co-owner's silent tolerance may be ordered to pay reasonable rent.[^19]

In [*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492), the Supreme Court clarified that in an accion publiciana, trial courts must provisionally resolve a defendant's co-ownership defense without treating it as a prohibited collateral attack on a Torrens title under Presidential Decree No. 1529. Once co-ownership is provisionally established, one co-owner cannot eject another prior to partition.[^20] In [*Teodoro v. Espino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56498), the Supreme Court held that a co-owner forcibly dispossessed by fellow co-owners from the specific site of an unpartitioned ancestral lot occupied by his predecessor must be restored to lawful possession.[^21]

With respect to improvements constructed on co-owned land, [*Spouses Belvis v. Spouses Erola*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65574) explained that [Article 448](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0448.md) does not generally apply to a co-owner who builds, plants or sows on property owned in common. Unlike an outsider under [Article 445](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0445.md), a co-owner builds on his or her own undivided land. Articles 484 to 501 of the Civil Code already govern the rights and reimbursement rules of co-owners.[^22]

## Hierarchy of Governing Law (Paragraph 2)

The second paragraph of Article 484 provides that in default of contracts or special provisions, co-ownership is governed by Title III of Book II. Supreme Court decisions illustrate both halves of this rule.

First, **Title III applies suppletorily where no contract or special provision conflicts**. In [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822), the Supreme Court directly applied the second paragraph of Article 484. Because Article 108 of the Family Code makes partnership rules suppletory to the conjugal partnership of gains and [Article 1811](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md) makes partners co-owners of specific partnership property, spouses Glenn and Karen Go were co-owners of the leased vehicles of Kargo Enterprises. Applying Article 484 paragraph 2, the Supreme Court invoked [Article 487](art-0487.md) so that Karen Go could file replevin suits to recover the vehicles without joining her husband as an indispensable party.[^23]

Second, **special statutory regimes prevail over Title III where applicable**:
- **Adulterous or bigamous cohabitation:** In [*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293), petitioner argued under Article 144 and both paragraphs of Article 484 of the Civil Code that he was an equal co-owner of a Makati lot acquired during his extramarital cohabitation. Rejecting that argument, the Supreme Court held that Article 148 of the Family Code, as a special provision, governs adulterous cohabitation retroactively and limits co-ownership strictly to properties acquired through proven actual joint contribution of money, property or industry.[^24]
- **Partition of a decedent's estate:** In [*Heirs of Ernesto Morales v. Agustin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64260), the Supreme Court distinguished ordinary partition of co-owned property under Title III and Article 484 from partition of an inheritance among heirs. Although both follow Rule 69 procedurally, estate partition is governed by Title IV on Succession, including [Article 777](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-0777.md), [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md), collation under [Article 1061](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1061.md) and Rule 74, requiring trial courts to collate all properties of the decedent rather than grant partial summary partition.[^25]

## Case Discussions

### [*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492): Provisional Co-Ownership Bars Ejectment in Accion Publiciana

George dela Cruz sued Spouses Salvador and Leonida Bangug and Spouses Venerandy and Jesusa Adolfo in the Municipal Trial Court in Cities of Ilagan City, Isabela (Civil Case No. 1627) to recover possession of a 2,172-square-meter parcel under TCT No. T-388110 (assessed at Php11,140.00), carved out of his grandmother Cayetana Guitang's 8,657-square-meter land under OCT No. 3367 via his father Severino's 1982 Affidavit of Adjudication and 1983 Deed of Reconveyance. Petitioners answered that they were co-heirs through Cayetana's excluded daughters Rufina and Juliana. The Municipal Trial Court in Cities, the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 164563 refused to resolve ownership as a prohibited collateral attack and ordered eviction. Reversing, the Supreme Court held that in an accion publiciana, courts must provisionally resolve ownership when inextricably linked to possession without violating Presidential Decree No. 1529. Applying Article 484 together with [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md), [Article 485](art-0485.md), [Article 486](art-0486.md) and [Article 493](art-0493.md), the Supreme Court provisionally found that Cayetana's seven children became pro indiviso co-owners (roughly 1,236 square meters undivided each), so respondent could not eject fellow co-owners prior to partition. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' November 5, 2020 Decision and February 10, 2022 Resolution and dismissed Civil Case No. 1627 for lack of cause of action, stressing that its ownership ruling was strictly provisional.[^26] This case shows that provisional co-ownership under Article 484 defeats an ejectment complaint filed by a fellow co-owner.

### [*Spouses Belvis v. Spouses Erola*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65574): Improvements by a Co-Owner Are Governed by Articles 484 to 501, Not Article 448

Spouses Conrado and Marilyn Erola sued Conrado's sister Cecilia Belvis and co-petitioners for unlawful detainer in the Municipal Circuit Trial Court of Pontevedra, Capiz (Civil Case No. 489) over Lot 597 (29,772 square meters, TCT No. T-26108). Petitioners claimed co-ownership by succession and good-faith improvements made over thirty-four years. The trial court ordered eviction plus Php1,000.00 monthly rental from July 2, 2012 and Php20,000.00 in litigation expenses and attorney's fees, which Branch 15 of the Regional Trial Court of Roxas City and the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CEB-SP No. 10632 affirmed. The Supreme Court agreed that petitioners failed to prove co-ownership or implied trust. It added that even assuming Cecilia were a co-owner, [Article 448](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0448.md) does not generally apply to a co-owner who builds on common land because a co-owner builds on his or her own undivided land under [Article 445](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0445.md) and co-ownerships are governed by Articles 484 to 501. However, because respondents knew of and did not oppose permanent improvements for over thirty-four years, mutual bad faith under [Article 453](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0453.md) triggered [Article 448](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0448.md) in relation to [Article 546](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0546.md) and [Article 548](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0548.md). The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed the Court of Appeals' August 7, 2017 Decision and April 16, 2018 Resolution and remanded the case to the court of origin to determine facts essential to apply Articles 448, 546 and 548 and physical possession.[^27] The ruling marks the doctrinal boundary between accession under Article 448 and co-ownership under Articles 484 to 501.

### [*Heirs of Ernesto Morales v. Agustin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64260): Estate Partition Under Succession Rules Distinguished from Ordinary Co-Ownership Partition

Astrid Morales Agustin sued her cousins, the Heirs of Ernesto Morales, in Branch 12 of the Regional Trial Court of Laoag City (Civil Case No. 14438-12) to partition Lot No. 9217-A (TCT No. T-37139) registered in their deceased grandfather Jayme Morales's name. Petitioners countered that another estate property had to be collated, yet the trial court rendered summary judgment partitioning Lot No. 9217-A alone, which the Court of Appeals affirmed in CA-G.R. CV No. 101991. Applying Article 484 to contrast the two regimes, the Supreme Court distinguished ordinary partition of co-owned property under Title III from partition of a decedent's estate based on successional rights under [Article 777](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-0777.md), [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md), collation under [Article 1061](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1061.md) and Rule 74. Although both use Rule 69 procedurally, estate partition requires collating the decedent's other properties rather than partial summary division absent extreme circumstances. The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' August 13, 2015 Decision and April 21, 2016 Resolution and remanded the case to Branch 12 of the Regional Trial Court of Laoag City for further proceedings with dispatch.[^28] The case clarifies that hereditary co-ownership of a decedent's estate is governed by succession partition rules rather than Article 484 alone.

### [*Victoria v. Pidlaoan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61799): Constructing a House on Another's Land Does Not Create Co-Ownership

Elma Pidlaoan bought a 201-square-meter lot in Lucena City (TCT No. T-50282) in 1984. Her companion Rosario Victoria built a house on it. Elma later transferred the lot to Normita Jacob Pidlaoan under a notarized Deed of Donation that concealed an unnotarized Php30,000.00 sale (the lot's 1994 market value was Php32,160). Rosario claimed co-ownership because her house was worth more than the lot. The Regional Trial Court ruled Elma donated only half, while the Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. CV No. 89235 holding Elma donated the whole lot. Applying Article 484, the Supreme Court squarely ruled that mere construction of a house on another person's land does not create co-ownership regardless of the house's value because a house and a lot are separately identifiable properties that can belong to different owners. Rosario failed to prove any contribution to buying the lot, leaving her recourse under [Article 448](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0448.md). Relying on respondents' judicial admissions, the Supreme Court held the donation was relatively simulated to conceal a valid absolute sale of the entire lot rather than an equitable mortgage. The Supreme Court partially granted the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' March 26, 2010 Decision and March 15, 2011 Resolution with the modification that Elma sold the whole lot to Normita and imposed costs against petitioners.[^29] This decision is the leading authority holding that separate ownership of house and lot negates co-ownership under Article 484.

### [*Quintos v. Nicolas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57149): Subsisting Co-Ownership Preserves Partition Despite Prior Dismissal for Failure to Prosecute

Three Ibarra siblings sued seven siblings and buyers Spouses Recto and Rosemarie Candelario (who bought the seven-tenths undivided share of a 281-square-meter lot in Camiling, Tarlac under TCT No. 318717, later TCT Nos. 390484 and 434304) in Branch 68 of the Regional Trial Court of Camiling, Tarlac (Civil Case No. 09-15) to quiet title. Respondents counterclaimed for partition even though their earlier partition suit (Civil Case No. 02-52) had been dismissed in 2004 for failure to appear. The trial court and the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CV No. 98919 upheld the thirty-to-seventy co-ownership and approved respondents' subdivision plan. Quoting [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649) on Article 484, the Supreme Court distinguished already segregated property from subsisting co-ownership. Because no final judgment had segregated shares in the thirty-to-seventy co-ownership, [Article 494](art-0494.md) prevailed over Section 3, Rule 17 so the prior dismissal was deemed without prejudice and neither res judicata nor laches barred partition. However, the falsified Agreement of Subdivision lacked petitioners' consent under [Article 496](art-0496.md). The Supreme Court partly granted the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' July 8, 2013 Decision and November 22, 2013 Resolution with modification and remanded the case to Branch 68 of the Regional Trial Court of Camiling, Tarlac for Rule 69 partition.[^30] The decision shows that subsisting undivided co-ownership keeps partition open despite an earlier non-merits dismissal.

### [*Teodoro v. Espino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56498): Co-Ownership Suffices to Restore Lawful Possession in Forcible Entry

Teodoro Teodoro (substituted by his sons Nelson and Rolando) sued collateral relatives in the Municipal Trial Court of Bulacan, Bulacan (Civil Case No. 1240) for forcible entry after respondents fenced and barricaded the cleared site of common grandfather Genaro Teodoro's ancestral house inside unpartitioned Lot No. 2476 (248 square meters, Tax Declaration No. 99-05003-0246), which Aunt Petra had devised to Teodoro in a probated holographic will. The Municipal Trial Court dismissed the suit. Branch 81 of the Regional Trial Court of Malolos, Bulacan (Civil Case No. 634-M-06) vacated the dismissal, restored Teodoro's possession and ordered respondents to pay costs of suit. The Court of Appeals reversed and dismissed in CA-G.R. SP No. 99805. Applying Article 484 and [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md), the Supreme Court held that Lot No. 2476 remained unpartitioned co-owned property registered in Genaro's name. Exclusive ownership was unnecessary because co-ownership entitled both sides to exercise possession and neither could exclude the other from the portion Petra had occupied. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' Decision, reinstated the trial court's Decision in Civil Case No. 634-M-06 and made no pronouncement as to costs.[^31] The case confirms that co-ownership under Article 484 supports restoration of possession in forcible entry without adjudicating final ownership.

### [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649): Approved Compromise Subdivision Plan Ends Co-Ownership Even Under One Title

Petitioners acquired a three-fifths interest in Lot No. 252 (TCT No. RT-488 / 12206) at a 1951 execution sale enforcing a 1947 judgment awarding Php500.00 yearly rent, while respondents retained two-fifths. On December 1, 1971, a final Compromise Judgment in Civil Case No. 36-C approved a subdivision survey plan concretely segregating petitioners' three-fifths and respondents' two-fifths portions. Seventeen years later, petitioners filed Civil Case No. 1153-87-C in Branch 36 of the Regional Trial Court of Calamba, Laguna for partition and damages (specifying only Php30,000.00 attorney's fees and Php500.00 per court appearance in the prayer). Both lower courts dismissed the suit on res judicata in CA-G.R. CV No. 26109. Applying Article 484 alongside [Article 494](art-0494.md) and [Article 1079](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1079.md), the Supreme Court held that there is no co-ownership when different portions are already concretely determined and separately identifiable, even if still embraced in one certificate of title. Because the 1971 compromise judgment terminated co-ownership, the new partition suit failed for res judicata, lack of cause of action and nonpayment of docket fees. The Supreme Court clarified that petitioners did not lose their three-fifths ownership and that Section 81 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 does not bar belated registration of the 1971 compromise judgment. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals' July 13, 2001 Decision.[^32] This landmark ruling establishes that concrete allocation under a compromise judgment terminates Article 484 co-ownership.

### [*Co Giok Lun v. Co*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26015): Partition Requires Preponderant Proof That Co-Ownership Exists

The heirs of Co Giok Lun sued the heirs of his brother Co Bon Fieng (represented by Jose Co) in Branch 54 of the Regional Trial Court of Gubat, Sorsogon (Civil Case No. 1601) to partition commercial land in Gubat (720.68 square meters, assessed at Php12,370.00) and a lot in Barcelona, Sorsogon. The trial court ordered a survey and partition, but the Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. CV No. 85920 and declared Co Bon Fieng and his heirs exclusive owners. Applying Article 484, the Supreme Court stressed that partition cannot be ordered unless the plaintiff first proves co-ownership by preponderance of evidence. Petitioners' tax declarations under Fieng's name paid by Lun, insurance papers identifying Lun as mere administrator, hearsay testimony claiming their father gave Php8,000.00 as partnership capital, an unproven Chinese custom under [Article 12](../preliminary-title/art-0012.md) and a 1923 Php1,200 deed to their father did not establish co-ownership against notarized 1935 and 1936 deeds conveying the Gubat lot solely to Fieng. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals' April 23, 2008 Decision and September 10, 2008 Resolution.[^33] The decision underscores that administrative control and tax payments do not establish Article 484 co-ownership.

### [*Spouses Bolaños v. Bernarte*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35479): Unpartitioned Estate Co-Owned Equally by Children from Two Marriages

Four children from Roman Zuñiga, Sr.'s second marriage sued buyers Spouses Mariano and Emma Bolaños and half-sisters Flavia and Cresencia in the Regional Trial Court of Legazpi City (Civil Case No. 10033) for partial nullity of June 20, 2001 deeds of sale over unregistered Lot No. 1-P (238 square meters in Rapu-Rapu, Albay). The trial court found Lot No. 1-P was Roman's capital property acquired while a widower in 1948, limited the buyers' interest to Flavia's and Cresencia's combined two-elevenths ideal shares and ordered defendants to pay Php15,000 attorney's fees, Php10,000 litigation expenses and costs. The Court of Appeals affirmed in toto in CA-G.R. CV No. 84452. Resolving the sole question of law on Article 484 relative to [Article 979](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-0979.md), [Article 980](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-0980.md) and [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md), the Supreme Court held that all eleven legitimate children from both marriages inherited Lot No. 1-P in equal undivided shares. Without partition, the two sisters could convey only their two-elevenths aliquot interest. Buyers of unregistered land aware of a tax declaration naming sisters and brothers could not claim good faith. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' March 30, 2007 Decision and November 26, 2007 Resolution and imposed costs against petitioners.[^34] The ruling illustrates how unpartitioned intestate succession creates equal Article 484 co-ownership across multiple marriages.

### [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822): Paragraph 2 Applies Title III Suppletorily to Allow Replevin by One Co-Owner Spouse

Karen T. Go, doing business as Kargo Enterprises, filed two replevin complaints (Civil Case Nos. 98-599 and 98-598) in Branch 37 of the Regional Trial Court of Cagayan de Oro City against Roger Navarro to recover two leased motor vehicles without joining her husband Glenn Go as principal co-plaintiff. The trial court denied Navarro's motion to dismiss. The Court of Appeals affirmed in CA-G.R. SP No. 64701. Holding for purposes of that case that Kargo Enterprises was conjugal property, the Supreme Court applied Article 108 of the Family Code (making partnership rules suppletory) and [Article 1811](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md) of the Civil Code to recognize Glenn and Karen Go as co-owners of the leased vehicles. Directly applying the second paragraph of Article 484, the Supreme Court ruled that in default of contracts or special provisions, Title III governs co-ownership. Either spouse may therefore sue under [Article 487](art-0487.md) to recover possession of personal property while joining the other spouse merely as a pro-forma party. It also held that prior demand is not a condition precedent to replevin under Rule 60. The Supreme Court denied the petition for review for lack of merit and imposed costs against petitioner Roger V. Navarro.[^35] This decision is the leading authority directly applying the second paragraph of Article 484.

### [*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293): Family Code Article 148 Supersedes General Article 484 Co-Ownership in Adulterous Unions

Lupo Atienza, a married man who cohabited with Yolanda De Castro beginning in 1983, sued her in Branch 61 of the Regional Trial Court of Makati City (Civil Case No. 92-1423) to partition a Bel-Air, Makati lot (TCT No. 147828) purchased in 1987 for Php2,600,000.00. The trial court declared equal co-ownership, but the Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. CV No. 69797 declaring Yolanda exclusive owner. On review, Lupo argued under Article 144 and both paragraphs of Article 484 of the Civil Code that he was a co-owner without proving contribution. Rejecting that argument, the Supreme Court held that Article 148 of the Family Code applies retroactively as a special provision to adulterous cohabitation and restricts co-ownership exclusively to properties acquired through proven actual joint contribution of money, property or industry. Because Lupo failed to prove any contribution to the Php2,600,000.00 price while Yolanda substantiated exclusive acquisition from her earnings as an accountant and businesswoman, no co-ownership arose. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' April 29, 2005 Decision and September 16, 2005 Resolution and imposed costs against petitioner.[^36] The case demonstrates how a special statutory provision displaces default Title III co-ownership under Article 484 paragraph 2.

### [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152): Co-Owner Suing Co-Owner Recovers Recognition of Co-Ownership and Rent, Not a Concrete Half

Jose Abejo sued co-owner Manuel De Guia, who had exclusively possessed their Bulacan fishpond (TCT No. 6358) since July 1974 and had offered to settle for Php300,000.00 on unacceptable conditions, for recovery of possession of his one-half undivided share and damages. The trial court ordered De Guia to vacate one-half and pay Php262,500.00 compensatory damages (consisting of Php212,500.00 in unpaid rent plus Php50,000.00 refunded by Abejo to a cancelled lessee) and Php20,000.00 attorney's fees (after an earlier related case had awarded Php20,000.00 attorney's fees). The Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CV No. 39875 reduced compensatory damages to Php212,500.00 (based on Php25,000.00 yearly rent) and affirmed Php20,000.00 attorney's fees. Applying Article 484 and [Article 487](art-0487.md), the Supreme Court held that while one co-owner may sue an exclusively possessing co-owner, the sole effect prior to partition is recognition of co-ownership and equal possession of the entire fishpond rather than delivery of a determinate half. However, because De Guia appropriated exclusive use without Abejo's silent tolerance, reasonable rent and attorney's fees under Article 2208(2) were recoverable before partition. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals' August 22, 1994 Decision and June 27, 1995 Resolution as to Php212,500 compensatory damages and Php20,000 attorney's fees. It modified the judgment to recognize co-ownership over TCT No. 6358 without prejudice to CA-G.R. CV No. 38031 and other cases, grant equal possession prior to partition and impose 6% annual interest until finality and 12% thereafter on the Php25,000 yearly rent from November 27, 1983 to May 1992 and from June 1992 to finality. It also ordered Php25,000 yearly rent after finality while De Guia retains exclusive possession unless modified by a proper court.[^37] This landmark ruling defines the exact scope of possessory and monetary relief between co-owners before partition.

### [*Spouses Si v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37419): Concretely Determined Portions Extinguish Co-Ownership and Redemption

Spouses Jose Armada and Remedios Almanzor sued buyers Spouses Serafin and Anita Si in Branch 113 of the Regional Trial Court of Pasay City (Civil Case No. 8023-P) to annul the March 27, 1979 Php75,000.00 sale of brother Crisostomo Armada's 113.34-square-meter share of a 340-square-meter Pasay lot (TCT No. 16007, later TCT No. 24751) and exercise co-owner legal redemption under [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md). The trial court dismissed the suit on August 29, 1989, but the Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. CV No. 30727, allowing redemption and awarding Php10,000.00 moral damages and Php10,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses. Applying Article 484, the Supreme Court held that there is no co-ownership when different portions are already concretely determined and separately identifiable, even if not yet technically described. Because the parents' October 2, 1954 three deeds of sale segregated each brother's portion and each share was separately tax-declared in 1970, co-ownership had ended and [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md) no longer applied. Moreover, Jose's February 22, 1979 letter proved actual notice of the impending sale. The Supreme Court granted the petition, annulled and set aside the Court of Appeals' March 25, 1994 Decision and March 24, 1995 and September 6, 1995 Resolutions, dismissed Civil Case No. 8023-P for lack of merit and reinstated the trial court's August 29, 1989 Decision.[^38] The case shows that deeds and tax declarations identifying each portion can end co-ownership and defeat a claim of legal redemption.

### [*Hernandez v. Quitain*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/nov1988/gr_l-48457_1988.html): Extrajudicial Partition Extinguishes Co-Ownership and Legal Redemption

Seven Manlapaz heirs extrajudicially partitioned their parents' land in Baleno, Masbate on December 30, 1974. On March 8, 1975, Sancho Manlapaz sold his adjudicated 46.40-square-meter lot to Perla Hernandez for Php3,000.00. Sister Ernesta Valdemoro sued for co-owner legal redemption, depositing Php3,000.00. Branch II of the Court of First Instance of Masbate (Civil Case No. 464-II) allowed redemption and ordered Hernandez to resell the lot for Php3,000.00. On direct review, the Supreme Court held that the foundation of legal redemption under [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md) is a subsisting co-ownership under Article 484 requiring an undivided spiritual share. Because the parties stipulated to the 1974 extrajudicial partition adjudicating Sancho's lot separately, co-ownership had ceased and neither [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md) nor [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md) applied. Ernesta also failed to allege or prove impending or perfected resale required for adjoining-owner pre-emption under [Article 1622](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1622.md). Rejecting respondent's objection to late transmittal of the record on appeal as directory under Section 3, Rule 46, the Supreme Court granted the petition and reversed the trial court's decision.[^39] The ruling confirms that extrajudicial partition ends Article 484 co-ownership and terminates statutory redemption among former co-heirs.

### [*Salatandol v. Retes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/jun1988/gr_l-38120_1988.html): Localized Actual Partition Ends Co-Ownership Even Under a Single OCT

Eufemia Omole sold her one-third share of Lot No. 513 (OCT No. O-V 9307) to Catalina Retes in January 1965 for the stipulated purchase price (which Retes later resold to Omole and received back by donation). Flavia Salatandol and co-plaintiffs, registered owners of another one-third share, sued for legal pre-emption after receiving a letter from the Register of Deeds, depositing the purchase price with the clerk of court and stipulating Php500.00 in incurred legal expenses. On May 31, 1966, the Court of First Instance of Negros Oriental (Civil Case No. 4367) allowed pre-emption and awarded Php300.00 attorney's fees and costs. The Court of Appeals certified the pure question of law to the Supreme Court. First, the Supreme Court held under [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md) that the thirty-day period requires written notice from the vendor rather than the Register of Deeds, so redemption had not accrued. Second, even assuming notice sufficed, the Supreme Court applied Article 484 to hold that because actual partition had already identified and localized Eufemia's one-third share despite sharing one OCT, co-ownership in its real sense no longer existed and [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md) could not be invoked. The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the appealed judgment and dismissed the complaint without costs.[^40] The case establishes that physical localization of shares ends Article 484 co-ownership regardless of a shared OCT.

## Key Cases

- [*Salatandol v. Retes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/jun1988/gr_l-38120_1988.html) (1988): Held that actual partition localizing each party's one-third share ended Article 484 co-ownership even under one OCT, barring legal redemption under [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md).
- [*Hernandez v. Quitain*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/nov1988/gr_l-48457_1988.html) (1988): Confirmed that extrajudicial partition adjudicating concrete lots extinguished co-ownership under Article 484 and terminated co-owner legal redemption.
- [*Spouses Si v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37419) (2000): Ruled that three deeds technically describing each brother's portion, with separate tax declarations, ended co-ownership under Article 484 and made [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md) inapplicable.
- [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) (2003): Held that an [Article 487](art-0487.md) suit between co-owners before partition yields only recognition of co-ownership, equal possession of the whole and reasonable rent for non-tolerated exclusive use.
- [*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293) (2006): Rejected reliance on Civil Code Article 484 in an adulterous union and applied Family Code Article 148 requiring proof of actual joint contribution.
- [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822) (2009): Directly applied Article 484 paragraph 2 to invoke [Article 487](art-0487.md) suppletorily, permitting one spouse to sue in replevin to recover conjugal vehicles.
- [*Spouses Bolaños v. Bernarte*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35479) (2010): Applied Article 484 with [Article 979](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-0979.md) and [Article 980](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-0980.md) to confirm equal co-ownership among eleven children from two marriages, limiting a sale by two sisters to two-elevenths.
- [*Co Giok Lun v. Co*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26015) (2011): Affirmed dismissal of a partition suit because administrative acts, tax payments and an unproven Chinese custom failed to prove Article 484 co-ownership.
- [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649) (2012): Held that a final compromise judgment with a subdivision plan ended Article 484 co-ownership even under one TCT, barring a second partition suit by res judicata.
- [*Teodoro v. Espino*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56498) (2014): Held under Article 484 and [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md) that co-ownership alone sufficed to restore a forcibly dispossessed co-heir to lawful possession.
- [*Quintos v. Nicolas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57149) (2014): Quoted [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649) on Article 484 to hold that where co-ownership still subsists, a prior dismissal for failure to prosecute does not bar partition under [Article 494](art-0494.md).
- [*Victoria v. Pidlaoan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61799) (2016): Held under Article 484 that building a house on another person's land does not create co-ownership regardless of the house's value because house and lot are separately identifiable properties.
- [*Heirs of Ernesto Morales v. Agustin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64260) (2018): Distinguished ordinary co-ownership partition under Article 484 from estate partition under succession law requiring collation under [Article 1061](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1061.md).
- [*Spouses Belvis v. Spouses Erola*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65574) (2019): Explained that [Article 448](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0448.md) does not generally apply to a co-owner building on common land because Articles 484 to 501 govern co-ownerships.
- [*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492) (2022): Applied Article 484 to provisionally recognize excluded heirs as pro indiviso co-owners in an accion publiciana and dismissed a fellow co-owner's ejectment complaint.

## Suggested Answers

### Hypothetical 1: House Built on Another's Lot vs. Co-Owner's Exclusive Possession

**Problem:** Clara buys a 250-square-meter residential lot in her sole name under TCT No. 100. With Clara's permission, her friend Ben spends Php2,000,000.00 constructing a two-story house on the lot, exceeding the lot's Php800,000.00 value. Ben later sues Clara for judicial partition claiming co-ownership under Article 484 because his house is worth more than the land. Meanwhile, in a separate case, siblings Mario and Luis inherit an undivided fishpond from their father. Mario occupies the entire fishpond exclusively against Luis's objection. Luis sues Mario to eject him and recover the northern physical half before any partition. Resolve both claims.

**Suggested Answer:** Ben's partition suit should be dismissed because no co-ownership exists under Article 484 of the Civil Code. As held in [*Victoria v. Pidlaoan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61799), mere construction of a house on another person's land does not create co-ownership regardless of the house's value. A house and a lot are separately identifiable properties that can belong to different owners. Unless Ben proves he contributed to purchasing the lot, his remedies lie under [Article 448](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0448.md) rather than partition. In the second dispute, Luis cannot eject Mario or recover the northern physical half prior to partition. As held in [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) and [*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492), every co-owner under Article 484 owns an undivided abstract share and enjoys an equal right to possess the whole property until partition. The court may only recognize the co-ownership, affirm Luis's equal right of possession over the entire fishpond and order Mario to pay reasonable rent for non-tolerated exclusive use, leaving physical division to an action for partition.

### Hypothetical 2: Legal Redemption After Concrete Subdivision Under a Single Title

**Problem:** Three siblings (Ana, Bert and Carl) owned a 600-square-meter lot covered by a single TCT. In 1990, they signed a notarized partition agreement assigning the western 200 square meters to Ana, the middle 200 square meters to Bert and the eastern 200 square meters to Carl. Each separately declared his or her lot for taxation and took exclusive possession. They never cancelled the single TCT. In 2020, Carl sold his eastern 200-square-meter portion to a stranger, Dante, without giving Ana written notice. Ana sues Dante to redeem Carl's portion as a co-owner under [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md) and [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md), arguing that the single TCT proves co-ownership still exists. Will Ana's action prosper?

**Suggested Answer:** No, Ana's action for legal redemption will not prosper. Under Article 484 of the Civil Code, co-ownership exists only when ownership of an undivided thing belongs to different persons. As consistently held in [*Salatandol v. Retes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/jun1988/gr_l-38120_1988.html), [*Hernandez v. Quitain*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/nov1988/gr_l-48457_1988.html), [*Spouses Si v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37419) and [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649), there is no co-ownership once the portions owned by different people are concretely determined and separately identifiable, even if not yet technically described or still embraced in one certificate of title. Because the 1990 partition segregated each sibling's 200-square-meter portion with separate tax declarations and exclusive possession, co-ownership terminated long before the 2020 sale. Without a subsisting co-ownership under Article 484, the right of legal redemption under [Article 1620](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1620.md) and [Article 1623](../book-4-title-06-sales/art-1623.md) no longer exists.

### Hypothetical 3: Governing-Law Hierarchy Under Paragraph 2 of Article 484

**Problem:** Ramon, a validly married man, cohabited with Elena from 1985 to 1995. In 1990, Elena bought a condominium unit in her sole name using her own salary and business profits. After they separated, Ramon sued Elena for partition invoking Article 484 of the Civil Code and claiming co-ownership without offering any proof of financial contribution. Separately, married spouses Leo and Maya operate a conjugal trucking business. Maya alone files a replevin complaint to recover a leased truck detained by a lessee who moves to dismiss for failure to join Leo as an indispensable party. Resolve both cases under Article 484 paragraph 2.

**Suggested Answer:** Ramon's partition complaint must be dismissed, while Maya's replevin complaint should be allowed to proceed. Under the second paragraph of Article 484, Title III governs co-ownership only in default of contracts or special provisions. As held in [*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293), Article 148 of the Family Code applies retroactively as a special provision to adulterous cohabitation and limits co-ownership solely to properties acquired through proven actual joint contribution of money, property or industry. Because Ramon proved no contribution while Elena proved sole acquisition, no co-ownership exists. Conversely, as held in [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822), conjugal spouses are co-owners of specific partnership property under Article 108 of the Family Code and [Article 1811](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md) of the Civil Code. In default of conflicting special provisions, Article 484 paragraph 2 brings [Article 487](art-0487.md) into operation so Maya alone may file replevin to recover the conjugal vehicle, with Leo joined only as a pro-forma party.

# Quotes

> "As described under Article 484 of the Civil Code, there is co-ownership whenever the ownership of an undivided thing or right belongs to different persons."[^41]

> "The foregoing provisions confirm the co-owners to have a pro indiviso, pro rata, pari passu right in the co-ownership. In other words, a co-owner's right is proportional to his or her share or interest in the undivided co-owned property that is on equal footing with the other co-owners. Such being the nature of a co-owner's right, petitioners have no right to possess the subject property better than that of respondent George."[^42]

> "We hold that mere construction of a house on another's land does not create a co-ownership. Article 484 of the Civil Code provides that co-ownership exists when the ownership of an undivided thing or right belongs to different persons. Verily, a house and a lot are separately identifiable properties and can pertain to different owners, as in this case: the house belongs to Rosario and the lot to Elma."[^43]

> "Article 484 of the New Civil Code provides that there is co-ownership whenever the ownership of an undivided thing or right belongs to different persons. Thus, on the one hand, a co-owner of an undivided parcel of land is an owner of the whole, and over the whole he exercises the right of dominion, but he is at the same time the owner of a portion which is truly abstract. On the other hand, there is no co-ownership when the different portions owned by different people are already concretely determined and separately identifiable, even if not yet technically described."[^44]

> "Under this provision, Glenn and Karen Go are effectively co-owners of Kargo Enterprises and the properties registered under this name; hence, both have an equal right to seek possession of these properties. Applying Article 484 of the Civil Code, which states that "in default of contracts, or special provisions, co-ownership shall be governed by the provisions of this Title," we find further support in Article 487 of the Civil Code that allows any of the co-owners to bring an action in ejectment with respect to the co-owned property."[^45]

> "Any co-owner may file an action under Article 487 not only against a third person, but also against another co-owner who takes exclusive possession and asserts exclusive ownership of the property. In the latter case, however, the only purpose of the action is to obtain recognition of the co-ownership. The plaintiff cannot seek exclusion of the defendant from the property because as co-owner he has a right of possession. The plaintiff cannot recover any material or determinate part of the property."[^46]

> "The basis and origin of the right of legal redemption granted under Article 1620 of the Civil Code, and relied upon by the trial court, is the existence of a co-ownership as defined under Article 484 of the Civil Code."[^47]

> "As expressed in Article 484 of the Civil Code, a co-ownership exists whenever the ownership of an undivided thing or right belongs to different persons. Under such concept, a co-owner cannot point to a particular portion of the property owned in common as his own, because his portion thereof is intangible rather than identifiable. Here, the portion of Eufemia Omole as well as those of the plaintiffs had been identified and localized, so that co-ownership, in its real sense, no longer exists."[^48]

# Tips

## Diagnostic Checklist for Article 484 Issues

1. **Check whether ownership is still undivided.** Look for deeds of partition, compromise subdivision plans, separate tax declarations and exclusive localized possession before assuming co-ownership subsists.
2. **Do not rely on a single Torrens title as conclusive proof of co-ownership.** A single OCT or TCT does not preserve co-ownership once portions are concretely determined and separately identifiable.
3. **Identify whether a special statutory regime displaces Title III.** Check for Family Code Article 148 in adulterous or bigamous cohabitation, or Title IV succession rules (including collation) when heirs divide a decedent's estate.
4. **Separate improvements from land ownership.** Building a house on another person's land does not make the builder a co-owner of the lot. Evaluate remedies under Article 448 instead.
5. **Gather affirmative proof of co-ownership before filing partition.** Tax payments, utility bills and administrative roles are insufficient against exclusive deeds of sale in another person's name.
6. **Match the remedy to the co-ownership stage.** Before partition, an ejectment or recovery suit against a fellow co-owner yields only recognition of co-ownership, equal possession and rent if exclusive use was not silently tolerated. File partition to obtain a concrete physical share.

> [!TIP]
> When defending an accion publiciana filed by a co-heir who registered an estate property solely in his or her name, raise co-ownership squarely as an affirmative defense. Trial courts must resolve ownership provisionally to determine better right of possession without treating the defense as a collateral attack on the Torrens title, as held in [*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492).

## Common Traps

- **Filing legal redemption after actual or extrajudicial partition:** Legal redemption under Articles 1620 and 1623 dies once co-ownership terminates through concrete segregation of shares ([*Salatandol v. Retes*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/jun1988/gr_l-38120_1988.html), [*Hernandez v. Quitain*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/nov1988/gr_l-48457_1988.html), [*Spouses Si v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37419)).
- **Demanding physical eviction of a fellow co-owner or delivery of a specific half before partition:** A co-owner holds an abstract share over the whole and cannot exclude another co-owner or claim a determinate portion until partition ([*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152), [*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492)).
- **Invoking general Article 484 co-ownership in an adulterous relationship:** Article 148 of the Family Code overrides general co-ownership and demands strict proof of actual joint contribution ([*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293)).
- **Filing a new partition suit after an approved compromise subdivision plan:** Once a compromise judgment segregates specific portions, co-ownership ceases and res judicata bars a second partition complaint. Register the compromise judgment under Section 81 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 instead ([*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649)).
- **Seeking partial summary partition of a single inherited lot without collation:** When heirs divide an estate property, the court must apply succession rules and collate other estate properties if alleged ([*Heirs of Ernesto Morales v. Agustin*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64260)).
- **Assuming a co-owner who builds on common land automatically invokes Article 448:** Co-owners building on co-owned land are governed by Articles 484 to 501 rather than Article 448 ([*Spouses Belvis v. Spouses Erola*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/65574)).

## What Worked and What Did Not Work

- **What worked:** Raising hereditary co-ownership provisionally to defeat a fellow co-owner's ejectment suit ([*Spouses Bangug v. Dela Cruz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68492)); proving prior concrete segregation via partition deeds, compromise subdivision plans or separate tax declarations to defeat legal redemption or a second partition suit ([*Hernandez v. Quitain*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/nov1988/gr_l-48457_1988.html), [*Spouses Si v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/37419), [*Rizal v. Naredo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26649)); invoking Article 484 paragraph 2 and Article 487 to sustain a sole spouse's replevin action for conjugal vehicles ([*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822)); and claiming reasonable rent from a co-owner whose exclusive possession was opposed ([*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152)).
- **What did not work:** Claiming co-ownership over a lot solely because one built a valuable house on it ([*Victoria v. Pidlaoan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/61799)); relying on tax payments, administrative capacity or an unproven custom to prove co-ownership ([*Co Giok Lun v. Co*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/26015)); claiming automatic co-ownership in an adulterous union without proof of actual contribution ([*Atienza v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42293)); and approving a unilateral falsified subdivision agreement without all co-owners' consent ([*Quintos v. Nicolas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57149)).

> [!NOTE]
> Even where a co-owner's earlier partition suit was dismissed for failure to prosecute without specifying that dismissal was without prejudice, partition remains available under Article 494 so long as the co-ownership under Article 484 has not yet been terminated by a final allocation of shares, as held in [*Quintos v. Nicolas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57149).
