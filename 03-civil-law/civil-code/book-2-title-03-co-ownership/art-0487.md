> ART. 487. Any one of the co-owners may bring an action in ejectment. (n)[^civil-code]

# Explanation

Article 487 of the Civil Code allows any single co-owner to file an action to recover possession of co-owned property without joining the other co-owners as co-plaintiffs. The suit is presumed to be instituted for the benefit of all co-owners.

**In brief:**
1. **Who may sue and what actions are covered:** Any co-owner may file forcible entry, unlawful detainer, accion publiciana, accion reivindicatoria or related recovery suits, including actions to recover co-owned personal property or conjugal credits.
2. **Effect of non-joinder and judgment:** Only the suing co-owner is an indispensable party. The other co-owners are neither indispensable nor necessary parties. A favorable judgment benefits all co-owners while an adverse judgment cannot prejudice unimpleaded co-owners.
3. **Limits and exceptions:** The rule does not apply when the suing co-owner repudiates the co-ownership and claims sole ownership for himself or herself alone, when a shareholder sues over corporate property or when a third party sues co-owners to establish tenancy over undivided land.
4. **Suits between co-owners:** Under the 2022 re-examination in [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), a co-owner who peacefully possesses a portion while recognizing the co-ownership cannot be ejected, but a co-owner who forcibly excludes another co-owner in prior possession may be evicted through forcible entry.[^2]

This article examines the scope of covered actions, the procedural rules on parties and pleadings, the exceptions to unilateral standing, the evolution of ejectment suits between co-owners, the relationship with judicial partition and the historical shift from pre-Code doctrine.

## Scope of Actions Covered

Although Article 487 uses the phrase action in ejectment, the Supreme Court has consistently interpreted the provision to cover all kinds of actions for the recovery of possession of co-owned property.

In [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152), the Supreme Court held that Article 487 embraces forcible entry and unlawful detainer (accion interdictal), recovery of the right to possess (accion publiciana) and recovery of ownership (accion de reivindicacion).[^3] The Supreme Court reiterated this broad coverage in [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411),[^4] [*Adlawan v. Adlawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40780),[^5] [*Wee v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46541),[^6] [*Estreller v. Ysmael*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48937),[^7] [*Plasabas v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49026),[^8] [*Marmo v. Anacay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50781),[^9] [*Heirs of Albina G. Ampil v. Manahan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55228),[^10] [*Ulay v. Bustamante*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67349)[^11] and [*De Vera v. Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935).[^12]

In [*Plasabas v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49026), the Court of Appeals had ruled that Article 487 could not apply to a complaint for recovery of title. The Supreme Court reversed that view and held that there is no need to determine whether a complaint is for ejectment or for recovery of title because Article 487 applies to both.[^13] Similarly, in [*Marmo v. Anacay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50781), the Supreme Court applied Article 487 to an action for annulment of a falsified deed of sale and recovery of title.[^14] In [*Clemente v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64997), quoting [*Catedrilla v. Lauron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55809) on Article 487 and applying [Article 493](art-0493.md), the Supreme Court allowed a single co-heir to prosecute an action for revocation of donation, reconveyance and recovery of possession for all co-heirs pro-indiviso.[^15]

### Extension to Co-Owned Personal Property and Credits

The Supreme Court has also extended Article 487 beyond real property to personal property and credits held in common by spouses under the conjugal partnership of gains.

In [*Carandang v. Heirs of De Guzman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42075), the Supreme Court read [Article 108 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-108.md) together with [Article 1811 of the Civil Code](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md) to treat spouses as co-owners of a conjugal credit. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that the husband could sue alone to recover money loaned from conjugal funds without impleading his wife.[^16]

In [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822), the Supreme Court applied [Article 484](art-0484.md), Article 487 and [Article 1811](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md) of the Civil Code alongside [Article 108](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-108.md) and [Article 124](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-124.md) of the Family Code to a replevin complaint filed by a wife to recover leased motor vehicles. The Supreme Court expressly acknowledged that ejectment is normally associated with real property, yet applied Article 487 to recovery of conjugal personal property so long as the acting spouse does not dispose of or encumber the property without the other spouse's consent.[^17]

## Who May Sue Alone and Procedural Consequences

When a co-owner files a recovery suit without repudiating the co-ownership, the law presumes that the action is brought for the benefit of all co-owners.

### Indispensable Parties and Effect of Judgment

In [*Carandang v. Heirs of De Guzman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42075), the Supreme Court synthesized the party-status rule under Article 487. All co-owners are real parties in interest in suits to recover co-owned properties, yet only the co-owner who filed the suit is an indispensable party. The other co-owners are neither indispensable nor necessary parties because complete relief can be afforded without their participation.[^18]

The Supreme Court has repeatedly defined the effect of judgment on unimpleaded co-owners. In [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411),[^19] [*Resuena v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42842)[^20] and [*Plasabas v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49026),[^21] the Supreme Court held that a favorable judgment inures to the benefit of all co-owners while an adverse judgment cannot prejudice the rights of the unimpleaded co-owners.

### Special Powers of Attorney, Representatives and Forum Shopping Certifications

Because Article 487 vests independent standing in every co-owner, defendants cannot defeat an ejectment complaint by challenging the absence of written authorization from non-suing co-owners.

- **No SPA required from other co-owners:** In [*Wee v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46541), the Supreme Court held that Special Powers of Attorney executed by non-suing co-owners were mere surplusage whose absence or late attachment did not affect the validity of the ejectment suit or the co-owner's verification and certification of non-forum shopping.[^22] In [*Heirs of Albina G. Ampil v. Manahan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55228), the Supreme Court likewise held that a co-owner may bring an action for unlawful detainer even without a Special Power of Attorney from his co-heirs when the complaint states that the property is held in common for all heirs.[^23]
- **Authority of a co-owner's attorney-in-fact:** In [*Spouses Mendoza v. Coronel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40583), the Supreme Court ruled that an attorney-in-fact needs authority only from the co-owner instituting the ejectment suit and not from all co-owners. Moreover, the attorney-in-fact who files the complaint pursuant to a Special Power of Attorney may validly sign the certification against forum shopping because Section 1, Rule 70 of the Rules of Court authorizes an owner's representative to institute ejectment proceedings.[^24]
- **Lone co-owner signature on verification and certification:** In [*Fernandez v. Villegas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57378), the Supreme Court held that because Article 487 permits any co-owner to bring ejectment for all, co-owner sisters who sued jointly shared a commonality of interest and cause of action. Consequently, the lone signature of one co-owner on the verification and certification against forum shopping in a Rule 42 petition substantially complied with Rule 7, Section 5 of the Rules of Court.[^25]

### Qualified Co-Owners and Standing in Particular Contexts

The Supreme Court has recognized Article 487 standing across varied forms of co-ownership:

- **Heirs in actual possession or before estate settlement:** In [*Vencilao v. Camarenta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1969/sep1969/gr_l-24308_1969.html), an En Banc decision, the Supreme Court held that a co-heir in actual prior physical possession could file forcible entry alone.[^26] In [*Esteban v. Spouses Marcelo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/56101), the Supreme Court upheld a Rule 45 petition prosecuted by one substituted compulsory heir without impleading the other compulsory heirs.[^27] In [*Clemente v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64997), quoting [*Catedrilla v. Lauron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55809) on Article 487 and applying [Article 493](art-0493.md), the Supreme Court held that an heir may sue for reconveyance even before settlement of the decedent's estate so long as the heir recognizes the other co-heirs as co-owners.[^28]
- **Surviving spouse suing for an estate:** In [*Soriente v. Estate of Concepcion*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50899), the Supreme Court held that even assuming the surviving spouse failed to submit documentary proof of her representative capacity for her late husband's estate, she was capacitated under Article 487 to prosecute the unlawful detainer suit as a real party-in-interest because she was a co-owner of the titled lot.[^29]
- **Buyer of undivided pro-indiviso shares:** In [*Ulay v. Bustamante*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67349), a buyer who validly purchased the undivided shares of four out of eight co-heirs became a co-owner of the lot and could recover possession from a non-co-owner occupant for the benefit of all co-owners under Article 487.[^30]
- **Co-owner faction over unsegregated public land:** In [*Church of Christ, Dadiangas Congregation, Inc. v. Vallespin*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/aug1988/gr_l-43726_1988.html), two church factions allocated unsegregated halves of public land by a Board of Liquidators resolution were co-owners pending subdivision, making one faction a proper party to file a pure ejectment suit under Article 487.[^31]
- **Named co-owner with a beneficial buyer:** In [*Estreller v. Ysmael*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48937), one registered co-owner under a Torrens title and a buyer whose sale was not annotated on the title were both real parties in interest to file accion publiciana against occupants by tolerance.[^32]

### Stockholders Are Not Co-Owners of Corporate Property

Article 487 requires an actual legal co-ownership over the property in dispute. In [*Tumagan v. Kairuz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64593), the Court of Appeals had invoked Article 487 to allow a stockholder whose family held thirty percent of a corporation's shares to file forcible entry against corporate employees over land already titled to the corporation. The Supreme Court reversed that ruling. It held that shareholders are in no legal sense owners or co-owners of corporate property because the corporation owns the property as a distinct legal person. A shareholder holds only an inchoate expectancy in corporate management, profits and assets upon dissolution.[^33]

## Limits and Exceptions to Unilateral Standing

Article 487 does not dispense with compulsory joinder under Section 7, Rule 3 of the Rules of Court in every dispute involving co-owned property.

### Repudiation and Suits for the Plaintiff Alone

The presumption that a co-owner sues for all co-owners disappears when the plaintiff claims exclusive ownership to the exclusion of the others.

In [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411), a co-heir sued alone to quiet title, recover possession and be declared sole absolute owner without impleading his six siblings or the Republic of the Philippines. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that if the action is for the benefit of the plaintiff alone who claims to be the sole owner, the action will not prosper unless the other co-owners are impleaded as indispensable parties. Their absence rendered all subsequent trial court proceedings and judgment ineffective for want of authority to act.[^34]

In [*Adlawan v. Adlawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40780), an acknowledged illegitimate son executed an affidavit of self-adjudication and sued his late father's siblings for unlawful detainer as sole heir and absolute owner, ignoring the co-ownership of his father's surviving legal wife and her heirs (or the State in default of heirs). Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of the complaint because the action operated as a complete repudiation of the co-ownership and sought possession and damages solely for the plaintiff.[^35]

In [*Marmo v. Anacay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50781), the Supreme Court restated the dividing line. Where a co-owner sues without repudiating co-ownership, the suit proceeds without impleading the other co-owners. Where the co-owner repudiates the co-ownership by claiming sole ownership, the other co-owners become indispensable parties who must be impleaded.[^36]

### Initial Pleading Allegation Cured at Trial and Non-Joinder Remedy

An erroneous claim of sole ownership in an initial complaint is not automatically fatal if the plaintiff later acknowledges the co-ownership at trial. In [*Plasabas v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49026), the plaintiffs alleged sole ownership in their 1974 complaint for recovery of title, yet acknowledged during trial that the land was co-owned with siblings who authorized them to pursue the case. The Supreme Court held that the complaint allegation was immaterial in view of the trial acknowledgement. Furthermore, the Supreme Court ruled that non-joinder of indispensable parties is not a ground for outright dismissal of an action. The proper remedy is for the court to order the plaintiff to implead the omitted party. Dismissal is proper only if the plaintiff refuses to comply with that order.[^37] The Supreme Court reiterated that non-joinder is not a ground for dismissal in [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822).[^38]

### Suits Against Co-Owners and Omission of Other Indispensable Parties

Article 487 applies to suits brought by a co-owner to protect common rights. Different rules govern actions brought against co-owners or actions whose relief requires additional indispensable parties.

- **Third-party suits against co-owners:** In [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264), a fishpond caretaker sued only three of six pro-indiviso co-owners to establish tenancy and security of tenure, omitting three co-owners residing abroad. Contrasting ejectment suits brought by a co-owner, the Supreme Court held that a tenant suing to establish tenancy over undivided land must implead all pro-indiviso co-owners as indispensable party-defendants. Failure to implead them deprived the trial court of jurisdiction and rendered the final judgment null and void, subject to an independent action for annulment.[^39]
- **Actions for quieting of title and nullity of documents omitting a co-owner:** In [*Bacalso v. Padigos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45228), respondents filed an action for quieting of title, declaration of nullity of documents, recovery of possession and ownership and damages while omitting a co-owner heir. Respondents invoked Article 487 to defend the omission. The Supreme Court rejected that contention, citing [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) and holding that in that action the absence of an indispensable party rendered all subsequent court actions null and void.[^40]
- **State or registered corporate owner as indispensable party:** In [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411), the Republic of the Philippines was an indispensable party-defendant because the complaint sought nullification of a Free Patent and Original Certificate of Title.[^41] In [*Tumagan v. Kairuz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64593), the registered corporate owner was an indispensable party in a forcible entry suit against its employees, rendering the appellate judgment rendered without it null and void.[^42]
- **Intertwined ownership dispute after ejectment becomes moot:** In [*Church of Christ, Dadiangas Congregation, Inc. v. Vallespin*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1988/aug1988/gr_l-43726_1988.html), once the occupants vacated the pastoral house, restoration of possession became moot and only ownership of the building remained. While one co-owner suffices in pure ejectment, the rival co-owner church faction was an indispensable party as to ownership of the building, so the Supreme Court reinstated the City Court judgment with the ruling on lot ownership eliminated without prejudice.[^43]

## Ejectment Suits Between Co-Owners: Evolution from [*De Guia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) to [*Mabalo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602)

A major doctrinal development under Article 487 concerns when one co-owner may file an ejectment suit against another co-owner.

### The Classical [*De Guia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) Rule and Peaceful Co-Owner Possession

In [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152), the Supreme Court held that a co-owner may file an action under Article 487 not only against a third person but also against another co-owner who takes exclusive possession and asserts exclusive ownership of the common property. However, under [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152), the sole purpose and effect of that action was to obtain recognition of the co-ownership and equal right to possess, use and enjoy the entire property. The plaintiff could neither exclude the defendant co-owner nor recover a material or determinate part prior to partition (*Engreso v. De La Cruz*, as cited in [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152)).[^44]

At the same time, [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) distinguished rent liability among co-owners. If one co-owner occupies a common residential house without opposition and without a lease agreement, the other co-owners cannot demand rent. By contrast, where the co-owners agreed to lease a common fishpond and one co-owner continued in exclusive possession after the lease expired, that co-owner became liable for reasonable rent or compensatory damages corresponding to the other co-owner's undivided share.[^45]

Subsequent decisions applied the non-exclusion rule where a defendant co-owner held possession peacefully in his or her own right:

- In [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519), a common-law partner sought to eject his former partner from an annex structure and sari-sari store presumptively co-owned in equal shares under [Article 147 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-147.md). Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that because neither party proved exclusive ownership and the respondent neither obtained possession by contract nor through intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth, she possessed in her own right as co-owner and could not be ejected.[^46]
- In [*Romero v. Singson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60821), a sister procured a Torrens title through a forged deed of sale signed by her long-deceased parents and sued her occupant siblings for unlawful detainer. Quoting [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519) on Article 487, the Supreme Court held that the forged deed passed no title, the siblings remained co-owners by succession and the plaintiff had no cause of action to eject co-owners who recognized the co-ownership and never claimed exclusive ownership.[^47]

### The 2022 [*Mabalo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602) Re-Examination and Four Governing Rules

In [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), the Supreme Court expressly re-examined the [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) rule barring the ejectment of a co-owner from common property. The Supreme Court identified two defects in an absolute bar against ejecting a co-owner. First, it failed to balance a co-owner's right of possession with the concomitant fiduciary obligation to respect the similar rights of fellow co-owners. Second, it failed to honor the core rationale of ejectment under [Article 536](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0536.md) and [Article 539](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0539.md) of the Civil Code, which forbids anyone from taking the law into his or her own hands and protects prior physical possession and due process.[^48]

Guided by [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) and [Article 536](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0536.md), the Supreme Court defined four governing rules for ejectment suits between co-owners under Article 487:

1. **Peaceful possession recognizing co-ownership:** If a co-owner takes possession of a definite portion of the common property in the exercise of the right to possession as a co-owner, that co-owner may not be ejected as long as he or she recognizes the co-ownership, being regarded as a trustee for the co-ownership.[^49]
2. **Forcible exclusion of a co-owner in prior possession:** If a co-owner takes exclusive possession of a specific portion resulting in the exclusion or deprivation of another co-owner in prior possession, any co-owner may file an action for ejectment to evict the co-owner who wrested possession by force.[^50]
3. **Plaintiff's burden of proof:** To evict a co-owner from the common property, the plaintiff co-owner bears the burden to prove that the defendant co-owner employed force, intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth when taking possession.[^51]
4. **Effect of failure of proof:** If the plaintiff fails to meet that burden, the plaintiff co-owner can neither exclude the defendant co-owner nor recover a determinate part of the property because the defendant is considered to have entered as a co-owner and trustee of the co-ownership.[^52]

Under this framework, the decisive issue is not the bare existence of the defendant's right as co-owner, but the manner in which the defendant exercised that right. Because petitioner Perlita Mabalo bought an undivided share from one heir and then fenced a portion occupied by her fellow co-owners, posted a no-trespassing sign and demolished two houses, her exclusion of prior possessors implied force and justified her eviction in a forcible entry suit filed within one year. However, the Supreme Court deleted the rent award because no supporting evidence established fair rental value and it would be absurd to require a co-owner to pay rent on property she is entitled to enjoy as a co-owner.[^53]

## Relationship with Judicial Partition

A recurring defense in Article 487 litigation is that the plaintiff co-owner should first file an action for judicial partition under Rule 69 of the Rules of Court. The Supreme Court has squarely rejected that defense.

In [*De Vera v. Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935), the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals dismissed an accion reivindicatoria filed by co-heirs against alleged forcible entrants, holding that judicial partition was the proper remedy to determine whether the defendants had validly acquired the mother's undivided share. Reversing, the Supreme Court held that under Article 487 a co-owner's right to bring any action for recovery of possession is not subject to the requirement that partition be effected first. An action to recover possession and an action for judicial partition serve different purposes. Neither is a prerequisite for the other and neither precludes the other.[^54]

The Supreme Court highlighted two practical consequences in [*De Vera v. Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935). First, the court hearing an action to recover possession may rule on the existence or non-existence of co-ownership between the parties, even though it lacks power to physically partition the property. Second, under [Article 486](art-0486.md) and [Article 494](art-0494.md), co-owners are not required to divide common property before enjoying it and may validly choose to keep the property undivided, so a court cannot force them to sue for partition instead of recovering possession.[^55]

By contrast, once co-ownership is recognized between two co-owners and they wish to physically segregate determinate portions or conduct an accounting of profits, judicial or extrajudicial partition is the proper recourse, as held in [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152).[^56]

## Departure from Pre-Code Law and the [*Arcelona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) Errata

Marked as a new provision in Republic Act No. 386, Article 487 departed from the earlier rule laid down in *Palarca v. Baguisi* (as cited in [*Resuena v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42842),[^57] [*Spouses Mendoza v. Coronel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40583)[^58] and [*Ulay v. Bustamante*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67349)[^59]), which had required an action in ejectment to be brought by all co-owners.

A notable printing error in the initial published text of [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) briefly caused confusion among lower courts. The uncorrected text had misstated that Article 487 belonged to the old Civil Code and had been superseded by a requirement to join all co-owners.[^60] In [*Spouses Mendoza v. Coronel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40583), the Supreme Court documented that an official SCRA errata had deleted that erroneous statement and replaced it to clarify that in the past a co-owner could not maintain ejectment without joining all co-owners, whereas Article 487 of the Civil Code now expressly authorizes any one of the co-owners to bring an action in ejectment.[^61]

## Case Discussions

### Re-Examination of Co-Owner Ejectment for Forcible Ouster

When Roman Babuyo died, his nine children took physical possession of his undivided 5,599-square-meter parcel under Original Certificate of Title No. P-10402 in Lagonglong, Misamis Oriental. On June 2, 2014, Perlita Mabalo bought a 364-square-meter portion from Segundina Babuyo Fernandez, daughter of another heir Rufino Babuyo. The next day, Mabalo stopped respondents' laborers from trimming tree branches, fenced the portion with a no-trespassing sign and demolished two houses. Respondents filed forcible entry on July 10, 2014. The 4th Municipal Circuit Trial Court of Balingasag-Lagonglong ordered Mabalo to vacate, remove improvements and pay P300.00 monthly rent from June 3, 2014 plus P5,000.00 attorney's fees and costs. Branch 20 of the Regional Trial Court of Cagayan de Oro City and the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 07450-MIN affirmed. Re-examining [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152), the Supreme Court applied Article 487 and [Article 536](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0536.md) to formulate four governing rules allowing ejectment of a co-owner who forcibly excludes a co-owner in prior possession upon proof of force, intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' March 15, 2018 Decision with modification by ordering Mabalo and all persons claiming under her to vacate and remove improvements and deleted the awards of rent (for lack of supporting evidence and because ordering a co-owner to pay rent is absurd) and attorney's fees (for lack of justification in the body of the lower court decisions).[^62] The ruling establishes that a co-owner who forcibly excludes fellow co-owners in prior possession may be evicted under Article 487.

### Partition Is Not a Prerequisite to Recovery of Possession

After National Housing Authority awardee Bernardo De Vera Sr. died in 1993, respondent Virgilio Manzanero and twenty men forcibly destroyed the house and took possession of the Las Piñas lot on September 9, 1995 based on an alleged 1994 Affidavit of Waiver by petitioners' mother Emelie. Petitioners paid the remaining National Housing Authority balance in 2013 and filed recovery of possession (accion reivindicatoria) on June 19, 2014. Respondents attached the Affidavit of Waiver to their Answer but failed to formally offer evidence. Branch 275 of the Regional Trial Court of Las Piñas City dismissed the complaint holding that judicial partition was the proper remedy. The Court of Appeals affirmed in CA-G.R. CV No. 106781. Applying Article 487 alongside [Article 486](art-0486.md), [Article 494](art-0494.md) and [Article 434](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0434.md), the Supreme Court held that recovery of possession and judicial partition serve different purposes, neither is a prerequisite for the other and the trial court may rule on the existence of co-ownership within the recovery action itself. Resolving the merits without remand to avoid further delay to indigent petitioners, the Supreme Court found petitioners proved ownership and lot identity while respondents offered no admissible evidence under Section 34, Rule 132. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' March 16, 2017 Decision and June 16, 2017 Resolution and ordered respondents to immediately vacate and return possession to petitioners with no pronouncement as to costs.[^63] The decision confirms that Article 487 recovery suits do not require prior partition.

### Buyer of Undivided Co-Heir Shares May Eject a Non-Co-Owner

Following a 1977 Deed of Extrajudicial Partition of Lot No. 1089 in Carmen, Davao, Gregoria Concha possessed Lot No. 1089-F while her mother Juana possessed Lot No. 1089-E, though an erroneous 1979 survey interchanged their lot designations. On January 16, 2001, four of Gregoria's eight children sold a 9,689-square-meter portion of Lot No. 1089-F to Jesus Ulay. On May 3, 2001, Maranguyod Bustamante, widow of Juana's son Ramon whose interest lay solely in Lot No. 1089-E, entered the lot and built a pre-fabricated house. Jesus filed recovery of possession (Civil Case No. 26-2003) and the Bustamantes filed annulment of deeds (Civil Case No. 37-2005). Branch 4 of the Regional Trial Court of Panabo City voided Jesus's Deed of Sale, dismissed his recovery action and ordered the four sellers to reimburse P60,000.00 with interest. The Court of Appeals affirmed with modification in CA-G.R. CV Nos. 03521-MIN and 03524-MIN. The Supreme Court ruled that the January 16, 2001 Deed of Sale was valid to the extent of the four sellers' pro-indiviso shares, making Jesus a one-half co-owner alongside the four non-selling heirs with one-eighth each. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that Jesus could recover the lot for all co-owners without joining them and that Maranguyod was a builder in bad faith under [Article 449](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0449.md) without right to reimbursement. The Supreme Court partly granted the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' March 31, 2016 Decision and October 25, 2016 Resolution with modification, invalidated the 1999 Deed of Exchange and 2004 Affidavit of Waiver, directed annotation of the five co-owners' shares on Original Certificate of Title No. P-41507, ordered Maranguyod to vacate and turn over the premises to the co-owners and ordered the four Concha co-owners to pay Jesus their proportionate shares of registration fees and related expenses.[^64] The case illustrates that a transferee of undivided shares acquires co-owner standing under Article 487.

### Co-Heir May Sue for Revocation and Reconveyance Before Estate Settlement

In 1963, four Clemente siblings donated a one-hectare Quezon lot to the Republic solely for constructing a government hospital. Only a foundation was built and in 2003 the Department of Public Works and Highways confirmed there were no plans or budget to build a hospital there. In 2004, heir Socorro Clemente alone sued for revocation of donation, reconveyance and recovery of possession in favor of all heirs pro-indiviso. Branch 64 of the Regional Trial Court of Mauban, Quezon dismissed the case as premature under Article 1197. The Court of Appeals affirmed in CA-G.R. CV No. 91522. Applying [Article 493](art-0493.md) while quoting [*Catedrilla v. Lauron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55809) (which quoted [*Wee v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46541) and [*Carandang v. Heirs of De Guzman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42075) on Article 487), the Supreme Court held that a co-heir who acknowledges the other co-heirs as co-owners may sue alone for reconveyance even before settlement of the estate because the suit benefits all co-owners. Finding rescission proper under Article 1191 without laches after more than fifty years of non-compliance, the Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' October 17, 2014 Decision and August 14, 2015 Resolution and ordered Branch 64 of the Regional Trial Court of Mauban, Quezon to cause cancellation of Transfer Certificate of Title No. T-51745 and issuance of a new title in the names of the heirs of the four Clemente siblings.[^65] The decision confirms that estate settlement is not required before one recognizing co-heir sues to recover common property.

### Shareholders Are Not Co-Owners of Corporate Property

Respondent Mariam Kairuz filed forcible entry before the 5th Municipal Circuit Trial Court of Tuba-Sablan, Benguet against the branch manager and geodetic engineers of Bali Irisan Resources, Inc. (BIRI) after BIRI padlocked a 5.2-hectare spring property already sold and titled in BIRI's name. The Kairuz family owned thirty percent of BIRI's stock. Both the Municipal Circuit Trial Court and Branch 10 of the Regional Trial Court of La Trinidad, Benguet dismissed the complaint for failure to implead BIRI as an indispensable party. The Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. SP No. 112613, quoting the rule under Article 487 to treat Mariam as a co-owner based on the family's thirty percent shareholding and ordering petitioners to allow her entry and pay P25,000.00 attorney's fees. Reversing the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court held that BIRI as registered owner was an indispensable party whose absence rendered the appellate ruling void for lack of jurisdiction, that shareholders are not co-owners of corporate property owned by the corporation as a distinct legal person and that the true dispute was an intra-corporate controversy outside Municipal Circuit Trial Court jurisdiction. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' December 21, 2010 Decision and July 22, 2011 Resolution and dismissed Civil Case No. 272 for lack of jurisdiction.[^66] The ruling establishes that corporate stockholders cannot invoke co-ownership standing over corporate realty.

### Co-Owner Holding Void Title Cannot Eject Recognizing Co-Owners

Respondent Engracia Singson caused Transfer Certificate of Title No. 12575 to be issued in her name over her deceased parents' 223-square-meter San Juan lot using a June 6, 2006 deed of sale ostensibly signed by her parents who had died in 1981 and 1997. She then filed unlawful detainer in Branch 58 of the Metropolitan Trial Court of San Juan City against her occupant siblings Consolacion, Rosario, Rafael and Ramon. The Metropolitan Trial Court ordered eviction, P2,000.00 monthly rent from August 7, 2006, P10,000.00 attorney's fees and costs. Branch 160 of the Regional Trial Court of Pasig City initially affirmed, then reversed on reconsideration in its December 11, 2009 Order, dismissing the complaint and awarding P8,000.00 attorney's fees to each sibling plus costs. The Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 114363 reversed and reinstated the Regional Trial Court's earlier affirmance. The Supreme Court held that failure to allege compromise efforts under [Article 151 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-05-the-family/art-151.md) was waived, that the forged deed and resulting Torrens title were void and open to attack under Section 16, Rule 70 and that all siblings remained co-owners by succession. Quoting [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519) on Article 487, the Supreme Court ruled that absent allegation or proof that the occupant siblings claimed exclusive ownership, respondent had no cause of action to eject them. Because the siblings shared inseparable co-ownership interests, reversal inured to non-appealing brothers Ramon and Rafael. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' February 29, 2012 Decision and reinstated and affirmed the Regional Trial Court's December 11, 2009 Order.[^67] The case confirms that a co-owner cannot use a forged title to eject fellow co-owners who recognize the co-ownership.

### Lone Co-Owner Signature Satisfies Verification and Forum Shopping Rules

Co-owner sisters Lourdes Fernandez and Cecilia Siapno sued Cecilia's daughter-in-law Norma Villegas and her family for ejectment before Branch 1 of the Municipal Trial Court in Cities of Dagupan City. The trial court ordered eviction, P1,000.00 monthly compensation, P10,000.00 attorney's fees and costs. Branch 40 of the Regional Trial Court of Dagupan City reversed, dismissed the complaint and ordered plaintiffs to pay P50,000.00 attorney's fees. When plaintiffs filed a Rule 42 petition before the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 116143, the appellate court dismissed it because only Lourdes signed the verification and certification against forum shopping. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that because any co-owner may file ejectment without joining the other co-owners and the suit benefits all, the sisters shared a commonality of interest and cause of action. Lourdes's lone signature therefore substantially complied with both verification and certification requirements under Section 5, Rule 7 of the Rules of Court. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' resolutions dismissing the appeal and denying reconsideration and reinstated and remanded the case to the Court of Appeals for immediate disposition.[^68] The decision shows that Article 487 supports substantial compliance when only one co-owner signs the verification and non-forum shopping certification.

### One Substituted Compulsory Heir May Prosecute Ejectment Alone

Gabriel Esteban filed unlawful detainer on December 6, 2005 against lessees Spouses Rodrigo and Carmen Marcelo after they stopped paying rent in March 2001 and ignored an October 31, 2005 demand to pay and vacate. The Metropolitan Trial Court ordered eviction, back rentals, rentals with legal interest, attorney's fees and costs. Branch 211 of the Regional Trial Court of Mandaluyong City affirmed. The Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 112609 reversed on the grounds that more than one year had passed since 2001 and that Presidential Decree No. 1517 and Republic Act No. 7279 protected respondents. When petitioner Mark Anthony Esteban substituted his late father on appeal, respondents objected that the other compulsory heirs were not impleaded. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court rejected that objection, holding that only the suing co-owner is an indispensable party while other co-owners are neither indispensable nor necessary parties. It also held that the one-year unlawful detainer period ran from the last demand, Presidential Decree No. 1517 was inapplicable and Republic Act No. 7279 could not be raised for the first time on appeal. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' January 17, 2011 Decision and July 15, 2011 Resolution, reinstated the Regional Trial Court's January 13, 2010 Decision and imposed costs against respondents.[^69] The ruling confirms that a single substituted co-heir may pursue an ejectment appeal under Article 487.

### Recognizing Co-Owner May File Unlawful Detainer Without Impleading Co-Heirs

Rey Castigador Catedrilla filed unlawful detainer alone in the Municipal Trial Court of Lambunao, Iloilo against Spouses Mario and Margie Lauron, who occupied Lot No. 5 by tolerance of his deceased mother Lilia's heirs. The Municipal Trial Court ordered eviction, P20,000.00 attorney's fees, P1,000.00 per court appearance, P1,000.00 yearly compensation from demand and litigation costs. Branch 26 of the Regional Trial Court of Iloilo City affirmed except deleting the P20,000.00 attorney's fees. The Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 00939 reversed, ruling that petitioner's co-heirs and Margie's sister Mildred Kascher were indispensable parties. Quoting [*Wee v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46541) and [*Carandang v. Heirs of De Guzman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42075) on Article 487, the Supreme Court reversed. Because petitioner alleged in his complaint that he was one of Lilia's heirs and did not claim exclusive ownership, he could sue alone for the benefit of all co-owners. The Supreme Court also held that Mildred was not an indispensable party, respondents' non-compliance with a barangay settlement allowed petitioner to regard it as rescinded under [Article 2041](../book-4-title-14-compromises-and-arbitrations/art-2041.md) and an unauthorized receipt did not prove a perfected contract to sell. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' February 28, 2007 Decision and July 11, 2007 Resolution and reinstated the Regional Trial Court's March 22, 2005 Order.[^70] The decision reinforces that acknowledging co-ownership in the complaint dispenses with joining co-heirs.

### Co-Owner Needs No Special Power of Attorney from Co-Heirs

Exequiel Ampil, representing the heirs of Albina Ampil, filed unlawful detainer in the Municipal Trial Court of Paombong, Bulacan to recover two lots occupied by tolerance by the Manahans. The Municipal Trial Court ordered surrender of the lots, P2,000.00 monthly rental from July 11, 2003 and P20,000.00 attorney's fees, which Branch 16 of the Regional Trial Court of Malolos, Bulacan affirmed in toto. The Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. SP No. 91568. Before the Supreme Court, respondents argued that Exequiel's Special Power of Attorney from his co-heirs was defective. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court ruled that because the complaint stated the property was held in common and the suit was brought for all heirs, Exequiel as a co-owner could institute unlawful detainer even without a Special Power of Attorney from his co-heirs. On the merits, petitioners' tax declarations, tax payment certification, survey plan, Original Certificate of Title No. P-13627 and Perfecto Manahan's prior sworn admission proved ownership and permissive occupancy over respondents' bare assertions. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' July 11, 2006 Decision and December 13, 2006 Resolution and reinstated the Municipal Trial Court's February 23, 2004 Decision as affirmed in toto by the Regional Trial Court.[^71] The ruling confirms that a defective or absent Special Power of Attorney from co-heirs does not bar a co-owner's Article 487 suit.

### Co-Owner Children Not Indispensable in Annulment of Falsified Sale

Widower Moises Anacay filed a complaint in Branch 90 of the Regional Trial Court of Dasmariñas, Cavite for annulment of a purportedly falsified Deed of Absolute Sale and recovery of title (or payment of the P347,000.00 balance) over a 50-square-meter house and lot co-owned with his late wife Gloria, without impleading his children. The Regional Trial Court denied petitioners' motion to dismiss for non-joinder of indispensable parties. The Court of Appeals dismissed their Rule 65 certiorari petition in CA-G.R. SP No. 94673. Applying Article 487 and synthesizing prior jurisprudence, the Supreme Court held that where a co-owner sues without repudiating the co-ownership, the other co-owners are neither indispensable nor necessary parties. Here, respondent recognized himself as a bona-fide co-owner and the falsification issue concerned only his and his wife's signatures. The Supreme Court distinguished [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264), *Orbeta v. Sendiong* and *Galicia v. Manliquez Vda. de Mindo* (the latter two as cited in [*Marmo v. Anacay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50781)) as involving suits against co-owners or claims of sole ownership affecting third parties. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' December 28, 2007 Decision and April 11, 2008 Resolution and imposed costs against petitioners.[^72] The case synthesizes the distinction between suits for all co-owners and suits repudiating co-ownership or filed against co-owners.

### Replevin of Conjugal Motor Vehicles by One Spouse

Karen Go, doing business as Kargo Enterprises, filed two complaints for replevin and sum of money in Branch 37 of the Regional Trial Court of Cagayan de Oro City to recover leased motor vehicles from Roger Navarro without impleading her husband Glenn Go. Both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 64701 rejected Navarro's motion to dismiss. Reading [Article 108 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-108.md) with [Article 1811](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md) and [Article 484](art-0484.md) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court held that Glenn and Karen were co-owners of the business and vehicles. Applying Article 487 in line with [*Carandang v. Heirs of De Guzman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42075) and [Article 124 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-124.md), the Supreme Court held that either spouse may bring a replevin action to recover conjugal personal property without joining the other as an indispensable party, needing only to implead the spouse as a pro-forma party. It added that non-joinder is not a ground for dismissal and prior demand is not a condition precedent to replevin. The Supreme Court denied the petition for lack of merit with costs against petitioner.[^73] The decision extends Article 487 to replevin of co-owned personal property.

### Surviving Spouse Co-Owner Has Standing as Real Party-in-Interest

Nenita Concepcion, representing the Estate of Arsenio Concepcion, filed unlawful detainer in Branch 59 of the Metropolitan Trial Court of Mandaluyong City against occupant by tolerance Angelina Soriente over a 295-square-meter lot titled in Arsenio's name married to Nenita. After Soriente failed to appear at the preliminary conference, the trial court rendered judgment under Section 7 of the 1991 Revised Rule on Summary Procedure ordering her to vacate and pay P5,000.00 monthly compensation from June 2000, P3,000.00 attorney's fees, litigation expenses and costs. Branch 213 of the Regional Trial Court of Mandaluyong City affirmed in toto. On direct Rule 45 review, Soriente challenged Nenita's legal capacity to sue for the estate. Besides finding waiver under Section 4, Rule 8 for failure to make a specific denial, the Supreme Court applied Article 487 and held that as successor-in-interest and co-owner of the lot, Nenita was capacitated to prosecute the ejectment suit as a real party-in-interest even assuming she lacked documentary proof of representative capacity. The Supreme Court also upheld the timeliness of the action, barred collateral attack on the Torrens title under Section 48 of Presidential Decree No. 1529 and ruled that consolidation of separate ejectment cases did not excuse Soriente's absence at the preliminary conference. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Regional Trial Court's October 3, 2003 Order with no costs.[^74] The ruling shows that co-owner standing under Article 487 independently sustains a surviving spouse's ejectment complaint.

### Reivindicatory Actions and Trial Acknowledgement of Co-Ownership

In 1974, Nieves Plasabas and Marcos Malazarte filed an action for recovery of title (accion reivindicatoria) over 2.6360 hectares of coconut land in Maasin, Southern Leyte, alleging sole ownership in their complaint. Trial testimonies revealed that Nieves co-owned the land with three siblings who authorized petitioners to pursue the case. After both parties rested, the Regional Trial Court dismissed the complaint and counterclaim without prejudice for failure to implead indispensable co-owners. The Court of Appeals affirmed in CA-G.R. CV No. 43085 holding that Article 487 did not cover reivindicatory actions. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court reversed, holding that the provision covers all recovery actions including accion publiciana and reivindicatory actions, that a favorable judgment benefits all co-owners while an adverse judgment cannot prejudice unimpleaded co-owners and that petitioners' complaint allegation of sole ownership became immaterial once they acknowledged co-ownership and co-owner authority at trial. The Supreme Court also ruled that non-joinder of indispensable parties is not a ground for outright dismissal absent refusal to obey a court order to implead. The Supreme Court granted the petition and remanded the case to the trial court with direction to decide on the merits with dispatch.[^75] The decision establishes that Article 487 governs reivindicatory actions and that trial acknowledgement of co-ownership preserves unilateral standing.

### Co-Owner Standing in Accion Publiciana Against Occupants by Tolerance

Registered co-owner Luis Miguel Ysmael and buyer Cristeta Santos-Alvarez filed accion publiciana before Branch 216 of the Regional Trial Court of Quezon City against fourteen occupants of lots under Transfer Certificate of Title No. 41698. Petitioners questioned respondents' standing because Ysmael owned only a small share and Alvarez's sale was not annotated on the title. They also invoked Presidential Decree Nos. 1517 and 2016 and Republic Act No. 7279. The Regional Trial Court ordered defendants to vacate and pay P400.00 each per month from extrajudicial demand, P20,000.00 exemplary damages, P20,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses and costs while dismissing counterclaims. The Court of Appeals affirmed in toto. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that Ysmael as a named co-owner could bring accion publiciana without joining his co-owners because the suit is presumed filed for all. It further held that non-annotation did not invalidate Alvarez's sale under [Article 1358](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1358.md) and that petitioners failed to prove any lease relationship or statutory qualification under Presidential Decree Nos. 1517 and 2016 or Republic Act No. 7279. The Supreme Court denied the petition for lack of merit and affirmed the Court of Appeals' March 14, 2005 Decision.[^76] The case applies Article 487 to an accion publiciana brought by one registered co-owner alongside an unannotated buyer.

### Co-Owner SPAs Are Mere Surplusage in Unlawful Detainer

George de Castro, for himself and his co-owner siblings, filed unlawful detainer in the Municipal Trial Court of Alaminos City against month-to-month tenant Leo Wee after Wee refused to pay an agreed rental increase from P9,000.00 to P15,000.00. Both the Municipal Trial Court and Branch 54 of the Regional Trial Court of Alaminos City dismissed the complaint. The Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. SP No. 90906 and ordered Wee to vacate and pay P15,000.00 monthly from March 2002, P30,000.00 rent arrearages from October 2001 to February 2002 with six percent annual interest from June 7, 2002 until finality and twelve percent thereafter, P20,000.00 attorney's fees and costs. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that George de Castro could maintain unlawful detainer without joining all co-owners and characterized his sisters' Special Powers of Attorney as mere surplusage whose attachment only to the position paper was innocuous and did not invalidate his verification or non-forum shopping certification. It also upheld lease rescission under Articles 1191 and 1659 and found the complaint sufficiently alleged unlawful withholding. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' September 19, 2006 Decision and January 25, 2007 Resolution in toto and imposed costs against petitioner.[^77] The ruling confirms that Special Powers of Attorney from co-owners are unnecessary surplusage in an Article 487 ejectment suit.

### Omission of Co-Owner Heir Fatal in Quieting of Title and Nullity Suit

Heirs of registered co-owners of Lot No. 3781 in Inayawan, Cebu sued petitioners Bacalso for quieting of title, declaration of nullity of deeds of sale, recovery of possession and ownership and damages, omitting Teodulfo Padigos who was an heir of co-owner Simplicio Padigos. Branch 16 of the Regional Trial Court of Cebu City ruled for respondents, voiding the deeds and awarding P50,000.00 actual and compensatory damages, P20,000.00 attorney's fees, P10,000.00 litigation expenses and costs. The Court of Appeals affirmed. Before the Supreme Court, respondents admitted omitting Teodulfo and quoted Article 487 to defend the omission. The Supreme Court rejected that contention, citing [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) and Section 7, Rule 3 to hold that in an action for quieting of title, declaration of nullity of documents, recovery of possession and ownership and damages, the absence of an indispensable party renders all subsequent court actions null and void. Independently, respondents failed to prove pedigree by preponderance of evidence and were barred by laches where petitioners had tilled the land since the 1950s and paid taxes since 1960. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' September 6, 2005 Decision and dismissed Civil Case No. CEB-17326, noting it could not grant relief to non-party lessees whose houses were demolished pending appeal.[^78] The decision marks a strict limit where Article 487 did not excuse omitting a co-owner heir in a title-nullification action.

### One Spouse May Recover Co-Owned Conjugal Credit Alone

Quirino de Guzman sued Spouses Arcadio and Maria Luisa Carandang without joining his wife Milagros to collect P336,375.00 paid for petitioners' stock subscriptions in Mabuhay Broadcasting System. Both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals held the spouses Carandang liable. Petitioners argued that Milagros was an indispensable party whose omission required dismissal. Reading [Article 108 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-108.md) and [Article 1811 of the Civil Code](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md), the Supreme Court held that Quirino and Milagros were co-owners of the conjugal credit. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court articulated the landmark rule that while all co-owners are real parties in interest, only the co-owner who filed the suit is an indispensable party while the other co-owners are neither indispensable nor necessary parties. Finding that petitioners failed to prove their alleged oral pre-incorporation agreement and that the debt was chargeable to their conjugal partnership rather than solidary personal liability, the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals Decision with modification, ordering Spouses Carandang to pay from their conjugal partnership properties P336,375.00, twelve percent annual interest from June 5, 1992 until full payment and P20,000.00 attorney's fees with no costs.[^79] The decision established the foundational party-status formulation under Article 487 and extended it to conjugal credits.

### Co-Owner Under Family Code Article 147 Cannot Be Ejected Absent Exclusive Claim

Common-law spouses John Abing and Juliet Waeyan lived together from 1986 to 1995 and renovated a house in Mankayan, Benguet with an annex housing a sari-sari store while Juliet remitted earnings from Korea. When they separated, they prepared an unsigned October 7, 1995 Memorandum of Agreement signed by witnesses under which Juliet paid P232,397.66 of P428,870.00 for John's share. When Juliet failed to pay the P196,472.34 balance, John filed ejectment. The Municipal Trial Court ordered Juliet to vacate and pay P2,500.00 monthly from June 1996, P5,000.00 attorney's fees and costs. Branch 64 of the Regional Trial Court of Benguet affirmed, but the Court of Appeals reversed in CA-G.R. SP No. 48675. Applying [Article 147 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-147.md), the Supreme Court found John failed to prove exclusive financing of the annex structure, making both parties equal co-owners. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that although a co-owner may sue a co-owner who takes exclusive possession and asserts exclusive ownership, no evidence showed exclusive ownership by either party and Juliet possessed in her own right as co-owner without contract or force, intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth. Modifying the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court held the partially performed unsigned Memorandum of Agreement binding between them, giving rise at best to an action for sum of money or rescission rather than ejectment. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Court of Appeals' October 24, 2000 Decision except the portion denying effect to the Memorandum of Agreement and imposed costs against petitioner.[^80] The ruling demonstrates that a co-owner peacefully occupying common property under Family Code Article 147 cannot be evicted by ejectment.

### *Arcelona* Errata and Attorney-in-Fact Authority from One Co-Owner

Co-owner Maria Coronel, represented by attorney-in-fact Juanito Coronel, filed unlawful detainer in the Municipal Trial Court of Hagonoy, Bulacan against Spouses Alfredo and Rosario Mendoza, occupants by tolerance of Lots 3250 and 3251. The Municipal Trial Court ordered eviction, P10,000.00 attorney's fees and litigation expenses, P500.00 monthly rental from receipt of the decision and costs. The Regional Trial Court of Malolos, Bulacan annulled the judgment for non-joinder of co-owners based on the uncorrected text of [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264). The Court of Appeals reversed and revived the Municipal Trial Court decision in CA-G.R. SP No. 67157. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court explained the official SCRA errata to [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) and reaffirmed that Article 487 departed from *Palarca v. Baguisi* (as cited in [*Spouses Mendoza v. Coronel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40583)). It further ruled that an attorney-in-fact needs authority only from the instituting co-owner rather than all co-owners and may validly execute the certification against forum shopping under Section 1, Rule 70. The Supreme Court denied the appeal and affirmed the Court of Appeals' May 30, 2002 Decision and November 12, 2002 Resolution reviving the Municipal Trial Court's May 29, 2001 Decision.[^81] The case settles both the [*Arcelona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) errata and the authority of a single co-owner's attorney-in-fact.

### Repudiation of Co-Ownership Bars Unilateral Unlawful Detainer

Arnelito Adlawan, claiming to be the acknowledged illegitimate son and sole heir of Dominador Adlawan, executed an affidavit of self-adjudication over Lot 7226 in Minglanilla, Cebu and filed unlawful detainer against Dominador's siblings Emeterio and Narcisa who had lived on the ancestral lot for over forty years. Dominador had been survived by his legal wife Graciana, whose blood relatives later sought to intervene and allowed respondents to stay. The Municipal Trial Court of Minglanilla dismissed the complaint. Branch 7 of the Regional Trial Court of Cebu City reversed and ordered turnover plus P500.00 monthly compensation from August 2000, while the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 74921 set aside the Regional Trial Court ruling and reinstated the Municipal Trial Court dismissal with costs against Arnelito. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that by intestate succession Graciana's heirs (or the State in default of heirs) were co-owners with petitioner. Because petitioner filed the action in his name alone vigorously claiming sole and absolute ownership and seeking possession and damages solely for himself, his suit operated as a complete repudiation of co-ownership and could not prosper under Article 487. The Supreme Court denied the petition and affirmed the Court of Appeals' September 23, 2003 Decision and January 8, 2004 Resolution.[^82] The decision is the leading authority barring an Article 487 action when the plaintiff repudiates the co-ownership.

### Categorical Authority Against Unproven Tolerance by Other Co-Owners

Co-owner Juanito Borromeo Sr., owning six-eighths of Lot No. 2587 and co-owning Lot No. 2592 in Talisay, Cebu, sued six occupants by tolerance who claimed alleged verbal permission from other co-owners based on an unpartitioned verbal arrangement. The Municipal Trial Court of Talisay dismissed the complaint for lack of partition. The Regional Trial Court of Cebu reversed and ordered defendants to vacate without prejudice to returning after partition to portions adjudicated to the co-owners who allegedly authorized them. The Court of Appeals affirmed. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court described the article as categorical and unqualified authority departing from *Palarca v. Baguisi* (as cited in [*Resuena v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42842)), with a favorable judgment benefiting all co-owners and an adverse judgment unable to prejudice unimpleaded co-owners. Petitioners failed to prove any written lease or assignment under [Article 1358](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1358.md), could not invoke estoppel as strangers to any co-owner arrangement and had no right to reimbursement under [Article 546](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0546.md) because occupants by mere tolerance are not possessors in good faith. The Supreme Court denied the petition, affirmed the Decision of the Court of Appeals and imposed costs against petitioners.[^83] The case confirms that unproven tolerance by another co-owner cannot defeat an Article 487 ejectment complaint.

### Sole-Ownership Claim Requires Joinder of Co-Heirs and the State

Alfredo Hular filed quieting of title, accion reivindicatoria, accion publiciana and damages (praying for P50,000.00 in moral damages) in Branch 51 of the Regional Trial Court of Sorsogon against the heirs of Iluminado Baloloy, praying to be declared absolute sole owner of land allegedly bought by his late father and seeking nullification of Free Patent No. 384019 and Original Certificate of Title No. P-16540 without impleading his six siblings or the Republic of the Philippines. In their Answer, the Baloloy heirs sought dismissal and P50,000.00 each in moral damages. Both the Regional Trial Court and the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. CV No. 51081 ruled for Hular. Resolving the non-joinder issue even though not raised below because it was decisive, the Supreme Court applied [Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md) and Article 487. While any co-owner may sue for the benefit of all, an action brought for the plaintiff alone claiming sole ownership will not prosper unless the other co-owners are impleaded as indispensable parties under Section 7, Rule 3. The Republic was likewise an indispensable party to nullify a Free Patent and Original Certificate of Title, rendering the trial proceedings and judgment ineffective. Independently, Hular failed to prove ownership and identity of the lot, as the original Spanish deed prevailed over an erroneous translation, an uncross-examined affidavit was hearsay and barred by the parol evidence rule and the land fell inside Lot No. 3353 rather than Lot No. 3347. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Regional Trial Court and Court of Appeals decisions and dismissed Hular's complaint with no costs.[^84] The decision establishes the sole-ownership exception and mandatory State joinder in patent nullification suits.

### Recognition of Co-Ownership and Rent Liability Between Co-Owners

Jose Abejo, owner of an undivided one-half share of a 79,220-square-meter fishpond in Meycauayan, Bulacan under Transfer Certificate of Title No. T-6358, sued co-owner Manuel De Guia (who exclusively possessed the entire fishpond after his sublease expired in 1979) to recover a determinate one-half area and damages. Branch 16 of the Regional Trial Court of Malolos, Bulacan ordered turnover of the half portion plus P262,500.00 in compensatory damages (consisting of P212,500.00 in rent and P50,000.00 returned to a lessee after lease cancellation, whereas De Guia had earlier offered P300,000.00 to settle under conditions unacceptable to Abejo), P20,000.00 attorney's fees and costs. On reconsideration in CA-G.R. CV No. 39875, the Court of Appeals reduced the compensatory damages from P262,500.00 to P212,500.00. Applying [Article 484](art-0484.md) and Article 487, the Supreme Court held that a co-owner may bring any recovery action against another co-owner in exclusive possession, yet prior to partition its effect is recognition of co-ownership and equal enjoyment of the entire fishpond rather than exclusion or delivery of a determinate portion (a non-exclusion rule later re-examined in [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602) where forcible ouster occurs). Physical division and accounting of profits require judicial or extrajudicial partition. However, because De Guia held over exclusively after expiration of a lease, he owed reasonable rent for Abejo's half share. The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals' August 22, 1994 Decision and June 27, 1995 Resolution as to P212,500.00 compensatory damages and P20,000.00 attorney's fees and modified the ruling by recognizing the co-ownership without prejudice to pending CA-G.R. CV No. 38031 and other cases, declaring equal possession and use prior to partition and ordering De Guia to pay P25,000.00 yearly rent from November 27, 1983 to May 1992 and from June 1992 until finality (with six percent annual interest until finality and twelve percent thereafter) plus P25,000.00 yearly rent after finality while exclusively possessing unless a court fixes a different rate.[^85] The ruling defines co-ownership recognition and holdover rent liability between co-owners under Article 487.

### All Co-Owners Are Indispensable Defendants in a Tenant's Suit

Caretaker-tenant Moises Farnacio filed Civil Case No. D-7240 in Branch 40 of the Regional Trial Court of Dagupan City for peaceful possession and security of tenure over an undivided fishpond under Transfer Certificate of Title No. 34341, suing only three of the six registered co-owners while omitting three co-owners residing in California. After the judgment became final and executory, the omitted co-owners filed an independent action for annulment of judgment before the Court of Appeals in CA-G.R. SP No. 24846, which denied relief holding that intervention at execution was their remedy. Contrasting actions under Article 487 (the pre-errata text of which was later documented as corrected in [*Spouses Mendoza v. Coronel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40583)), the Supreme Court held that a tenant suing to establish tenancy over undivided co-owned property must implead all pro-indiviso co-owners as indispensable party-defendants under Section 7, Rule 3. Because the title recitals in the record revealed the omitted co-owners, failure to implead them deprived the trial court of jurisdiction and rendered the final judgment null and void both as to absent and present parties. Intervention during execution was not the sole remedy. The Supreme Court granted the petition, reversed and set aside the Court of Appeals' Decision and annulled and set aside the decisions in Civil Case No. D-7240, AC-G.R. SP-05237-CAR and G.R. No. L-71217 for lack of jurisdiction with no costs.[^86] The ruling establishes that Article 487 does not excuse a third party from impleading all co-owners as defendants.

### Pure Ejectment Standing Versus Intertwined Ownership of Public Land

After a church congregation in General Santos City split into two factions, petitioner Church of Christ, Dadiangas Congregation, Inc. filed unlawful detainer against expelled members Spouses Pelegrino and Rosalia Vallespin occupying a pastoral house by tolerance on public land. Board of Liquidators Resolution No. 220 (series of 1975) allocated the land in unsegregated halves to petitioner and the rival faction Church of Christ (N.T.). Branch III of the City Court of General Santos City ordered eviction, removal of the house, P500.00 attorney's fees and costs. Branch I of the Court of First Instance of South Cotabato reversed and dismissed the complaint for failure to implead the rival faction. Meanwhile, respondents vacated the house but refused to remove it. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that pending subdivision the two factions were co-owners and one co-owner was a proper party to file a pure ejectment suit. However, because respondents had already vacated and only ownership of the pastoral house remained, the rival faction was an indispensable party on ownership, which lay outside ejectment jurisdiction. The Supreme Court reversed and set aside the Court of First Instance decision in Civil Case No. 1568 and reiterated the City Court decision except eliminating the ruling on lot ownership without prejudice to filing the proper action in the proper forum.[^87] The decision distinguishes unilateral co-owner standing in pure ejectment from mandatory joinder when building or lot ownership is disputed.

### En Banc Foundation of Unilateral Forcible Entry Standing

Co-heir Leopoldo Vencilao, who remained in actual physical possession of a house and five parcels of land in Maribojoc, Bohol after Vicenta Obenza's death, filed forcible entry and detainer before the Justice of the Peace Court against defendants who forcibly entered on September 16, 1960. The Justice of the Peace Court ordered defendants to vacate and pay P75.00 monthly compensation from September 16, 1960, P100.00 attorney's fees and costs. On appeal, the Court of First Instance of Bohol first denied defendants' motion to dismiss citing Article 487 and issued a March 30, 1962 writ of execution pending appeal, yet later dismissed the case on June 1, 1962 for lack of appellate jurisdiction and non-joinder of co-heirs. On pure questions of law certified by the Court of Appeals, the Supreme Court En Banc applied Article 487 of the New Civil Code and held that any one of the co-owners, especially a co-heir in actual prior physical possession, may bring forcible entry and detainer alone without joining all other co-owners. It further held that defendants' assertion of ownership or tenancy on appeal could not divest the inferior court or appellate court of jurisdiction over prior physical possession. The Supreme Court set aside the June 1, 1962 dismissal order, remanded Civil Case No. 1434 to the Court of First Instance of Bohol for trial on appeal, revived the March 30, 1962 writ of execution subject to further orders as law and equity may require and imposed costs against defendants.[^88] This En Banc decision laid the modern foundation for unilateral co-owner ejectment suits under Article 487.

## Related Provisions

Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152)[^89] and [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822).[^90]

Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935) to confirm that co-owners need not partition before enjoying common property.[^91]
- [Civil Code, Article 493](art-0493.md): Defines each co-owner's right over his or her undivided aliquot share, applied in [*Clemente v. Republic*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64997) to permit an heir to sue for reconveyance before estate settlement.[^92]
- [Civil Code, Article 494](art-0494.md): Recognizes the right to demand partition or agree to keep property undivided for up to ten years, cited in [*De Vera v. Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935) to confirm courts cannot force co-owners to partition instead of recovering possession.[^93]

Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602).[^94]
- [Civil Code, Article 434](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0434.md): Requires a plaintiff in an action to recover to prove ownership and identify the property, applied in [*De Vera v. Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935).[^95]
- [Civil Code, Article 448](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0448.md) and [Article 449](../book-2-title-02-ownership/art-0449.md): Govern builders in good faith and bad faith, applied in [*Ulay v. Bustamante*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67349) to deny reimbursement to a non-co-owner who built in bad faith on co-owned land.[^96]
- [Civil Code, Article 546](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0546.md): Limits reimbursement of useful expenses to possessors in good faith, applied in [*Resuena v. - [Civil Code, Article 1078](../book-3-title-04-succession/art-1078.md): Establishes hereditary co-ownership of an undivided estate prior to partition, applied with Article 487 in [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411).[^98]
- [Civil Code, Article 1358](../book-4-title-02-contracts/art-1358.md): Addresses public-instrument formalities for contracts affecting real rights, discussed in [*Resuena v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42842)[^99] and [*Estreller v. Ysmael*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48937).[^100]

Heirs of De Guzman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42075)[^101] and [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822).[^102]

## Key Cases

- [*Vencilao v. Camarenta*](https://lawphil.net/judjuris/juri1969/sep1969/gr_l-24308_1969.html): Co-heir in actual possession of five parcels and a house sued forcible entrants before the Justice of the Peace Court (which ordered eviction, P75.00 monthly compensation, P100.00 attorney's fees and costs). The Court of First Instance of Bohol dismissed on appeal for non-joinder of co-heirs. The Supreme Court En Banc held that under Article 487 any co-owner in actual possession may sue alone for forcible entry and detainer without joining other co-heirs, set aside the dismissal, remanded for trial on appeal and revived the writ of execution with costs against defendants.[^106]
- [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264): A fishpond tenant sued only three of six registered co-owners for security of tenure in Branch 40 of the Regional Trial Court of Dagupan City, obtaining a final judgment that the Court of Appeals refused to annul. Contrasting unilateral co-owner suits under Article 487, the Supreme Court held that all pro-indiviso co-owners are indispensable party-defendants in a tenant's suit affecting undivided land, reversed the Court of Appeals and annulled the decisions in Civil Case No. D-7240, AC-G.R. SP-05237-CAR and G.R. No. L-71217 for lack of jurisdiction.[^107]
- [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152): A one-half undivided fishpond co-owner sued a fellow co-owner holding over exclusively after lease expiration. Branch 16 of the Regional Trial Court of Malolos and the Court of Appeals ordered turnover of a half portion and damages. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that prior to partition the action yields recognition of co-ownership and equal use rather than exclusion or delivery of a determinate share (a bar later re-examined in [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602) for forcible ouster), affirmed P212,500.00 compensatory damages and P20,000.00 attorney's fees and modified the decree to recognize co-ownership without prejudice to pending cases while imposing P25,000.00 yearly holdover rent with six and twelve percent interest.[^108]
- [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411): A co-heir sued alone to quiet title, claim sole ownership and nullify a Free Patent and Original Certificate of Title without impleading his six siblings or the Republic. Branch 51 of the Regional Trial Court of Sorsogon and the Court of Appeals ruled for plaintiff. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that a co-owner suing solely for himself claiming sole ownership must implead all co-owners and the State as indispensable parties, found plaintiff also failed to prove ownership and identity, reversed both lower courts and dismissed the complaint.[^109]
- [*Resuena v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42842): A six-eighths co-owner sued occupants by tolerance claiming unproven verbal consent from other co-owners. The Municipal Trial Court dismissed for lack of partition, while the Regional Trial Court of Cebu and the Court of Appeals ordered eviction without prejudice to returning after partition. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held the article gives categorical authority to eject unauthorized occupants for all co-owners, rejected reimbursement under [Article 546](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0546.md) and affirmed the Court of Appeals with costs.[^110]
- [*Adlawan v. Adlawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40780): An illegitimate son executed an affidavit of self-adjudication and sued his late father's siblings for unlawful detainer as sole owner, repudiating the co-ownership of the surviving wife's heirs. The Municipal Trial Court dismissed, Branch 7 of the Regional Trial Court reversed and the Court of Appeals reinstated dismissal. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that an ejectment suit brought solely for the plaintiff who repudiates co-ownership will not prosper and affirmed dismissal.[^111]
- [*Spouses Mendoza v. Coronel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40583): A co-owner's attorney-in-fact filed unlawful detainer against occupants by tolerance. The Municipal Trial Court ordered eviction, P10,000.00 attorney's fees and P500.00 monthly rent, the Regional Trial Court annulled citing the uncorrected [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) text and the Court of Appeals revived the Municipal Trial Court ruling. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court clarified the [*Arcelona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) errata, held an attorney-in-fact needs authority only from the suing co-owner and may sign the non-forum shopping certification and affirmed the Court of Appeals.[^112]
- [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519): A common-law partner filed ejectment against his former partner over a store annex after she failed to pay the balance under an unsigned partition agreement. The Municipal Trial Court and Regional Trial Court ordered eviction, which the Court of Appeals reversed. Applying [Article 147 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-147.md) and Article 487, the Supreme Court held that a co-owner peacefully possessing in her own right cannot be ejected, upheld the binding effect of the partially performed Memorandum of Agreement for collection or rescission and affirmed the Court of Appeals with that modification and costs.[^113]
- [*Carandang v. Heirs of De Guzman*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/42075): A husband sued alone to recover P336,375.00 loaned from conjugal funds without joining his wife. Both lower courts ruled for plaintiff. Applying Article 487 with [Article 108 of the Family Code](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-108.md) and [Article 1811 of the Civil Code](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md), the Supreme Court held that only the suing co-owner is an indispensable party while other co-owners are neither indispensable nor necessary parties. It affirmed recovery from defendants' conjugal properties with twelve percent interest from June 5, 1992 and P20,000.00 attorney's fees.[^114]
- [*Bacalso v. Padigos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45228): Co-heirs sued for quieting of title, nullity of deeds, recovery of possession and ownership and damages while omitting a co-owner heir. Branch 16 of the Regional Trial Court of Cebu City and the Court of Appeals ruled for plaintiffs. Rejecting plaintiffs' invocation of Article 487, the Supreme Court held the omitted co-owner heir was an indispensable party whose absence voided the proceedings, found failure of pedigree proof and laches, reversed the Court of Appeals and dismissed Civil Case No. CEB-17326.[^115]
- [*Wee v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46541): One co-owner filed unlawful detainer against a defaulting lessee. The Municipal Trial Court and Regional Trial Court dismissed, while the Court of Appeals ordered eviction, P15,000.00 monthly rent from March 2002, P30,000.00 arrears with six and twelve percent interest and P20,000.00 attorney's fees. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held that Special Powers of Attorney from co-owners are mere surplusage and affirmed the Court of Appeals in toto with costs.[^116]
- [*Plasabas v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49026): Plaintiffs alleged sole ownership in a 1974 recovery-of-title complaint but acknowledged co-ownership and co-owner authority at trial. The Regional Trial Court dismissed without prejudice after trial and the Court of Appeals affirmed. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held the article covers reivindicatory actions, trial acknowledgement cured the complaint allegation and non-joinder is not a ground for outright dismissal, remanding the case for decision on the merits with dispatch.[^117]
- [*Navarro v. Escobido*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50822): A wife sued for replevin of conjugal leased motor vehicles without joining her husband. Branch 37 of the Regional Trial Court of Cagayan de Oro City and the Court of Appeals denied dismissal. Applying [Article 484](art-0484.md) and Article 487 with [Article 108](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-108.md) and [Article 124](../../family-code/title-04-property-relations-between-husband-and-w/art-124.md) of the Family Code and [Article 1811](../book-4-title-09-partnership/art-1811.md) of the Civil Code, the Supreme Court extended Article 487 to replevin of personal property and denied the petition with costs.[^118]
- [*Fernandez v. Villegas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57378): One of two co-owner sisters signed the verification and non-forum shopping certification in their Rule 42 ejectment appeal, which the Court of Appeals dismissed. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held the lone co-owner's signature substantially complied with Rule 7, Section 5 due to their commonality of interest, reversed the Court of Appeals and remanded the case for disposition.[^119]
- [*Tumagan v. Kairuz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64593): A thirty-percent family stockholder filed forcible entry against corporate employees over corporate-titled spring property. The Municipal Circuit Trial Court and Regional Trial Court dismissed for non-joinder of the corporation, while the Court of Appeals reversed invoking Article 487. Reversing, the Supreme Court held stockholders are not co-owners of corporate property, the corporation was an indispensable party and the dispute was an intra-corporate controversy, dismissing Civil Case No. 272 for lack of jurisdiction.[^120]
- [*De Vera v. Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935): Co-heirs filed accion reivindicatoria against forcible entrants claiming an unoffered waiver from their mother. Branch 275 of the Regional Trial Court of Las Piñas City and the Court of Appeals dismissed holding partition was the proper remedy. Applying Article 487, the Supreme Court held partition is neither a prerequisite nor a bar to a recovery suit, resolved the merits and ordered respondents to vacate and return possession with no costs.[^121]
- [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602): A buyer of one heir's undivided share fenced a portion occupied by fellow co-owners and demolished two houses. The Municipal Circuit Trial Court, Regional Trial Court and Court of Appeals ordered eviction plus rent and attorney's fees. Re-examining [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) under Article 487 and [Article 536](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0536.md), the Supreme Court held that a co-owner who forcibly excludes a co-owner in prior possession may be evicted upon proof of force, intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth, affirmed eviction and improvement removal and deleted the awards of rent and attorney's fees.[^122]

## Suggested Answers

### 1: Forcible Entry and Rent Claim Against a Co-Owner Who Bought an Undivided Share

**Facts:** Nine siblings took physical possession of an undivided 5,599-square-meter titled parcel left by their deceased father. A buyer purchased a 364-square-meter portion from an heir of a tenth sibling, then immediately entered the lot, halted the siblings' tree-trimming work, erected a perimeter fence with a no-trespassing sign and demolished two houses standing on the portion. Within one year, the nine siblings sued the buyer for forcible entry and prayed for eviction, removal of improvements and monthly rent. The buyer argued that as a co-owner by purchase she could not be ejected prior to partition. May the buyer be evicted and ordered to pay rent?

**Suggested Answer:** Yes, the buyer may be evicted through forcible entry, but she should not be ordered to pay rent absent supporting proof of fair rental value and because she is a co-owner. Under Article 487 and [Article 536](../book-2-title-05-possession/art-0536.md) of the Civil Code as articulated in [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), the Supreme Court re-examined the non-exclusion rule in [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) and established four governing rules for ejectment between co-owners. While a co-owner who peacefully possesses a portion and recognizes the co-ownership cannot be ejected, any co-owner may file an action for ejectment to evict a co-owner who takes exclusive possession of a specific portion and forcibly excludes another co-owner in prior possession. Because the buyer acquired only her seller's undivided share and had no prior possession, her act of fencing the lot and demolishing two houses excluded prior possessors and necessarily implied force, satisfying all requisites of forcible entry filed within one year. However, following [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), the claim for rent must be denied where fair rental value is unsupported by evidence and because requiring a co-owner to pay rent on property she is entitled to enjoy as a co-owner is absurd.[^123]

### 2: Unlawful Detainer by One Co-Heir Without an SPA Versus One Claiming Sole Ownership

**Facts:** Two separate unlawful detainer complaints are filed over undivided inherited lots occupied by tolerance. In Case A, one co-heir files the complaint stating that the lot is owned in common by all heirs and seeking recovery for the benefit of all, without attaching any Special Power of Attorney from his co-heirs. In Case B, an acknowledged illegitimate son executes an affidavit of self-adjudication, ignores the deceased father's surviving legal wife and her heirs and files unlawful detainer solely in his own name claiming to be absolute sole owner entitled to possession and damages for himself alone. Which complaint should prosper?

**Suggested Answer:** Case A should prosper while Case B must be dismissed. Under Article 487 of the Civil Code, any one of the co-owners may bring an action for unlawful detainer without joining the other co-owners because the suit is presumed to be instituted for the benefit of all. As held in [*Wee v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46541)[^124] and [*Heirs of Albina G. Ampil v. Manahan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55228),[^125] Special Powers of Attorney from non-suing co-owners are mere surplusage and are not required when the complaint acknowledges co-ownership and seeks recovery for all co-heirs. In contrast, as held in [*Adlawan v. Adlawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40780),[^126] where a co-owner brings an unlawful detainer suit for his benefit alone while vigorously asserting sole and absolute ownership to the exclusion of fellow co-owners, the action operates as a complete repudiation of the co-ownership and will not prosper under Article 487.

### 3: Prior Partition Defense and Non-Joinder of Co-Owner Defendants

**Facts:** In Suit 1, heirs of a deceased landowner file an accion reivindicatoria to recover possession of an undivided lot from third persons who forcibly entered claiming an unoffered waiver from the heirs' mother. The trial court dismisses the complaint on the ground that the heirs must first file an action for judicial partition under Rule 69. In Suit 2, a fishpond caretaker files an action to establish tenancy and security of tenure over an undivided co-owned fishpond, suing only three of the six registered pro-indiviso co-owners and omitting three co-owners residing abroad. Did the trial court err in Suit 1? Can the judgment in Suit 2 stand?

**Suggested Answer:** The trial court erred in dismissing Suit 1. Any judgment rendered in Suit 2 is null and void. In Suit 1, as held in [*De Vera v. Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935), a co-owner's right under Article 487 to file any action for recovery of possession, including accion reivindicatoria, is not subject to a prior partition requirement. An action to recover possession and an action for judicial partition serve different purposes, neither is a prerequisite for the other and the trial court may decide whether co-ownership exists within the recovery action itself.[^127] In Suit 2, as held in [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264), Article 487's unilateral standing rule does not apply when a third party sues co-owners to establish tenancy over undivided property. All pro-indiviso co-owners are indispensable party-defendants under Section 7, Rule 3 of the Rules of Court, so failure to implead the three absent co-owners deprives the court of jurisdiction and renders the judgment null and void as to both absent and present parties.[^128]

# Quotes

> "If a co-owner takes exclusive possession of a specific portion of the common property, which results in the exclusion or deprivation of another co-owner in prior possession, any co-­owner may file an action for ejectment to evict the co-owner who wrested its possession by force."[^129]

> "To evict a co-owner from the common property, the burden is on the plaintiff co-owner to prove that the defendant co-owner employed force, intimidation, threat, strategy, or stealth when they came into possession of the common property."[^130]

> "At this juncture, the Court rules that when the issue of co-ownership is involved, an action to recover possession of a property and an action for partition serve different purposes. One is not a pre-requisite for the other, nor does one preclude the other. Both remedies are available to the parties, depending on the circumstances of each case."[^131]

> "Article 487 allows anyone of the co-owners of an immovable property to bring an action in ejectment without joining the other co-owners, the same being deemed instituted for the benefit of all."[^132]

> "In fact, Article 487 of the Civil Code explicitly provides that any of the co-owners may bring an action for ejectment, without the necessity of joining all the other co-owners as co-plaintiffs because the suit is deemed to be instituted for the benefit of all."[^133]

> "We read these cases to collectively mean that where the suit is brought by a co-owner, without repudiating the co-ownership, then the suit is presumed to be filed for the benefit of the other co-owners and may proceed without impleading the other co-owners. However, where the co-owner repudiates the co-ownership by claiming sole ownership of the property or where the suit is brought against a co-owner, his co-owners are indispensable parties and must be impleaded as party-defendants, as the suit affects the rights and interests of these other co-owners."[^134]

> "Article 487 of the Civil Code provides that any one of the co-owners may bring an action for ejectment. The article covers all kinds of actions for the recovery of possession, including an accion publiciana and a reivindicatory action.A co-owner may file suit without necessarily joining all the other co-owners as co-plaintiffs because the suit is deemed to be instituted for the benefit of all. Any judgment of the court in favor of the plaintiff will benefit the other co-owners, but if the judgment is adverse, the same cannot prejudice the rights of the unimpleaded co-owners."[^135]

> "Even then, the Court views the SPAs as mere surplusage, such that the lack thereof does not in any way affect the validity of the action for ejectment instituted by respondent George de Castro."[^136]

> "In sum, in suits to recover properties, all co-owners are real parties in interest. However, pursuant to Article 487 of the Civil Code and relevant jurisprudence, any one of them may bring an action, any kind of action, for the recovery of co-owned properties. Therefore, only one of the co-owners, namely the co-owner who filed the suit for the recovery of the co-owned property, is an indispensable party thereto. The other co-owners are not indispensable parties. They are not even necessary parties, for a complete relief can be accorded in the suit even without their participation, since the suit is presumed to have been filed for the benefit of all co-owners."[^137]

> "It should be stressed, however, that where the suit is for the benefit of the plaintiff alone who claims to be the sole owner and entitled to the possession of the litigated property, the action should be dismissed."[^138]

> "Any co-owner may file an action under Article 487 not only against a third person, but also against another co-owner who takes exclusive possession and asserts exclusive ownership of the property. In the latter case, however, the only purpose of the action is to obtain recognition of the co-ownership. The plaintiff cannot seek exclusion of the defendant from the property because as co-owner he has a right of possession. The plaintiff cannot recover any material or determinate part of the property."[^139]

> "Anent the question of whether an action of forcible entry and detainer should be brought in the name of all co-owners, We hold that under Article 487 of the new Civil Code any of the co-owners may bring the action."[^140]

# Tips

> [!TIP]
> **Diagnostic Checklist for Article 487 Actions**
> 1. **Confirm legal co-ownership:** Verify that the plaintiff actually holds an undivided share by succession, purchase, conjugal partnership or Family Code Article 147 cohabitation, and is not merely a corporate stockholder as in [*Tumagan v. Kairuz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64593).
> 2. **Plead co-ownership expressly:** State in the complaint that the property is held in common and that recovery is sought for the benefit of all co-owners, avoiding the fatal sole-ownership repudiation in [*Adlawan v. Adlawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40780) and [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411).
> 3. **Check whether the defendant is a co-owner:** If suing a fellow co-owner, distinguish peaceful possession recognizing co-ownership (non-ejectable under [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519) and [*Romero v. Singson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60821)) from forcible ouster of a co-owner in prior possession (evictable upon proof of force, intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth under [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602)).
> 4. **Verify indispensable third-party defendants:** Implead the Republic when seeking to nullify a Free Patent or Original Certificate of Title as in [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411), implead the registered corporation as in [*Tumagan v. Kairuz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64593) and implead all co-owners when suing against co-owners as in [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264).
> 5. **Separate recovery from physical division:** Do not dismiss or delay recovery pending partition, since recovery and partition serve distinct purposes under [*De Vera v. Manzanero*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/67935), while reserving physical segregation and profit accounting for partition under [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152).

### Common Traps

> [!NOTE]
> **Do Not Treat the Non-Exclusion Rule in [*De Guia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152) as Absolute After 2022:** Under the earlier rule in [*De Guia*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152), one co-owner could not eject another co-owner. After [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602), always distinguish between a co-owner who peacefully occupies a portion while recognizing co-ownership and a co-owner who forcibly ousts a fellow co-owner in prior physical possession. The latter may now be evicted in forcible entry upon proof of force, intimidation, threat, strategy or stealth.

> [!NOTE]
> **Do Not Confuse Suits By Co-Owners With Suits Against Co-Owners:** Article 487 dispenses with joining co-owners as co-plaintiffs when one co-owner sues for the benefit of all. It does not apply when a tenant or third party sues co-owners to bind undivided property, where all pro-indiviso co-owners remain indispensable party-defendants under [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) and [*Marmo v. Anacay*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/50781).

> [!NOTE]
> **Watch Out for Quieting of Title and Nullity Complaints Omitting a Co-Owner:** While [*Plasabas v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/49026) applied Article 487 to an accion reivindicatoria where co-ownership and authority were acknowledged at trial, [*Bacalso v. Padigos*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45228) rejected reliance on Article 487 where a co-owner heir was omitted in an action for quieting of title, declaration of nullity of documents, recovery of possession and ownership and damages. When nullifying deeds or patents, joining all co-owners avoids fatal jurisdictional challenges.

> [!NOTE]
> **Do Not Cite the Uncorrected [*Arcelona*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) Text:** Older editions of [*Arcelona v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/35264) contained a printing error suggesting Article 487 was the old Civil Code rule. As documented in [*Spouses Mendoza v. Coronel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40583), an official SCRA errata corrected that passage to affirm that Article 487 of the Civil Code allows any single co-owner to bring an action in ejectment.

### What Worked and What Failed in Decided Cases

- **What worked:** Pleading co-ownership and suing for all co-owners even without an SPA succeeded in [*Wee v. De Castro*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/46541), [*Heirs of Albina G. Ampil v. Manahan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55228) and [*Catedrilla v. Lauron*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/55809). Having an attorney-in-fact act under an SPA from just the suing co-owner worked in [*Spouses Mendoza v. Coronel*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40583). Having one co-owner sign the verification and non-forum shopping certification worked in [*Fernandez v. Villegas*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/57378). Proving forcible ouster by a co-owner secured eviction in [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602). Proving holdover exclusive possession after a lease expired secured yearly rent in [*De Guia v. Court of Appeals*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/45152).
- **What failed:** Suing as sole owner under an affidavit of self-adjudication or praying to be declared absolute sole owner failed in [*Adlawan v. Adlawan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/40780) and [*Baloloy v. Hular*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/48411). Claiming co-ownership as a corporate stockholder failed in [*Tumagan v. Kairuz*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/64593). Seeking to eject a peaceful co-owner without proving exclusive claim or force failed in [*Abing v. Waeyan*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/41519) and [*Romero v. Singson*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/60821). Claiming rent from an evicted co-owner without fair rental value evidence failed in [*Mabalo v. Heirs of Roman Babuyo*](https://elibrary.judiciary.gov.ph/thebookshelf/showdocs/1/68602).
